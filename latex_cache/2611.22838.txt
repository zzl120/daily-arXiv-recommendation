The gains are mostly reported on the first ten results, yet in deployment such models hand hundreds of candidates to a downstream ranker or reward model. We show that RL post-training has a hidden cost: it improves accuracy at the top of the list but, on all five datasets we study, lowers the recall of the candidate list . The model becomes a better ranker and a worse retriever. Controlled experiments trace the loss to the negative advantages that RL assigns to the candidates it samples, the rollout. The root cause is that only one next item is logged per user, so RL treats every other candidate as wrong. Yet these candidates are the model's most plausible alternatives, and often the next items of other users with similar histories. Penalising them pushes down exactly what similar users choose next, and these items drop out of those users' candidate lists. This diagnosis points directly to a remedy: out-of-rollout anchoring, a single additional loss term that preserves the SFT model's ordering of the items outside the rollout. It keeps the gain at the top and restores recall to the SFT level on all five datasets, and it is effective under other RL configurations as well. The loss appears on all five datasets, widely used benchmarks that differ in domain and catalogue size; it persists under beam search, and an evaluation confined to the top of the list sees only the improvement. The objective is not to blame: the exact solution of the KL-regularised RL problem cannot lower the expected at any $K$ . The loss comes from how the update is estimated. SFT raises the likelihood of the logged item and names no item as wrong. GRPO also lowers every sampled candidate that is not the logged item. In the reasoning tasks it was designed for, such an answer is wrong; in recommendation it is among the policy's top choices for the user, a plausible next item that happened not to be the one recorded, akin to the unlabelled positives of dense retrieval . All users share one small item set, so such an item is penalised for many users, far more often than it is rewarded, and its score falls for users with similar histories too, including those whose next item it is. If it lies outside such a user's rollout, the rollout earns no reward and nothing pushes back, so rarely sampled items move above it . Controlled experiments support this account. Removing the negative advantages removes the loss, and the gain with it; keeping only them makes the loss at least three times larger. And the loss falls where the penalties fall: the test users who lose the most recall are those whose next item is penalised most, and these items can be identified from the SFT model before RL starts. The cause points to the remedy: if the damage is done outside the rollout, protecting the items there should be enough. does exactly this: it leaves GRPO unchanged inside the rollout and adds, for every prompt, a term that keeps the relative order the SFT model gave to $M$ items sampled outside the rollout . Whenever the target is in the rollout, the other $G-1$ candidates are pushed down. This improves the top of the list, because the target rises above the items that competed with it . The few targets that never appear in a training rollout gain rank as well : items that are not penalised move up as the penalised ones move down. GRPO was developed for reasoning tasks with verifiable answers, where lowering a sampled answer that misses the label is justified: the answer is wrong, it is rarely the correct answer to a similar prompt, and only the first answer is used. Next-item recommendation differs on each count. The label is not unique: a history is compatible with several next items, of which the log records one, so a penalised candidate is often an acceptable recommendation. The answers are shared: all users choose from one small closed catalogue, and users with similar histories make similar choices, so the same item is a candidate for some users and the logged item for others. And the list is used in depth: a retrieval channel consumes ranks up to $10^3$. Section~ shows where the damage is done: outside the rollout, on items that lose rank for users whose rollouts do not contain them. The KL term of GRPO does not reach these items: it is estimated on the rollout too, and so constrains the policy only on its top candidates. What is needed is a constraint on the items outside the rollout that leaves the update inside it untouched. To answer RQ3 we therefore propose out-of-rollout anchoring, which is simple and, as Section~ shows, effective: GRPO is left unchanged, and one added term keeps the order that the SFT model gave to a few items sampled outside the rollout . Generative recommendation with semantic IDs~ adopted RL through the OneRec systems~ and MiniOneRec~; OneRec-Think, OpenOneRec, Rec-R1 and Rank-GRPO use the same family of objectives~, alongside DPO-style ones~. A recent line of work observes that most rollouts contain no target and therefore no signal, and repairs this with modified groups, target-prefix hints, prefix-level credit or step-aligned rewards~. Evaluated at $K 10$, in a few cases up to 32, these methods do not see what we find: the rollouts that do carry signal are the source of a recall loss. Several other industrial papers report observations that agree with ours. GenRec finds that GRPO without its relevance gate lowers HR@50 below the SFT model~, and RGD reports a lower HitRate@604 after DPO or GRPO alignment although the reward rises~. In PinGER and Multi-Decoder OneRec recall collapses unless an additional term ties the model to the pretrained one~, and GrowthGR describes a trade-off between Recall@10 and Recall@1000 that depends on the rollout strategy~. TSGR notes that rollouts concentrate on head candidates, so that reward normalisation suppresses items still within the top 1{,}000~. Each is a single observation; we provide a controlled measurement across $K$ and an explanation that accounts for the gain and the loss together. In reasoning tasks, RL with verifiable rewards improves pass@1 while base models keep a higher pass@$k$ at large $k$~; that effect concerns the coverage of sampling, whereas ours changes the deterministic ranking of a closed item set and transfers across users. Existing regularisers towards the initial model~ act on the samples that the policy or the data provide, while our anchor is restricted to items outside the rollout and to their relative scores. The negatives of GRPO in recommendation are hard negatives in the sense of dense retrieval and implicit-feedback recommendation, where candidates mined from a model's own top results are often unlabelled positives~. RL post-training improves a generative recommender where it is usually evaluated, at the top of the list, and damages it where it is usually deployed, in the candidate list that it hands on. On all five datasets the same model becomes a better ranker and a worse retriever, and an evaluation confined to the top of the list sees only the gain. The root cause is that only one next item is logged per user: RL penalises the model's other candidates as if they were wrong, although they are its most plausible alternatives and often the next items of similar users, and because the model generalises across users, these items drop out of the candidate lists of the users who would have chosen them. The remedy is as simple as the cause. Out-of-rollout anchoring adds a single term that keeps the SFT model's order of the items outside the rollout; it preserves the gain at the top, restores recall to the level of the SFT model, and helps under every RL configuration we tried. We regard the diagnosis as the main result: it explains why the anchor suffices and where a better reward should act. We recommend that RL post-training be evaluated at the depth at which the model is deployed, not only at the top of the list. These results open three directions. The anchor preserves recall and often lifts it slightly above the SFT level. RL itself still has no learning signal for targets outside the rollout; an RL objective designed for large $K$, with rollouts and rewards that reach deep into the list, could strengthen recall itself and improve both ends of the list at once. The root cause can also be addressed at the reward: one that recognises several acceptable items per user, for example the choices of users with similar histories, would not penalise plausible candidates in the first place. Finally, the trade-off should be measured at industrial scale, with larger catalogues and models and with the downstream ranker in the loop, where the recall of the candidate list becomes an online gain.
