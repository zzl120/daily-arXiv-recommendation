 High-precision retrieval over external knowledge bases is essential for LLM agents to operate effectively. However, existing Information Retrieval research primarily optimizes query-side retrievers and generators , while reducing corpus-side representation to chunking. This leaves index-side quality an underexplored lever. We propose Pre-Retrieval Index Modeling \& Enhancement , an index-side optimization framework built on two complementary tracks: an LLM-driven Semantic Restructuring track that transforms long documents into structured, information-dense sections, and a deterministic Metadata Synthesis and Hierarchical Retrieval track that extracts semantic anchors and heading structure for coarse-to-fine section retrieval. Our evaluations show that deterministic metadata matches structure-aware chunking's retrieval quality while cutting embedding computation by 45.8× and index vector count by 22.4×. On top of these gains, Semantic Restructuring further improves Answer-F1 by 13.7\ Efficient information retrieval is essential for LLM agents, as the quality of retrieved contexts serves as the foundational bottleneck for downstream generation . While query-side retrievers and generators have been extensively refined—from sparse models to dense dual-encoders , late-interaction frameworks , and cross-encoder rerankers—corpus-side representation has largely remained a fixed input. Conventional preprocessing merely alters text without enhancing its information density or logical structure. {attachments/paradigm1.png} {attachments/Method1.jpeg} Recent methods began to exploit document structure for indexing, and show that once a strong cross-encoder reranker is present, common retrieval enhancements yield no reliable additional gain. This raises the question of whether index-side representation can be improved and whether its benefits are compensated by a strong pipeline. We propose that retrieval quality is bounded by the information density of the indexed corpus. Once a strong retriever and reranker are in place, the remaining leverage lies in information structure and density at the index level. Guided by this principle, we present PRIME, an index-side quality optimization framework that operates on two complementary tracks without modifying the retriever. Semantic Restructuring employs an LLM-driven transformation to explicate implicit argument structure and compress descriptive prose within each section, increasing knowledge density for long structured documents. In parallel, Metadata Synthesis and Hierarchical Retrieval form a fully deterministic track: MS extracts semantic anchors and heading structure from the original document, which HR then consumes with lightweight high-density embeddings for coarse document filtering and a cross-encoder for fine-grained section reranking. Query-side retrieval has been extensively optimized. From sparse retrieval to learned sparse representations such as SPLADE . Dense retrieval progressed through dual-encoder architectures including DPR , ANCE and Contriever , multi-vector late-interaction models represented by ColBERT , and multilingual general-purpose encoders like BGE-M3 . Multi-stage refinement adds cross-encoder rerankers and LLM-based listwise ranking approaches such as RankGPT . On the query formulation side, methods including HyDE expand queries via hypothetical passages. However, these methods optimize how queries are encoded, matched, and ranked against a fixed document representation. On Corpus-Side, several lines of work have modified the corpus before indexing. Early document preprocessing techniques adjust surface token forms. Early structured document retrieval exploited pre-existing field markup for weighted term scoring but relied on author-provided structure. Extractive summarization for IR selects a subset of existing sentences and trades recall for conciseness without generating new, denser text. Document expansion via query prediction such as Doc2Query , appends synthetic queries to broaden lexical coverage for term matching, yet leaves the document's original knowledge content and information density unchanged. Chunking strategies determine where to place segment boundaries but preserve the token composition within each segment. Across these approaches, a common pattern emerges: each modifies how text is packaged, not what the text conveys. Recent methods have begun exploiting document structure for indexing. Multi-view Content-aware Indexing , DISRetrieval , and PaperRegister organize documents by content structure, discourse, or register but derive representations from original tokens without transformation. Summary-Augmented Chunking injects document-level context into chunks, improving retrieval without rewriting the indexed text. RAPTOR generates recursive LLM summaries but relies on embedding-driven clustering. GraphRAG and follow-up graph-based methods such as PathRAG and LeanRAG reorganize at the graph level without modifying indexed text. Across these methods, structure is exploited but text is not transformed; text is enriched but its information density is not increased. add a critical constraint: once a strong cross-encoder reranker is present, retrieval enhancements yield no Holm-corrected gain. SR transforms each document $D_i$ into a knowledge-dense representation $D_i^*$ while preserving the original H2 structure. The Hierarchical Retrieval module consumes MS-generated metadata through a two-stage pipeline: coarse document filtering followed by section-level reranking. This design reflects the observation that document-level relevance can be assessed via coarse semantic signals, while section-level relevance requires fine-grained token interaction. Stage 1 retrieves candidate documents using two parallel pathways. The BM25 pathway indexes the full document text for exact keyword matches. The vector pathway indexes the high-density metadata encoded via BGE-small-en-v1.5 . Results from both pathways are merged via Reciprocal Rank Fusion to form a top-30 candidate set. Under Dense-only, PRIME leads at 0.214 (+22.2\ Our results reveal that index-side optimization is effective at the context level. However, the fact that deterministic metadata matches structured chunking while SR's QA gains flow primarily through improved retrieval rather than context substitution points to a broader issue: retrieval metrics alone are an incomplete lens on corpus-side preprocessing. Additionally, the three-regime pattern across BRIGHT points to a broader implication. Index-side interventions are not universally beneficial, their utility is predictable from corpus properties measurable before retrieval. This shifts the evaluation focus from absolute improvement to corpus-conditioned utility, precisely the dimension that standard IR benchmarks, averaged over heterogeneous corpora, cannot resolve. Four open problems follow. SR's quality-fidelity trade-off raises the question of when rewriting can be trusted without verification; its break-even cost motivates lighter restructuring strategies without losing information; the interference on heading-free corpora suggests corpus-adaptive strategies; and our text-only pipeline is inherently limited to textual evidence — table and figure content in QASPER is not indexed. These call for a principled theory of index-side optimization conditioned on document structure, rather than empirical heuristics. We presented PRIME, an index-side optimization framework combining LLM-driven Semantic Restructuring with deterministic Metadata Synthesis and Hierarchical Retrieval.
