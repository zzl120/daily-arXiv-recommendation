GRU4Rec~ applied recurrent networks to interaction sequences, and later models moved to transformer encoders. SASRec~ is trained with causal next-item prediction, BERT4Rec~ with masked item modelling, and S-Rec~ adds further self-supervised objectives. More recent work scales this recipe to industrial catalogues~, or retrieves items by generating their semantic identifiers autoregressively~. Semantic-identifier models are generative too, but over discrete item codes. In contrast, DARTS samples continuous next-item representations, so the same history can produce different rankings. Diffusion recommenders aim at generating conditional recommendation distribution. They fall into two families. DiffRec~ and DDRM~ diffuse a user's interaction vector, while the second family, which includes DARTS, diffuses a representation of the target item conditioned on the history. In this second family, DiffuRec~ denoises the target embedding with a transformer, DreamRec~ adds classifier-free guidance, and ADRec~ warm-starts its denoiser from collaborative embeddings. study the effect of offset noise and cross-attention. Other methods change the training signal or the corruption process. PreferDiff~ derives a ranking loss compatible with diffusion, and PreferGrow~ diffuses discrete preference ratios. Most target-item methods learn the item representation together with the denoiser, and many add item-level losses during denoising. DARTS freezes a pretrained latent space, trains the denoiser by latent regression only, and applies ranking supervision only to the final decoder. Latent diffusion~ separates representation learning from generation by training and freezing an autoencoder first, then fitting the diffusion model in its latent space. In language, Diffusion-LM~ diffuses in embedding space but repeatedly projects back onto tokens. ELF~ instead stays continuous until a single final discretisation, and shows that this improves both generation quality and efficiency. We bring both ideas, a fixed latent space and one final discretisation, to recommendation, and test whether they keep sampling stochastic when tokens are replaced by catalogue items. DARTS is composed by three architectural components: an encoder, a decoder, and a denoising diffusion model. The training of these components is realized in three separated stages that are key for a diffusion model for recommendation : the representation learning of the catalogue item in a latent space, the latent generation, and the item ranking. Each stage updates a different set of parameters of the model. The encoder and decoder are trained with masked item modelling~, which defines a latent space tied to the item catalogue. With the encoder and decoder frozen, a conditional denoiser learns to recover the latent of the next item from its noisy version, using the user history as context. With the encoder and denoiser frozen, the decoder is finetuned on sampled latents to rank the true next item first, which corrects the shift between the latents it was pretrained on and those generated at inference. Inference follows the same sampling and decoding path . Sequential recommendation aims to predict the conditional distribution of users' future interactions given their past behavior. Most commonly, these systems are trained to predict the next item given the preceding interactions. Given the diversity of user behaviors and the large size of the item space, these conditional distributions can be difficult to model accurately. The dominant approach relies on Transformer-based recommenders, from SASRec~ and BERT4Rec~ to their extensions to industrial-scale catalogues~ and semantic identifiers~. Despite architectural differences, they share one training paradigm: the history is encoded into a single representation, scored against the catalogue, and optimised with a catalogue cross-entropy or ranking loss. Although effective, such discriminative models may struggle to capture the diversity of user behaviour. Their output distribution can in principle be sampled to obtain varied predictions, but it does not faithfully reflect that diversity, and sampling from it degrades accuracy . These models are therefore used deterministically, returning the same ranking for the same history. Diffusion models~ provide an alternative to discriminative models for learning complex distributions, and a growing body of work has recently incorporated them into recommender systems~. Because generation starts from random noise, two samples conditioned on the same user history can, in principle, lead to different items. This gives diffusion-based recommenders the potential to preserve multiple plausible continuations rather than collapsing them into a single prediction~. In practice, however, we find that this diversity largely fails to materialize. In most settings, repeatedly sampling existing diffusion recommenders for the same user history yields nearly identical rankings and only exhibit a weak diversity , so that the resulting sampler behaves almost deterministically. We attribute this behavior to two design choices. First, item embeddings are learned jointly with the denoiser, allowing the latent space to reshape itself until little uncertainty remains for the denoising process to model. Second, the denoiser's intermediate outputs are supervised using catalogue-level cross-entropy or ranking losses, which encourage different denoising trajectories to converge towards the same high-scoring items. Said otherwise, relevance based recommenders naturally produce homogeneous and redundant lists. In this setting, we observe a tension between diversity and recommendation performance, as measured by accuracy metrics such as Hit Rate and NDCG: increasing diversity typically comes at the expense of recommendation accuracy, and vice versa. This is known as the diversity-accuracy dilemma . Successful continuous generative models avoid these design choices. Latent diffusion models for images fix the representation before training the generative model~, while recent continuous language models such as ELF learn the mapping back to discrete tokens only at the final stage~. We carry these two principles over to sequential recommendation and introduce , a generative recommendation model that can simultaneously improve prediction diversity and recommendation quality relative to the discriminative and diffusion-based baselines considered in our experiments. To the best of our knowledge, DARTS is among the first generative sequential recommenders to improve both sampling diversity and ranking performance, suggesting that generative modeling can capture multiple plausible user continuations without sacrificing recommendation quality. DARTS proceeds in three stages. We first pretrain a sequence autoencoder and freeze its latent space. We then train a denoiser in this fixed space using only a regression loss, without catalogue-level supervision. Finally, we fine-tune the decoder with a ranking objective. Existing diffusion recommenders are generative by construction, yet their repeated samples yield nearly identical recommendation lists. We traced this collapse to two design choices, learning the representation jointly with the denoiser and supervising the reverse process with catalogue losses, and showed by ablation that each one sharply reduces latent variation and degrades ranking. Removing both keeps stochastic variation through decoding while the model leads every comparison in Table~, and averaging several draws further improves all four ranking metrics , a gain that injecting noise into a discriminative model does not provide . Leave-one-out evaluation observes a single next interaction, so ranking metrics cannot tell whether alternative samples are plausible futures.
