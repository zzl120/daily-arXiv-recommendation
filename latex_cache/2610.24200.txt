On a cosmetics site, the same lipstick can be viewed, added to a cart, bought or removed from the cart, and each action says something different about what the shopper will want next. A sequential recommender has to turn that history into a score for every candidate item. One design choice is whether to summarize the history once per user or to let each candidate decide which parts of the history matter for it. Letting the candidate decide is appealing, because evidence that helps score one item may say little about another. Deep Interest Network weights past behaviors by their relevance to the candidate , and the Multi-Interest Network with Dynamic Routing represents each user with several interest vectors . But a self-attentive encoder already selects information from the history . An extra candidate-dependent weighting step might duplicate what the encoder has learned, or reshuffle the ranking without improving the metric used to judge it. We test this with a small residual routing module added to a baseline modeled on the Self-Attentive Sequential Recommendation model . The module averages the item embeddings in each behavior stream , weights the streams with attention and adds the result to the baseline's score. The vector that sets the weights, which we call the routing vector, comes either from the user's history representation or from the candidate's embedding . The two versions have the same number of parameters, so comparing them isolates the effect of letting the candidate choose, and comparing each with the baseline shows what routing adds at all. On REES46, with five paired seeds and full-catalog evaluation, candidate-dependent routing barely changes our primary metric, normalized discounted cumulative gain at cutoff ten : the average change is +0.003\ We also revisit earlier Transformer models that apply candidate-dependent routing inside their attention blocks, on REES46, RetailRocket and KuaiSAR. These models use a different mechanism and were selected under different procedures, so we report them separately, as context for the main experiment rather than a second test of it. Sequential recommenders differ in how they keep and select past interactions. GRU4Rec processes a session with gated recurrent units , whereas SASRec uses self-attention to pick out relevant earlier items . Both summarize the history in one representation that is used to predict the next interaction. Our baseline follows the self-attentive design. Candidate-dependent methods let the same history produce a different representation for each item being scored. Deep Interest Network weights past behaviors with a learned attention function, its local activation unit, according to their relevance to a candidate advertisement . MIND routes history embeddings into several interest vectors, and during training the target item determines how these vectors are weighted . Our module is simpler: it averages the item embeddings in each predefined behavior stream, weights these averages and adds the result to an existing sequential score. Multi-behavior recommenders model relationships among actions as well as among items. The Multi-Behavior Hypergraph-Enhanced Transformer combines a multi-scale Transformer with a hypergraph to capture dependencies across behavior types , and the Multi-Behavior Sequential Transformer Recommender pairs a multi-behavior Transformer with a sequential-pattern component and behavior-aware prediction . Search-aware recommendation separates what can be learned from search histories and from recommendation histories . Each of these designs changes the history representation, the training signal or both. We keep the encoder architecture and training procedure fixed and change only the source of the routing vector, which isolates one design choice rather than comparing whole architectures. Repeat behavior is a second source of variation. RepeatNet explicitly chooses between recommending an item from the session history and exploring new items . Our models make no such explicit choice, but we examine whether their predictions differ for targets that do or do not appear in the recent history the model sees. Whether a new component helps depends on the strength and tuning of the baseline and on the evaluation protocol . Metrics computed by ranking a sampled subset of items can disagree with full-catalog metrics , and the way ties are broken can change a result even when the scores do not . We therefore rank the full catalog, break exact ties by catalog index and take target and competitor scores from the same scoring pass. asked a similar added-value question about a different component: whether features from diagnostic tests improve a selector that chooses among recommendation interfaces. The retrospective analysis covers three Transformer variants on each of three datasets, with five seeds per variant. Each behavior stream is encoded by its own causal Transformer, in which a history position cannot attend to later positions. Additional attention blocks then jointly update a routing token and the stream states. The token starts from either a summary of the history or the candidate embedding. Under a local mask, streams cannot attend to one another directly, although information can pass between them through the routing token; a full mask allows direct cross-stream attention. The routing comparison changes the token's starting point from the history summary to the candidate embedding under the local mask, and the cross-stream comparison switches the candidate-dependent model from the local to the full mask. Unlike the residual module in Equations~--, which only reweights stream means, these blocks let the routing token and the stream states update each other. With the same number of parameters, the user-dependent module did slightly better than the candidate-dependent one, although neither moved NDCG@10 by more than a fraction of a percent relative to the baseline. The result concerns one specific way of using behavior information. The routing module works with the mean item embedding of each stream, which discards the order of events within the stream , and it adds no sequence encoder of its own. The experiment asks whether this extra summarize-and-weight step helps a shared sequential model. It does not measure the value of behavior-aware encoders in general, of richer candidate-dependent attention blocks, or of the published architectures discussed in Related Work. Although average NDCG@10 barely moves, candidate-dependent routing changes what the model recommends. More queries get a relevant item into the top ten, most often near the bottom. Among queries that both models already served, most keep the same first-hit rank, but downward moves are more frequent and more costly than upward ones. The recall gains hold for lists of three to twenty items and come from queries with at least one target in the recent history. Candidate-dependent routing is thus better at filling a short list with relevant items when repeat interactions are part of the task, but it is not a better ranker overall. The form of the residual term suggests one explanation. It compares the candidate's embedding with projected summaries of items already in the history, so the trained models may favor candidates similar to recently seen items. We have not tested this mechanism: the decompositions show where the metric changes, not which learned component causes the change. The history-exclusion task does rule out one simple version of the explanation. Even with history items removed from the candidate list, C ranks novel targets lower than B does, so crowding by familiar items cannot fully account for its losses on novel queries. A retrieval stage that passes a short list to a reranker might value the extra relevant items, but we did not evaluate a two-stage system. RepeatNet's explicit choice between repeating and exploring suggests a more targeted design, in which routing also accounts for how likely the user is to repeat an item. Testing such a model would require a new time period and a stated downstream objective.
