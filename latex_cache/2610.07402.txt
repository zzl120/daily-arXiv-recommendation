Traditional sequential recommendation relies on item ID-based paradigms, where each item is represented as a unique identifier and predicted via embedding matching~. Although effective, such approaches lack semantic compositionality and struggle to generalize to new or infrequent items. With the advancement of large language models , generative recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm that formulates recommendation as sequence generation~. Some methods~ treat recommendation as a text generation task, leveraging pretrained semantic knowledge. However, operating directly in natural-language space without explicit discrete item representations makes efficient large-scale retrieval challenging. Semantic ID-based methods address this limitation by representing items as discrete token sequences from a shared vocabulary~, enabling compositional representations and scalable retrieval. Despite their success, existing approaches differ significantly in how semantic IDs are constructed and decoded. Existing approaches to semantic ID construction can be broadly categorized into two dominant quantization-based strategies. RQ-based methods~ produce hierarchically dependent codes typically paired with autoregressive decoding. For example, TIGER~ employs RQ-VAE, which iteratively quantizes item embedding residuals across multiple codebooks to construct a multi-level tuple of discrete codewords as the item’s semantic ID. In contrast, PQ-based methods~ generate unordered codes compatible with parallel prediction. Despite these differences, both approaches rely on data-dependent quantizers learned from item embeddings, introducing additional training complexity and potential misalignment with recommendation objectives. Recent work further explores semantic IDs tailored to bidirectional or diffusion-based decoding~; see Appendix~ for discussion. As an alternative to learned quantization, locality-sensitive hashing methods~, such as SimHash~, provide a training-free mechanism for constructing semantic IDs via random projections, without requiring quantizer learning. However, prior work~ reports inferior performance of hashing-based tokenization, leading to the common belief that such methods are less effective. In this work, we revisit this conclusion by examining the interaction between tokenization structure and decoding strategy in generative recommendation. In this work, we demonstrate that the effectiveness of semantic ID-based generative recommendation depends less on tokenization complexity and more on structural compatibility with the decoding paradigm and alignment with the underlying semantic space. Specifically, our empirical results reveal that a lightweight, training-free SimHash tokenization scheme can achieve competitive performance when paired with parallel decoding and explicit semantic grounding. It also exhibits stronger generalization than multi-stage learned quantization pipelines. These findings suggest a critical shift in perspective for generative recommendation: cross-stage compatibility and semantic alignment may be more important than increasing tokenization complexity. This highlights the potential of simple, principled designs to rival or even outperform more complex alternatives. Despite its strong performance and efficiency, \ has several limitations. First, it relies on pre-computed text embeddings, so its effectiveness depends on the quality of the underlying representations and may degrade when textual information is sparse or noisy. Second, hashing-based tokenization may introduce collisions. Although our empirical analysis shows that collisions are limited under the evaluated configurations, they may become more pronounced at larger scales or under different codebook settings, potentially reducing the discriminability of semantic IDs. Third, our experiments are conducted on public recommendation benchmarks, and industrial-scale validation remains necessary to assess the scalability and robustness of \ in real-world recommendation systems. Finally, our study focuses on textual signals and does not explicitly incorporate other modalities such as images. Extending the framework to richer multimodal settings is an important direction for future work, particularly when item semantics cannot be fully captured by text alone. Each item $i$ is first encoded into a dense representation $_i ^{d_{}}$ using a pre-trained LLM based on its textual metadata . These embeddings are computed once offline and kept fixed. For each code position $j \{1, , m\}$, we sample $B = _2 M $ random hyperplanes $\{_{j,b}\}_{b=1}^B ^{d_{}}$ from a standard Gaussian distribution, where $M$ is the codebook size. The code at position $j$ for item $i$ is obtained by projecting $_i$ onto these independent sets of hyperplanes and converting the resulting sign bits into an integer: c_j^{ } = M + 1. We restrict the codebook size $M$ to be a power of 2, so that the modulo mapping distributes binary sequences more uniformly across the codebook. The modulo operation yields values in $\{0, , M-1\}$, and we shift the result by $+1$ to obtain 1-indexed semantic IDs. The hyperplanes are fixed and shared across all items, resulting in a training-free tokenization process. SimHash-based semantic IDs exhibit structural properties that inform model design. First, codes across positions are generated individually without residual dependencies. Moreover, SimHash imposes no intrinsic ordering over positions, making autoregressive decoding structurally mismatched. Finally, the discretization process introduces information loss despite preserving coarse-grained similarity. A key direction within this paradigm is semantic ID -based recommendation, where each item is represented as a sequence of discrete tokens derived from its content, and a sequence model is trained to generate the semantic ID of the next item~. This shift to composable semantic codes enables knowledge sharing across items, improves generalization to rare or unseen items, and eliminates the need for large item-specific embedding tables. A central question is how to construct semantic IDs that are both expressive and well aligned with the decoding process. Among existing approaches, residual quantization ~ and product quantization ~ are two dominant strategies for semantic ID construction. RQ-based methods, represented by TIGER~, adopt a Residual-Quantized Variational Autoencoder ~ to produce hierarchically dependent codes and rely on autoregressive decoding. However, they require training a dedicated quantizer and are typically limited to short semantic ID sequences due to the high inference cost of AR decoding, restricting their ability to capture fine-grained item semantics. In contrast, PQ-based methods, represented by RPG~, apply optimized product quantization ~ to generate unordered codes and adopt parallel decoding, thereby improving efficiency and enabling longer semantic IDs. Despite these differences, both approaches depend on learned quantizers, motivating the exploration of training-free alternatives such as Locality-Sensitive Hashing ~, which maps item embeddings to discrete codes via random projections. While training-free and efficient, this crude discretization inevitably discards fine-grained semantic information compared to optimized quantizers. Furthermore, TIGER’s ablation study evaluates SimHash~ and finds that it performs significantly worse than RQ-VAE~. This observation has led to the prevailing view that LSH-based tokenization is less effective in generative recommendation. {r}{0.6 } {Figures/prelim_bar.pdf} In this work, we revisit this conclusion and show that the performance gap can be attributed to a mismatch between tokenization structure and decoding paradigm, rather than the tokenization itself. Specifically, SimHash produces codes that do not admit a natural ordering and lack inherent dependencies across positions, whereas autoregressive decoding imposes sequential dependencies. Our preliminary study shows that replacing autoregressive decoding with parallel decoding significantly improves the performance of LSH-based tokenization, indicating that such incompatibility introduces harmful inductive bias. However, this improvement alone does not fully close the gap with learned quantization methods, suggesting that additional factors beyond decoding compatibility play an important role.
