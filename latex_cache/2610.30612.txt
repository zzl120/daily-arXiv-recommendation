We do not evaluate answer generation. Because the tax is largely a ranking-position effect , its consequence for a downstream consumer depends on how rank-sensitive that consumer is; the reranking experiment measures the case that matters most in practice, and we state the remaining exposure without claiming an effect on generated answers. Dense retrievers such as DPR~ and late-interaction models such as ColBERT~ match queries and documents in an embedding space and therefore handle paraphrase and synonymy, but can miss rare terms, identifiers, and out-of-distribution vocabulary. BM25~ remains a strong and cheap baseline, especially for keyword- and entity-heavy queries, and its Anserini/Pyserini implementation~ is the reference point for BEIR~. Learned sparse models such as SPLADE~ add learned term expansion to an inverted-index representation. Because each family fails on a slice of queries, hybrid fusion is attractive; for the same reason, aggregate gains can coexist with per-query harm. Hybrid systems combine sparse and dense rankings either by rank or by score. RRF~ sums reciprocal ranks with a constant $k$: it is parameter-light and scale-free, but discards score magnitude, treating a confidently top-ranked document identically to a marginal one. Score-based methods preserve magnitude but must first place two incomparable score distributions on a common scale. A review of vendor and framework documentation, as accessed in September 2026, shows that RRF is the recommended or sole fusion method in Elasticsearch~, Azure AI Search~, and LangChain's ~; Qdrant documents it as the safe default~, Pinecone recommends it when fusing across indexes~, and it is the fusion used in the canonical hybrid examples of Milvus~ and LlamaIndex~. Recent RAG systems adopt it for the same reason, explicitly to avoid score calibration between heterogeneous retrievers: the winning SIGIR 2025 LiveRAG system fuses BM25 and dense lists with RRF ~ in a challenge where most teams used hybrid search~, as do RAG-Fusion~ and several 2025--2026 systems~. The default is not universal: OpenSearch's hybrid pipeline defaults to min--max normalization with an arithmetic mean and added RRF only in version 2.19~, and Weaviate reversed its default from rank fusion to min--max ``relative score'' fusion in version 1.24 after internal benchmarks~; Vespa's tutorial reports linear normalization beating RRF on NFCorpus~. Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, the two largest open-source engines, thus disagree on the default, which is exactly the rank-versus-score question our per-query analysis speaks to. This survey is a time-sensitive account of documented defaults rather than a peer-reviewed comparison of effectiveness: it rests on documentation that changes from release to release , and it records what practitioners are steered toward, not what has been shown to work; the peer-reviewed comparisons of rank and score fusion are reviewed next. When is score fusion preferable to rank fusion? The metasearch literature answered this early: Lee~ attributed fusion gains to the ``unequal overlap'' of relevant versus non-relevant documents across runs, Vogt and Cottrell~ characterized when a linear combination of scores helps, and Renda and Straccia~ found score-based aggregation superior to rank-based aggregation whenever scores are available and can be normalized. The normalization step is the crux: simple min--max or z-score normalization~ is sensitive to outliers and to the depth of the list, whereas score-distribution models~ calibrate scores on a relevance scale at the cost of fitting a model per run, and the interpolation weight itself can be set from component effectiveness~. For sparse--dense hybrids, Ma et al.~ and Chen et al.~ used weighted score interpolation and reported consistent zero-shot gains, and Bruch et al.~ showed that a convex combination of min--max normalized scores outperforms RRF and is sample-efficient to tune. The practical reading is that interpolation wins when the two score distributions can be put on a comparable scale and one component is reliably stronger, because it keeps the stronger component's confidence; rank fusion is the safer choice when scores are incomparable or unavailable, or when the two components are similarly strong, so that neither should dominate. Our results reproduce both halves of this picture . Query Performance Prediction estimates retrieval quality without relevance judgments~. Post-retrieval predictors are computed from the score distribution of a single ranking: NQC~ measures the standard deviation of top scores and WIG~ the gain of the top results over the collection mean; both are free, since the retrieval scores are already in hand. Faggioli et al.~ report that classical predictors degrade on neural rankers, which is one reason cheap routing over dense retrievers is hard. Recent work applies QPP to the components in fusion~. We ask instead whether the same signals predict fusion , and whether that prediction transfers across corpora. Routing studies differ in what the per-query decision is. Some decide to retrieve at all~; some choose retriever or pipeline to run, from sparse versus dense on efficiency grounds~ to a mixture of expert embedding models~ or a complexity-tiered pipeline~; and a growing group sets the per query, from an LLM judge of each component's likely effectiveness to query-specificity heuristics~, zero-shot mixtures of sparse and dense retrievers~, margin-based confidence~, or a learned regressor from the query representation~. Mothe and Ullah~ frame configuration selection as a risk-sensitive decision. All of these presume that per-query component effectiveness is reliably learnable. Our work is complementary and diagnostic: rather than proposing another router, we characterize when fusion fails and test whether the cheap signals such routers rely on transfer across corpora, finding that they do not . This also suggests why DAT resorts to an LLM judge: the signal it needs is not available from cheap, transferable query features. That fusion can harm individual queries while helping on average has roots in risk-sensitive evaluation~. Benham and Culpepper~ gave a per-query risk--reward view of rank fusion, reporting that unsupervised fusion improves most topics but degrades a minority . The fusion-QPP line~ predicts the absolute effectiveness of an already-fused list from reference lists; because it never compares fusion to a component, it cannot establish that fusion , and because its estimators are fit per collection, it makes no cross-corpus claim. A natural response to the tax is to fuse only when fusion is predicted to help. Section~ shows why this is harder than it looks: the failure signal exists within a corpus but its carriers and their directions change across corpora, so a router calibrated on one corpus can be at or below chance on another, the practitioner has no cheap way to know which, and the held-out router of Supplementary Table~S11 confirms that acting on the signal does not pay. This is consistent with QPP-weighted fusion working best when tuned to a target distribution~ and with the observation that classical QPP degrades on neural rankers~. It also frames the per-query gap that routing would have to close: the oracle gains 0.02--0.05 nDCG@10 over static fusion on every corpus, which is worth pursuing, but the evidence here says the signal must come from richer, per-corpus, or model-based sources rather than transferable heuristics.
