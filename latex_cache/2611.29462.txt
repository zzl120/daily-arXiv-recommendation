These systems aim to predict a user’s future interactions by learning a mapping from historical behavioral logs to a probability distribution over candidate items. However, learning such a mapping is inherently challenging because user preferences are heterogeneous and non-stationary. A single interaction sequence often reflects multiple underlying behavioral dynamics, including short-term intent reflecting immediate needs, recurring consumption patterns shaped by habitual preferences, and long-term interests that evolve gradually over time. To model such behavioral diversity, attention-based architectures have become a primary focus in sequential recommendation research~. Among these, transformer architectures have emerged as the de facto standard due to their ability to model complex, long-range dependencies in user interaction sequences~. Transformer-based models usually process histories as time-ordered item embeddings, which are then processed through multiple encoding layers, where dependencies across interactions are captured via self-attention to produce contextualized representations. Though effective, these models typically use a fixed computation depth, processing every interaction through the same stack of layers regardless of its complexity. This can be suboptimal in real-world recommendation scenarios, as user interaction sequences are often highly heterogeneous. For example, repetitive or habitual interactions may require only shallow reasoning, whereas interactions that signal shifts in user preference may benefit from deeper contextual modeling. Consequently, there is a need for models that can dynamically adapt their computational depth to the complexity of individual interactions while maintaining a unified representation framework. Recent research on transformer efficiency has largely focused on two complementary strategies, namely parameter sharing to reduce memory footprint ~ and adaptive computation to modulate processing depth~. Parameter efficiency is usually achieved through layer tying, where weights are reused across layers to reduce model size while preserving expressiveness. In parallel, adaptive computation methods, such as early exiting, allocate computation dynamically by processing simpler inputs with fewer layers. However, these paradigms have largely been developed in isolation. Recursive transformers offer a natural framework for unifying parameter sharing and adaptive computation~. Yet most existing recursive transformers keep the recursion depth fixed, as dynamic recursion often requires specialized training procedures and remains challenging to deploy efficiently. Mixture-of-Recursions ~ introduces adaptive computation by allowing different items to undergo different numbers of recursive transformations within a shared transformer architecture. A learned router determines the recursion depth for each item, so items that can be adequately represented with fewer transformations receive shallow computation, while items requiring more complex contextual modeling are processed through additional recursive steps. Despite these advances, the adoption of such adaptive computation paradigms in sequential recommendation remains limited. In particular, existing models largely rely on uniform computation across interactions, failing to account for the varying complexity of user behavior sequences. To address this limitation, we propose , a novel transformer-based framework that enables item-wise adaptive computation through a Mixture-of-Recursion mechanism~. By dynamically adjusting the computational depth for each interaction within a shared recursive architecture, effectively captures heterogeneous user behavior while maintaining parameter efficiency. To further improve efficiency and representation learning, the proposed framework inherits the recursion-aware key-value sharing of MoR and further introduces a gated attention mechanism that adaptively regulates contextual signals during sequence modeling. Sequential recommendation has evolved significantly with the advancement of deep learning techniques. Early approaches primarily relied on feed-forward and MLP-based architectures to model user behavior patterns~. These models capture feature interactions and co-occurrence relationships but lack an explicit mechanism for modeling temporal dependencies. To address this limitation, recurrent neural network -based methods, particularly those built on LSTM and GRU architectures, were introduced to model sequential dynamics~. Additionally, convolutional architectures have been employed to capture local transition patterns within interaction sequences~. While these methods improve temporal modeling, their inherently sequential nature limits parallelization and restricts their ability to capture long-range dependencies effectively. Transformer-based models address these limitations by using self-attention to model global dependencies in parallel. SASRec~ introduced unidirectional self-attention for autoregressive sequential recommendation, enabling selective attention over past interactions. BERT4Rec~ extended this to bidirectional attention with masked item prediction for richer contextual representations. TiSASRec~ incorporated time intervals into attention to capture temporal dynamics beyond positional ordering. Subsequent work has explored representation quality and efficiency. BSARec~ uses Fourier-based inductive bias to mitigate oversmoothing by disentangling low- and high-frequency components. DuoRec~ addresses representation degeneration through dual contrastive learning with dropout augmentation and supervised sampling. STRec~ employs sparse attention to reduce redundant item-pair interactions, while ScaleRec~ captures multi-scale temporal dynamics through dual attention across intra- and inter-patch granularities. Improving computational efficiency is increasingly important in transformer-based sequential recommendation as longer interaction sequences raise computational and memory costs. LinRec~ reduces self-attention complexity from quadratic to linear by reordering dot-product computation, while FMLPRec~ replaces self-attention with frequency-domain filtering via Fast Fourier Transformation. LRURec~ employs linear recurrent units for parallelizable, stable long-range modeling at sub-quadratic cost. LightSANs~ uses low-rank decomposed self-attention to reduce parameters while preserving sequence modeling capacity. More recently, state space models, particularly Mamba-based architectures~, have emerged as efficient alternatives for long-sequence modeling. SSD4Rec~ adopts structured state-space duality for efficient contextual modeling, while MaTrRec~ combines Mamba with transformer attention in a hybrid pipeline. Other works integrate Mamba with graph encoders~ or develop lightweight bidirectional methods like SIGMA~ for long-tail user scenarios. Although these approaches improve efficiency and scalability, they still use fixed-depth architectures that apply uniform computation across interactions. This can incur unnecessary overhead for interactions requiring only shallow reasoning. To address this, we propose , the first framework to introduce the Mixture-of-Recursion paradigm to sequential recommendation. Its core idea is that interactions within a user sequence require different computational depths. By routing tokens across recursion depths, allocates more computation to complex interactions while processing simpler ones efficiently. In this section, we describe the proposed framework in detail. We presented , a sequential recommendation framework that introduces interaction-level adaptive computation through a Mixture-of-Recursion paradigm. Motivated by the observation that interactions within a user sequence may require different levels of contextual reasoning, dynamically allocates computation across recursion depths while maintaining efficiency through shared recursive modeling and key--value reuse. A gated attention mechanism further improves the modeling of contextual dependencies, while auxiliary routing objectives support stable training. Experiments on three benchmark datasets against fifteen competitive baselines show that consistently achieves strong performance and favorable efficiency--accuracy trade-offs, with ablation studies further validating the contribution of its core components. These results highlight the promise of adaptive recursion for sequential recommendation. Future work includes improving the interpretability of learned routing behavior and extending the framework to more challenging settings such as multi-modal and cross-domain recommendation settings, where behavioral heterogeneity is even more pronounced.
