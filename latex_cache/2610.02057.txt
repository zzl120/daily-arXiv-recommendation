Hence, a recommender must absorb interaction data continuously while sharing the training fleet with other production workloads~. At this scale, training efficiency is not only the speed of a training step. A job occupies GPUs from allocation until release, including periods in which it waits, initializes, compiles, checkpoints, publishes, or recovers. Our largest ads recommendation models use thousands of GPUs and train on examples per day. Our largest ads recommendation models only spent of its end-to-end wall time training on new data. At this scale, time spent outside the training loop is idle GPU capacity, not just a minor startup cost. Existing metrics do not close this gap. Model FLOPs Utilization measures efficiency inside the training loop~, but it does not account for the rest of the job lifecycle. ML Productivity Goodput provides the correct end-to-end view by separating productive work from idle and lost work~. In our environment, however, its top-level categories do not identify the individual infrastructure phase that regressed or the team that can correct it. Optimization requires both an end-to-end objective and a diagnostic path from that objective to an owned system component. We address this operational gap with Effective Training Time (ETT\ fraction of end-to-end wall time spent advancing training on new data. The central idea is the hierarchy behind the scalar. At Level~1, ETT\ lost time to Time to Start , Time to Recover , and Number of Failures . At Level~2, instrumented phase boundaries localize the loss to six components, each mapped to an infrastructure system and an owning team. This structure turns a fleet-level efficiency regression into a bounded systems problem with a measurable target. The individual optimization techniques in this study are established systems methods, including asynchronous checkpointing and fast recovery~. Our contribution is the fleet-scale process for deciding which method to apply, measuring its end-to-end return, and preserving the gain as models and infrastructure evolve. The hierarchy also exposes interactions across the lifecycle: when recovery repeats part of startup, an improvement to that shared phase reduces both TTS and TTR. We measure this restart effect rather than assuming that the benefits of separate changes are independent. This decouples checkpoint I/O from the training critical path. : Async checkpointing initially used a custom stream staging path: the trainer copied tensors one at a time into a fixed-size shared memory buffer drained concurrently by a separate uploader process, bounding trainer memory by a configured budget rather than by checkpoint size. We originally ran publishing as the last stage of the trainer. This design kept the final model state, its configuration, and the publishing logic in one process. It also provided a simple completion contract: the training job was complete only after it produced a deployable snapshot. This coupling wasted GPU capacity as models and their publish workloads grew. The trainer stopped using the GPUs after the final training step, but the scheduler retained the complete allocation until publishing finished. Snapshot generation does not require accelerator compute, so this design held scarce GPUs while CPU and storage work completed. It also coupled two unequal failure domains. A non-GPU failure in the publish stage could invalidate a completed GPU workflow and force the trainer job to restart. Under the old design, publish-stage failures affected of jobs. We separated the stages through a durable checkpoint handoff. The trainer now writes an anchor checkpoint and terminates, which releases its GPU allocation. A separate CPU job reads the checkpoint and produces the inference-ready snapshot. This job can scale and retry independently without repeating training. The change reduced GPU-held shutdown time, a Level-2 component of ETT\ minutes per job. Figure~ contrasts the two designs.
