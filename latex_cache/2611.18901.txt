Retrieving relevant evidence from these documents is essential for a wide range of document understanding tasks, particularly question answering that requires integrating information across different modalities and locations~. However, the diversity of document layouts and the dependencies between distant elements make accurate multimodal retrieval challenging. =-1 To resolve the challenge, recent advances in multimodal document retrieval have made substantial progress in identifying relevant document content. Visual retrieval methods, such as ColPali~ and VisRAG~, directly represent document pages as images to preserve visual and structural information. M3DocRAG~ extends multimodal retrieval to complex multi-page and multi-document settings, while MMDocIR~ further highlights the importance of retrieving evidence at both page and layout-element levels. Agentic frameworks, such as MDocAgent~ and ViDoRAG~, additionally explore iterative retrieval for document question answering. Despite these advances, existing retrieval approaches largely rely on the individual similarity between a question and a document page or element, without explicitly modeling relationships among the retrieved evidence. =-1 This reliance on individual similarity becomes problematic when the document element most relevant to a question does not directly contain the answer. For example, a figure caption may closely match the question, while the associated figure contains the actual answer. Similarly, a matching paragraph may refer to a table located several pages away, where the required information is presented. Although these figures and tables contain essential evidence, they may receive low retrieval scores due to their limited direct similarity to the question and consequently be overlooked. As a result, independently ranking document elements can produce fragmented evidence, failing to recover the complementary information needed for reliable question answering. To overcome this limitation, retrieval should consider not only the relevance of individual document elements but also the relationships among them. In fact, long documents naturally contain structural connections, such as reading order, captions, spatial proximity, and shared sections, that associate related textual and visual elements. Beyond these local connections, semantic dependencies may further link figures, tables, and passages across distant pages. By explicitly modeling these relationships, retrieval can move beyond independent similarity matching to recover complementary evidence that may receive low retrieval scores despite being essential to answering the question. =-1 Motivated by this insight, we introduce , a layout-grounded multimodal retrieval framework that organizes document elements into an evidence graph and exploits their relationships for question-conditioned retrieval. To capture dependencies beyond individual pages, we introduce Cross-Page Relation Refinement , which proposes potential connections using multimodal retrieval cues and verifies them with a vision-language model. Together, these relations provide a unified representation of local document structure and long-range evidence dependencies. We first parse each document into individual elements with Nemotron-Parse v1.2~, including paragraphs, headings, captions, figures, tables, and other layout components. By preserving both element types and spatial locations, this representation provides the foundation for constructing document relationships and enables precise evidence retrieval and citation. =-1 Building on the parsed document elements, we construct an evidence graph $= $, where each node represents a document element and each edge captures a relationship between elements. We first establish four types of direct relationships: 1) reading-order edges connect consecutive elements within a page; 2) caption edges associate figures and tables with their captions; 3) prose-context edges link visual elements to surrounding paragraphs; and 4) spatial edges connect neighboring elements based on their bounding-box proximity. Beyond pairwise relationships, we introduce groups to represent collections of document elements that share a common semantic or structural context. Specifically, a visual group contains a figure, table, or equation together with its associated caption and explanation, while a section group contains a heading and the elements belonging to that section. Each group is represented as an auxiliary node, connected to its constituent elements through membership edges. This allows retrieval to capture related elements through their shared context, even when only one member directly matches the question. Each edge records its relation type and confidence, providing an interpretable structure for subsequent graph-guided retrieval. =-1 The relationships established above primarily capture intra-page document structure, but supporting evidence may also be connected across distant pages. To capture these dependencies, we introduce Cross-Page Relation Refinement , which extends the initial evidence graph through relation proposal and VLM-based verification. In the proposal stage, we identify candidate pairs across different pages using complementary cues, including lexical and semantic similarity, visual similarity, textual references, shared numerical anchors, and potential table continuations. We restrict candidates to pairs containing at least one visual element to maintain multimodal connectivity. The details of each cues are listed in supplementary meterial Section~. In the verification stage, a VLM examines each candidate pair using the corresponding image regions, textual content, page indices, and element types. It determines whether a meaningful relationship exists and predicts its type and confidence. High-confidence connections are incorporated into the evidence graph as cross-page edges, enabling retrieval to discover related information beyond local document structure. Additional implementation details are provided in the supplementary material Section~. Together, the layout-grounded relationships and verified cross-page connections form a unified evidence graph that captures both local document structure and long-range multimodal dependencies. By preserving these relationships, enables subsequent retrieval to move beyond directly matching elements and discover complementary supporting evidence within and across pages. Any difference between the three settings therefore stems from the retrieved evidence. P@$k$ is the fraction of ground-truth pages retrieved among the top-$k$ pages, and L@$k$ is the fraction of ground-truth region area covered by the top-$k$ retrieved elements on the gold pages. On MMDocIR, retrieval results are averaged over its ten domains, following the official protocol. The same metrics are applied to DocScope and FinRAGBench-V using their available evidence annotations. FinRAGBench-V is evaluated in a single-document setting, where the target document is provided. Page-level retrieval and QA use the 539 questions with gold page annotations, while layout-level retrieval uses the 91 questions with human-annotated regions. Therefore, our results measure within-document retrieval and answering rather than corpus-level document discovery, and are not directly comparable with the benchmark's corpus-level leaderboard. DocScope reports answer accuracy separately for its 679 answerable and 51 unanswerable questions, as well as overall. For both benchmarks, we follow the official LLM-as-a-judge procedure, using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B as the judge. Model configurations, prompts, rendering settings, and artifact hashes are recorded for reproducibility. For the analyses in Sections~ and~, questions are grouped by the modality of their ground-truth evidence (text, table, figure, or cross-modal when the evidence spans several modalities) and by whether the evidence lies on a single page or on multiple pages. FinRAGBench-V contains no cross-modal subset. This section reports results that complement the main paper; results already shown there are not repeated.
