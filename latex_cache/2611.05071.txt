In the following, we discuss the results with respect to each research question introduced in Section~, and then outline the theoretical and practical implications of MM-SAN for dataset recommendation over real-world scholarly infrastructures. {}} The first research question concerns robustness to the structural sparsity of . Our results show that MM-SAN is robust to sparsity thanks to two complementary components of its architecture. First, the augmentation phase connects otherwise unrelated regions of the graph: entity and topic nodes act as hubs that link publications and datasets sharing no direct structural path. Tables~ and~ confirm that removing augmentation, or either of its two sources, consistently degrades performance on both MES and PubMed, showing that the improvement stems from bridging distant regions of the graph rather than from merely adding nodes. The relative contribution of the two sources depends on the graph: entity nodes are more beneficial on denser and curated graphs such as MES, whereas topic nodes provide a larger benefit on sparser graphs such as PubMed, where topic-based links recover connections that the original topology lacks. Second, the weighted random-walk sampling favors neighbors that are both structurally close and semantically similar to the target, instead of spending neighbor slots on weakly informative nodes, as uniform sampling tends to do. Accordingly, it consistently outperforms uniform random sampling at every hop distance. MM-SAN copes with the structural sparsity of by enriching the graph with entity and topic nodes, which connect otherwise distant regions, and by using weighted random walks to select the most informative neighbors within sparse and unbalanced neighborhoods. {}} Flexibility refers to the ability of MM-SAN to recommend datasets in transductive, semi-inductive, and fully inductive settings, i.e., also for nodes not seen during training. Across both datasets and all metadata-availability levels , all methods achieve their best results in the transductive setting, but MM-SAN shows the smallest degradation when moving to the semi- and fully inductive settings, and is the only method that remains competitive in all of them, which makes it suitable for dynamic graphs where retraining is impractical or infeasible. This behavior follows from how MM-SAN builds node representations: rather than relying on node-specific parameters learned during training, it constructs the representation of a node at inference time from its sampled neighborhood. For a previously unseen publication or dataset, the weighted random walks select the most structurally close and semantically relevant nodes in its local context, so that MM-SAN can build a meaningful representation. Since no node-specific parameters need to be learned or updated, the same trained model can be directly applied to newly introduced nodes without retraining. MM-SAN generalizes to unseen nodes because it builds their representations at inference time from neighbors selected through weighted random walks, rather than from node-specific parameters. The same trained model can therefore serve new publications and datasets without retraining, making MM-SAN particularly suitable for dynamic scholarly graphs. {}} We define versatility as the ability to limit performance degradation as textual metadata becomes progressively scarcer (100\ MM-SAN's versatility stems from its cross-attention mechanism, which adjusts the contribution of textual and topological information according to their availability, allowing the model to rely on whichever modality remains informative. {}} From a theoretical perspective, our findings suggest that dataset recommendation over real-world should jointly account for the semantic and structural dimensions of scholarly resources. Since datasets are sparsely connected to publications and their metadata is often incomplete, the two modalities provide complementary evidence: text captures the semantic relationship between a publication and a dataset, while graph structure provides useful signals when descriptions are missing or of low quality. This is reflected in our experiments, where MM-SAN maintains comparatively strong performance as metadata availability decreases and in the more challenging inductive settings. More broadly, robustness to incomplete multimodal information and the ability to generalize to evolving graphs emerge as key properties for recommender systems operating on scholarly infrastructures. From a practical perspective, MM-SAN could be integrated into scholarly repositories, digital libraries, or dataset discovery services in two complementary ways: to suggest candidate datasets to authors during manuscript preparation or submission, and to surface related datasets to readers exploring a published work, supporting reproducibility and data reuse. For example, when a researcher searches for a publication, the system could identify potentially relevant datasets from the underlying , even when they are not directly linked to it, providing a discovery mechanism that complements keyword-based search. Such applications are particularly valuable because existing contain hundreds of millions of nodes yet only sparse publication–dataset links, as reflected in the extreme sparsity of MES and PubMed. The choice of learning setting should be guided by the update frequency and growth rate of the target . The transductive setting, which yields the best performance, suits relatively stable resources for which the model can be periodically retrained offline, e.g., on a monthly or quarterly basis. The semi-inductive setting fits repositories that continuously receive new publications while recommending from an established dataset catalog. Finally, the fully inductive setting suits large aggregators such as , where both publications and datasets are added continuously and a complete retraining cycle at every update would be impractical; here, the ability to represent entirely unseen nodes, on both the publication and the dataset side, avoids the computational and operational cost of continuous retraining. Lastly, deployment should be framed within the FAIR principles -- indability, ccessibility, nteroperability, and eusability~. MM-SAN mainly contributes to Findability, by surfacing datasets that lack explicit links to publications, and thereby indirectly to Reusability. However, identifying a relevant dataset is only the first step towards its reuse: a dataset may require authentication or an access request, and its license may restrict redistribution, commercial use, or the creation of derivative resources. Recommendations should therefore be accompanied by up-to-date information on access conditions, licenses, and provenance, allowing researchers to distinguish immediately reusable resources from those requiring additional permissions. In future extensions, such metadata could also be incorporated as additional signals or constraints in the recommendation process. More generally, while the inductive capability reduces the need for frequent retraining, it does not remove the underlying data-management and governance requirements of real-world deployment. Compared to publications and datasets, author nodes do not directly benefit from the graph augmentation process, making the recommendation task inherently more difficult. Furthermore, author nodes are substantially sparser due to the absence of reliable author disambiguation, which often results in multiple nodes representing the same individual and, consequently, a large number of low-degree nodes. As a result, the information available from the query node itself is often limited, increasing the importance of effectively exploiting the surrounding graph structure. In this context, neighborhood exploration becomes a key component of the recommendation process. MM-SAN mitigates the effects of author sparsity by leveraging the local graph structure whenever authors are connected to multiple research artifacts or when those artifacts are embedded in a richer context of related entities, such as organizations, topics, keywords, and other scholarly objects. Through its graph augmentation and neighborhood sampling mechanisms, MM-SAN can exploit indirect relational signals that would otherwise remain inaccessible from the author node alone, enabling more effective recommendations despite the sparsity of the author layer. Following the same procedure adopted for the publication--dataset recommendation task, we constructed a second set of five edge splits for the author--dataset recommendation task, where authors serve as query nodes and datasets as candidate items to be ranked. A similar splitting strategy was applied to the complete set of author--dataset edges $ _{d,a}$, resulting in training, validation, transductive test, semi-inductive test, and fully inductive test sets. For the MES dataset, the validation and each of the three test sets contain approximately 400 edges, while the training set contains approximately 2,800 edges. For PubMed, the validation and each test set contain approximately 1,000 edges, with approximately 20,000 edges assigned to training. In conventional recommender systems, relevance signals typically arise from direct user–item interactions, such as clicks, purchases, ratings, or reviews. These interactions are continuously generated and provide abundant, relatively inexpensive supervision for both model training and evaluation. In contrast, publication–dataset relationships in scholarly graphs emerge through a much slower and less systematic process. A dataset is first produced and deposited, usually with associated metadata, and may subsequently be used, mentioned, or formally referenced by a publication. However, the presence of such a reference in a publication does not necessarily result in a publication–dataset link being recorded in the .
