Cross-passage prediction outperforms within-passage reconstruction , and reconstruction-based pretrainers such as RetroMAE and DupMAE end the retriever they are initialized from . The first fact is expected. The second is not: a weaker training signal should yield a smaller gain, not a loss. The analysis explains both through a single mechanism. Under squared error or KL, the optimal predictor is the average target given the input. When the target is another passage of the same document, this average removes everything except what the two passages share: their subject, which is exactly what relevance depends on . When the decoder also reads a masked copy of the target and the conditioning vector has limited capacity, reconstruction pays for each kind of information in proportion to how often the copy hides it. Topical words recur within a passage, so the copy reveals the subject more often than passage-specific content, and the subject loses the competition for capacity. In the limit of an unrestricted encoder, the optimum carries no information about the subject at all . This is consistent with the degradation. The results assume that sibling passages share nothing beyond their subject and that queries are encoded like passages. They therefore also indicate where cross-passage training should help less: siblings that share non-topical content, and queries unlike passages. This result highlights promising directions for future work by improving how cross-passage training pairs are constructed for self-supervised retrieval model training. Across diverse tasks spanning all task types, we observe that \ consistently outperforms the strongest baseline by $ Dense retrievers are a core component of modern search, open-domain question answering and retrieval-augmented generation . A dual encoder maps queries and passages to vectors in a shared space and retrieves candidates from a prebuilt index by approximate nearest-neighbour search . In RAG, a language model answers from the passages it is given , so a passage the retriever misses cannot be recovered later in the pipeline. Since the encoder is the whole retriever, retrieval quality rests on how well a single pooled vector stands in for a passage. Learning that vector has traditionally relied on costly supervision: large collections of annotated query-passage pairs, mined hard negatives and cross-encoder filtering . Such data exist for a handful of well-resourced domains and are prohibitively expensive to curate elsewhere. This cost motivates learning from unlabelled text. Pre-trained language models such as BERT , RoBERTa and T5 are the standard initialization for dense retrievers . However, they are pre-trained with token-level supervision, and the pooled vector never learns to represent a passage as a whole, in a manner that is suitable for retrieval , and thus labelled data must close the gap. aims to close part of this gap without labels, by training the pooled vector directly on unlabelled text. methods build pseudo-relevant pairs from the corpus, such as an inverse-cloze pair or two crops of the same document, and train the pooled vector to prefer its counterpart over passages from other documents . methods instead train the pooled vector to summarize its passage . The encoder compresses a passage into the vector, and a decoder must reconstruct the passage from it, so the vector is pushed to retain the passage's content. {!}{} , on one FineWeb page. Auto-encoders reconstruct a passage from itself, so the pooled vector is never asked to anticipate content it has not read, which is exactly what a query asks of the passage that answers it. Contrastive methods look beyond the span, but pair it with a second span chosen by position or at random. They reduce the relation between the two to a single label, same document or not, applied with equal weight whether the spans share a subject or only a URL. CoT-MAE does look outside the passage, but supervises only a single adjacent context span. Two document-level signals thus go unused: passages carry a document's subject, and those passages relate to one another. This raises our central question: We propose \ , a self-supervised objective that exploits both signals using the document alone. Content, not position, decides what a training example contains. A passage becomes an only if its embedding under the frozen initial encoder lies close to the centroid of its document, which keeps boilerplate out of the query side. The anchor's are the passages of the same document most related to it under fused dense and lexical similarity . Supervision then runs passages rather than within one. A contrastive loss makes the anchor single out its most related sibling among passages of other documents . A lightweight decoder that sees the anchor only through its embedding predicts the masked tokens of sibling passages the encoder never read . In all of them the positive is fixed by position or by chance, so a pair counts as equally relevant whether its two passages share a subject or only a URL. A second family reconstructs a passage through the pooled vector, weakening the decoder so that the vector is the only route to the target . Later variants change what is reconstructed rather than where it comes from: a bag-of-words term , replaced-language modeling , a lexicon bottleneck , or the passage's own words with no decoder at all . Every target still comes from the passage just read. CoT-MAE reconstructs one neighbouring span alongside the input , and Revela reaches the retriever through a language model that predicts each passage while attending to the others in its batch . Our machinery is standard: soft targets from a better-informed view , distillation that transfers geometry rather than logits , and InfoNCE read through alignment and uniformity . What \ adds is which passages to supervise , which separates it from contrastive objectives whose positive is a random crop, an adjacent span or a structural counterpart, from auto-encoders whose targets never leave the encoded passage, and from Revela, where the relation between passages reaches the retriever through a language model's attention rather than through the embedding.
