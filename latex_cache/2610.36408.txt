co-occurrence embeddings are paired with next-day behavior without future-behavior leakage. Multi-day click-through rate training therefore mixes versions whose directions and norms may differ for the same item. We compare Word2Vec and an item-graph XSimGCL adaptation across initialization, inheritance, post-processing, training constraints, and downstream augmentation. The inventory contains 48 configurations, including 32 common-protocol CTR evaluations. Observed metrics are reported as relative percentage changes from the corresponding Word2Vec baseline (0\ item co-occurrence embeddings; cross-day compatibility; warm starts; scale management; click-through rate prediction. Item co-occurrence embeddings capture relationships in behavioral sequences and provide compact features for recommendation and advertising. Word2Vec with skip-gram negative sampling , and its item-based applications, learn these relationships from observed context pairs~. Graph alternatives aggregate neighborhood information and use contrastive objectives~. Both approaches require updates as behavior and the item catalog change. Our pipeline pairs an embedding trained on information available through day $t$ with exposure and click logs from day $t+1$. This pairing incorporates recent information while excluding future behavioral observations from each example's embedding features. A CTR model trained on several days of logs consequently receives multiple daily embedding versions. The same item can have different directions and norms across those versions, so good retrieval within each version does not ensure that the versions function as compatible features for a shared downstream network. Cross-day differences can reflect changing behavior, initialization, optimization, and non-identifiability of coordinates. For example, applying the same orthogonal transformation to both SGNS tables preserves their inner products. Independently trained versions can thus represent useful within-version relationships in different coordinate systems, even when their rolling data windows overlap substantially. In the experiments below, this mismatch remains visible in the studied industrial item catalog. Representation compatibility has been studied through alignment and continuity of training. Orthogonal Low Rank Embedding Stabilization addresses version mixing through low-rank representations and orthogonal alignment~. TwHIN considers warm starts and historical regularization in a large-scale embedding workflow~, while GraphSAIL studies structure-preserving incremental recommendation~. These studies motivate comparing post-processing, initialization, and inherited training states. Our downstream consumer is retrained for each evaluated configuration on mixed daily versions; the objective is compatibility within this training workflow. Geometric continuity also needs to be assessed together with the representation's use. Prior work examines downstream instability of word embeddings~ and the interpretation of embedding cosine similarity~. Spherical text embeddings provide precedent for restricting representation geometry~. Parameter rescaling in warm-started neural training offers another related perspective~. Our global rescaling of both SGNS tables uses a reference mean item norm and no added perturbation; our spherical experiments constrain SGNS itself. We examine these interventions as existing or adapted components of an industrial pipeline. We conduct systematic ablations and controlled comparisons across Word2Vec and an item--item graph adaptation of XSimGCL. Within each comparison, we retain the relevant data and evaluation protocol while adding, removing, or combining components: ID-based initialization and warm starts; OP, affine, MLP, and output-scale transformations; spherical constraints and temporal regularization; and downstream residual-noise or historical-version augmentation. Representation-family comparisons, fixed-version input strategies, encoder diagnostics, and deterministic-execution trials provide complementary evidence. The inventory contains 48 configurations, including 32 with CTR results under a common protocol. Three questions organize the study: Can upstream stabilization improve cross-day compatibility and CTR, and how do geometric and retrieval metrics help explain its effects? Given the strong performance of warm starts, how should inherited scale accumulation be managed, and how does global state rescaling differ from per-item output normalization? Can CTR learning further adapt to residual version differences, improving cross-day directional agreement in the encoder space? Our results support three corresponding findings. Multiple upstream interventions improve compatibility and GAUC, with joint evaluation revealing their trade-offs. Warm starts perform strongly, and their scale trajectories support global state management as a promising strategy. Finally, CTR-trained encoders improve directional agreement beyond dimension and architecture controls in multiple configurations. Results presents these findings first; Methods specifies the protocol and interventions, and Discussion interprets their mechanisms, practical implications, and scope. Daily item co-occurrence embeddings introduce cross-version compatibility challenges in multi-day CTR learning. Systematic ablations and comparisons across two representation pipelines show that initialization control, post-hoc alignment, and warm starts can improve compatibility and downstream prediction. Joint evaluation of geometry, behavioral retrieval, and CTR identifies the benefits and trade-offs of these interventions. Warm starts perform strongly and exhibit accumulated scale beyond their high directional agreement. Global state rescaling limits the observed norm growth and attains the highest observed GAUC, supporting its prioritization in the studied inherited-training workflow and its separate assessment from per-item output normalization. This appendix summarizes the interventions needed to interpret the comparisons. Routine optimizer, architecture, and execution settings are omitted. Word2Vec uses SGNS on item co-occurrence sequences~; we export the item vectors. The graph baseline is a study-specific weighted item--item adaptation of XSimGCL~, combining neighborhood aggregation and contrastive learning with embedding perturbations. Its construction and objective differ from the original recommendation model. A common positive rescaling sets its exported median norm to a fixed reference scale without individually unit-normalizing the vectors. ID-init assigns each item a deterministic initial state from its namespace and item key, independently of date or vocabulary order. This controls initialization; vectors remain trainable and parallel update order is not fixed. Warm starts from saved prior-day item vectors and reinitializes missing context states. Subsequent dates inherit both item and context tables while updating the vocabulary. \ changes only the exported vectors from that warm chain; \ is a separate chain that inherits globally rescaled states . OP fits an orthogonal map between current and preceding processed versions using anchor directions, then applies it to the full vectors. It preserves within-version inner products, norms, and cosine rankings. Affine and MLP alternatives instead fit unconstrained linear-with-intercept and nonlinear maps to the preceding processed vectors by squared-error matching. The first day is unchanged, and fitting anchors are disjoint from the evaluation cohort. Output unit normalization removes per-item norms after training. The \ operation in Section~ rescales both inherited tables by a common factor, retaining relative item norms and affecting subsequent training. The spherical SGNS variant constrains both item and context vectors to unit norm during training~. This changes the optimization, unlike normalizing exported vectors afterward. Ordinary and ID-init variants differ in initialization; OP is applied after training where specified. Warm+spherical initializes from normalized prior-day item directions and fresh unit-norm contexts, then inherits both tables across days. Its loss, optimization settings, and initial contexts differ from ordinary warm, so the comparison does not isolate one operation. Both temporal pilots restart with ID-init and encourage agreement with frozen preceding-day directions for common items; new items have no historical term. The graph variant adds a cosine-distance penalty. The Word2Vec variant alternates ordinary SGNS updates with directional blending toward the previous version while preserving current norms. These use different optimization procedures and are evaluated only in the short pilot scope identified in the inventory. Residual noise uses normalized differences between adjacent training-date embeddings: \[ _{i,t}=-x_{i,t-1}}{ /2}, x=x+ \|x\|_2 , \{-1,+1\}. \] For a subset of eligible training inputs, residuals are sampled across items from the same method's training-period reservoir with random sign. Noise strengths are identified in the inventory. Validation/test inputs are unperturbed, and noisy inputs are not renormalized. Historical augmentation either substitutes an eligible earlier version for selected rows or expands each row across available versions, retaining labels and other features. Partial replacement retains one input per row and falls back to the original when history is unavailable. Fixed-version training instead uses the designated later embedding version for earlier exposures; it therefore changes information timing and coverage. Validation/test mappings remain unchanged. Single-worker Word2Vec and ID-init XSimGCL were bitwise repeatable in the tested environment. Parallel Word2Vec variants retain scheduling variability. Synchronous prototypes control update ordering but also change sampling and optimization; they are separate implementations rather than isolated scheduling changes. Their repeatability was verified only in small pilots, using a separate same-day retrieval protocol. Supplementary Figure~S6 reports the relative runtime and retrieval results. Random orthogonal projection and uncentered SVD provide fixed mappings to the same output dimension as the encoder. Random projection has no additional rescaling. SVD uses only training dates, with equal date weights and equal item weights within each date; it uses neither labels nor held-out dates and applies no whitening. The untrained encoder uses a fresh fixed initialization of the trained encoder's architecture. The trained encoder uses the validation-selected checkpoint. All mappings are shared across dates and evaluated without additional output normalization, allowing task learning to be compared with dimensional and architectural controls.
