Current automated approaches rely on retrievers to find work that may be applicable to a given problem~. However, the benchmarks used to evaluate these retrievers pair queries with relevant papers that are semantically similar and limited in field coverage . In this work, we extend the field coverage and ask whether current retrievers can support such : finding papers across fields whose ideas can be transferred. To study this question, we build a Cross-Pollination Retrieval benchmark with a pipeline that mines citations through which a paper transferred an idea from another topic. The pipeline scales CPR-eval to 1,300 queries across 26 fields, 14$ $ more queries and 1.9$ $ more fields than the closest prior work . Evaluating existing retrievers on CPR-eval, we find that they fail at cross-pollination retrieval . On average, existing retrievers reach only about twice the Recall@10 of random ranking. Surprisingly, retrievers built specifically for scientific literature retrieval perform worse on average than general-purpose ones. We hypothesize that retrievers fail on cross-pollination retrieval because they lack training data for it. We therefore construct CPR-train, a large training corpus of 10,573 pairs, each linking a research problem to a paper on another topic whose idea was transferred to it. Fine-tuning three retrievers, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and 4B and llama-embed-nemotron-8b, on CPR-train substantially improves all three on CPR-eval, whereas training them on existing retrieval datasets brings little gain . CPR-train also generalizes beyond its own distribution. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and 4B trained without one of the four domains, the top level of the OpenAlex taxonomy~ , still improve on the held-out domain. Importantly, the trained retrievers improve on AnaloBench~ and BRIGHT~ more than the same retrievers trained on existing data: web search queries , scientific citations , or data built for reasoning-intensive retrieval . We note that these gains come from standard contrastive fine-tuning without architectural modifications, which highlights the value of CPR-train as training data. Given a research problem, cross-pollination retrieval is the task of finding papers on other topics whose ideas can be transferred to that problem. We denote as $q$ a base paper addressing a research problem $p_q$, and let $x_q$ be a natural-language query describing this problem. We aim to retrieve a paper $d$ addressing another research problem $p_d$, whose ideas can help solve $p_q$. Finding transferable ideas across fields means that: The retrieved paper has to come from outside the original problem's field. We argue that a good proxy for quantifying this is requiring that no topics describing the two papers are shared. However, being from different topics does not tell us how an idea developed for one problem could be applied to another. We focus on cases where the two problems share some underlying structure, allowing us to identify correspondences between them and explain how a solution developed for one could be transferred to the other. We characterize such connections through structural analogy. Finally, a correspondence does not guarantee that the transfer is useful. Even if two problems are analogous, the solution developed for one might not help solve the other. We therefore also require that the retrieved paper's contribution, carried through this correspondence, helps solve the original problem. We collect positive pairs in the following three steps: We take the topics of each paper from , which organizes research into 4 domains, 26 fields, 252 subfields, and about 4,500 topics. It assigns up to three topics to each paper using a classifier that combines a fine-tuned multilingual BERT encoder of its title and abstract with its journal name and citations. We collect the papers for dataset construction from Semantic Scholar~, which provides both their full text and citation information. For each of the 26 OpenAlex fields, we take the venues in the top 20\ Among the papers $d$ cited by each base paper $q$, we extract the pairs whose topics do not overlap. An LLM reads the titles and abstracts of $q$ and $d$ and the sentences in $q$ that cite $d$. It extracts the object mapping $M$ and shared relations $S$ of Section~ and keeps the pair only if they form an analogy and $q$ uses, or could use, the contribution of $d$ through it . This judgment discards about 91\ A second LLM call checks that $q$ actually uses the contribution of $d$, rejecting pairs that address the same problem, cite $d$ only as background, or rest on unsupported mappings . This verification agrees with a human annotator at Cohen's $ $ = 0.84 . For each remaining pair, an LLM writes a query $x_q$ that states only the problem of $q$ . It reads the abstract of $q$ and the sentences citing $d$, with the citation masked, and leaves out the contribution of $d$. When several queries from the same $q$ ask about the same problem, an LLM keeps the pair with the strongest analogy, carrying over most of $d$'s method, formulation, or finding . Finally, since the query omits the citation context, an LLM rates on a 0--3 scale how well $d$ can solve the problem stated in $x_q$, giving $u $, and we keep only pairs with $u 2$ . Binarized at 2, these scores agree with a human annotator at Cohen's $ $ = 0.84 . Hard negatives are papers that look relevant to the query but cannot be used to solve the problem of $q$. They serve two purposes. In evaluation, they keep a retriever from succeeding through topical similarity alone; benchmarks for reasoning-intensive retrieval and inspiration retrieval likewise add candidates that are related to the query but do not meet its need~. In training, they keep a retriever from learning the shortcut of ranking by surface similarity: retrievers for reasoning-intensive retrieval are likewise trained with negatives that look relevant to the query but do not help answer it~. To collect such negatives, we first pool candidates that look relevant to the query , and then we judge every candidate before using it as a negative . We draw candidates from the papers in the top 20\ For each query, we consider only papers published before $q$, since these were the papers available when its authors faced the problem. We then split them into three groups by topic: papers sharing a topic with $q$, papers sharing a topic only with the positive $d^{+}$, and the rest. Each group first takes the papers that $q$ cites but that the analogy judgment of Section~ rejected. The rest of the group is filled by TREC-style pooling~: we merge the top 300 papers retrieved by E5~, SPLADE-v3~, and SciNCL~ in round-robin order . Each candidate is rated with $u $ on the same 0--3 scale as the positives , but without an analogy, since a candidate has none . Only papers with $u 1$ become hard negatives: going down each group in this order, we keep the first 50 such papers per group in CPR-eval and the first 3 per group in CPR-train . Queries average 20.4 words. Positives lie far from their base papers: 79.9\ *{Limitations} Several limitations come from how we build CPR-Dataset. First, positives come only from papers that the base paper cites, so a paper whose idea could be transferred but was never cited cannot become a positive. Second, our pipeline relies on LLMs to judge analogy, transfer, and negatives; the transfer and usefulness judgments agree with a human annotator at Cohen's $ $ of 0.84 to 0.92 , but they may still contain errors or systematic biases. Each query is also written by an LLM from its base paper and may differ from the questions that researchers would ask. Third, whether two papers are on different topics is decided by the OpenAlex topic classification, so a different taxonomy would yield a different set of cross-topic pairs. Finally, we build CPR-Dataset from Semantic Scholar~ papers that meet our selection criteria, such as having full text available, and the fields, venues, and years it represents may be biased toward such papers. Our evaluation uses a fixed pool of 151 candidates per query rather than a full corpus. Retrieval over a full corpus is likely harder, and the Recall@10 of the LLM reranker likewise assumes a pool that already contains the positive. Our evaluation also leaves out general retrieval benchmarks, since retrieval by topical similarity is outside the scope of this work; whether training on CPR-train affects it remains open. Our training experiments cover three retrievers, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and 4B and llama-embed-nemotron-8b, trained with a single contrastive objective. Larger retrievers and other training methods may benefit from CPR-train differently. *{Ethical Considerations} Retrievers trained on CPR-train may return papers whose ideas appear transferable but do not actually help solve the given problem, so researchers should verify retrieved papers before building on them. Because CPR-Dataset is built from papers whose full text is available, fields and venues with less available full text are underrepresented, and retrievers trained on it may serve researchers in those fields less well. Building CPR-Dataset and training retrievers required LLM calls and GPU training, which consume energy; to avoid repeating this computation, we release CPR-Dataset and the pipeline that collects it, and will release the trained Qwen3-Embedding retrievers upon publication. CPR-Dataset is released under ODC-BY as Semantic Scholar paper identifiers, titles, labels, and generated queries; abstracts are included only for papers whose open-access license is CC-BY, CC0, or public domain, and a script retrieves the others from Semantic Scholar. This is the source side of every object mapping. - D / solution domain: the cited paper whose structure or contribution may be transferred. This is the target side of every object mapping. - Functional object mapping: a correspondence between a q-side object and a D-side object based on what each does in the relevant system, not merely on a shared name, topic, or attribute. - Shared relation: the same predicate that holds among mapped objects in both domains. A property of one object, such as "is liquid" or "is large", is not a shared relation. - Structural analogy: at least two grounded functional object mappings and at least one grounded shared relation, with the same relational pattern holding across the two domains. - Actual transfer: the relevance text shows that q uses, adapts, or builds on D's contribution to address q's problem through a validated cross-domain relational correspondence. The candidates are shuffled with a fixed seed per query and shown with their title and abstract. In the first round, the 151 candidates are split into eight groups of 18--19, and the model selects from each group, with a listwise prompt, the five candidates whose methods are most likely to solve the problem, leaving 40. In the second round, these 40 are split into two groups of 20, and the model again selects five from each group, leaving ten finalists. Each finalist is then scored separately from 0 to 10 with a pointwise prompt. The finalists are ranked by score; when several finalists share the top score, one more listwise call orders them. Candidates eliminated in the second round are ranked after the finalists, followed by those eliminated in the first round. The two selection rounds use low reasoning effort, and the pointwise scoring and the tie-breaking call use the model's default. Over the 1,300 queries, the reranker made 20.2 calls per query on average , and the whole run cost \$91.78 . AnaloBench ranks a pool of 200 stories.} We introduce cross-pollination retrieval: given a research problem, find papers on other topics whose ideas can be transferred to it. We also release CPR-Dataset, built from citations by a scalable pipeline. On CPR-eval, existing sparse, dense, and late-interaction retrievers rarely find such papers. Standard contrastive fine-tuning on CPR-train substantially improves retrieval. The gain carries over to held-out domains and to analogy and reasoning-intensive retrieval, suggesting that retrievers learn relevance beyond topical similarity. We believe that analyzing what the trained retrievers learn and using them to supply cross-field inspiration to research-assistant systems are promising directions for future work. We hope that CPR-Dataset helps retrieval systems bring ideas from other fields to researchers and, in turn, contributes to scientific progress through cross-pollination. A paper from nonequilibrium statistical physics~ can: it moves a system from an initial to a final equilibrium state, as the base paper turns the data distribution into a tractable one. The query and titles are shortened.} In scientific literature retrieval, inspiration retrieval is the task of finding prior papers that can inspire new research ideas. ScholarCatalyst~ defines an inspiration as a prior paper that, according to the authors of a project, helped or could have helped advance it. MUSES~ defines it as a prior paper that the authors cite in their next paper. MOOSE-Chem~ and ResearchBench~ define it as a reference that domain experts or an LLM, respectively, judge necessary to form the main hypothesis of a paper. None of these definitions requires an inspiration to come from a field other than that of the query. Some work does look for papers in other fields: SOLVENT~ finds analogies between papers across domains, and Same Problem, Different Field~ finds papers in other fields that solve the same computational problem. Because finding such matches by hand is costly, both are evaluated on small curated sets of at most 109 papers. In contrast, CPR-Dataset requires every positive to come from another topic and builds such pairs at scale from citations. Most retrievers for scientific literature learn relevance from citations. SPECTER~ treats a paper that the query paper cites as a positive and an uncited paper as a negative, SciNCL~ samples positives and negatives from neighborhoods in a citation embedding space, and SPECTER2~ extends this training to multiple task formats. The OpenScholar retriever~ instead adapts to scientific text without relevance labels, continuing the unsupervised contrastive pre-training of Contriever~ on scientific papers. None of these retrievers is designed for cross-pollination retrieval, for which we build CPR-eval as an evaluation set and CPR-train as a training set. Several lines of work define relevance beyond the semantic similarity of content. Reasoning-intensive retrieval benchmarks~ are designed so that the relevant documents for a query can be identified only through additional reasoning steps. Specifically, BRIGHT pairs natural-language questions from seven StackExchange sites with the web pages cited in their answers, alongside coding and mathematics subsets that rely on domain-specific notation~. Analogy datasets~ instead pair texts that share the same underlying relational structure. In AnaloBench, the authors write pairs of analogous sentences on different topics, and GPT-4 expands each sentence into stories of 10 and 30 sentences. We define cross-pollination retrieval as another line, in which relevant papers come from a different topic yet offer insights transferable to the research problem of the query. We also test whether what retrievers learn from CPR-train extends to the other two lines.
