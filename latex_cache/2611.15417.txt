We identify a specific weakness in this capability: the evaluated models perform well when lexical or attribute cues distinguish the target, but become much less reliable when relevance depends on a directional relation over the same entities. We introduce SwitchIR, a controlled diagnostic that contrasts Constraint Shift and Explicit Filtering with Relational Flip, where the query and document pair remain fixed while the requested relation direction reverses. Across representative embedding and late-interaction retrievers, the primary joint metric is high on the two cue-available controls and consistently low on Relational Flip; the same ordering persists in an LLM-assisted, manually reviewed realistic variant. An exact margin decomposition shows that both directions are correct only when the direction-sensitive query--document interaction exceeds the direction-invariant pair preference. Empirically, direction-sensitive changes are common, but their aligned interaction is usually too weak to overcome that preference. Diagnostic fine-tuning shows that models can learn relational behavior that generalizes across relation families, with stronger generalization in the larger model. Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents into a shared vector space, supporting efficient semantic matching over large corpora and driving progress in open-domain question answering and web search . As such retrievers are increasingly embedded inside retrieval-augmented generation, conversational search, and personalized assistants, the relevance target a user wants is rarely captured by the query alone: real intent often carries finer-grained criteria such as filtering conditions, audience constraints, or relation directions, requiring the retriever to adjust its relevance target according to natural language instructions. Recent instruction-aware retrievers perform well on evaluations that vary explicit constraints , user persona and context , or document-level attributes such as audience, format, and negation . These evaluations show that current models can condition their relevance judgments on natural-language criteria, but they leave direction changes with nearly unchanged vocabulary unresolved. In these evaluations, relevant and irrelevant documents can often be distinguished through explicit persona, constraint, attribute, or negation cues, leaving relational role binding largely unisolated. Many search intents, however, differ only in relation direction or semantic-role assignment while preserving the same entities and content words. Figure~ illustrates this diagnostic gap. {Figures/figure1_cropped.pdf} To directly evaluate whether retrievers can resolve such relational distinctions, we introduce SwitchIR, a controlled diagnostic set organized around instruction contrasts attached to a fixed query and a fixed pair of mutually hard-negative documents. Constraint Shift and Explicit Filtering test changes supported by lexical or attribute cues. Relational Flip reverses the target-defining relation while holding the entities and token inventory nearly fixed, echoing broader concerns about directional relational generalization . The experiments show the same category ordering on controlled and realistic data: models perform strongly on Constraint Shift, decline on Explicit Filtering, and remain weakest on Relational Flip. Our representation analysis gives an exact account of the final gap. A ranking reverses correctly in both directions only when the direction-sensitive query--document interaction exceeds the direction-invariant preference for one document in the pair. The dominant empirical failure region contains an aligned direction signal that is too weak to overcome this pair preference. Diagnostic training further shows that the directional decisions are learnable, while transfer to unseen relation families depends on training diversity and model capacity. Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate relational instruction following as a controlled pairwise ranking problem and introduce SwitchIR in controlled and realistic variants. Second, we establish a consistent zero-shot gap between cue-available instruction changes and relation-direction reversal across embedding, late-interaction, and reranking models. Third, we derive the exact interaction-versus-preference condition governing successful reversal and use diagnostic training to identify relation specificity and model-size-dependent transfer. Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents into a shared embedding space and ranks candidates by vector similarity, moving beyond sparse lexical matching . Follow-up work has strengthened this paradigm through harder negative mining, late interaction, and large-scale unsupervised pre-training . A growing line of work equips embedding and retrieval models with the ability to condition on natural-language instructions in addition to the query . One line of work builds general-purpose embedding models, scaling model size and training data to achieve strong transfer across retrieval and embedding tasks, with instruction-following emerging as one of several capabilities . A second line of work instead targets instruction-following retrieval directly, either by training bi-encoder retrievers on instruction-augmented data with instruction negatives , or by fusing the instruction representation into the query through an explicit attention mechanism before contrastive training . Existing instruction-following retrieval benchmarks show that recent models can shift rankings in response to changed instructions . find that ranking gains in aspect-conditioned exploration do not necessarily track instruction sensitivity. SwitchIR isolates the corresponding decision failure under fixed queries and candidate pairs, with the target changed solely through relational role binding. Our work is also related to targeted diagnostic evaluation in NLP and LLMs. Behavioral testing frameworks show that aggregate held-out scores can miss systematic failures on specific capabilities . Other diagnostics expose shallow heuristics and sensitivity to controlled structural perturbations, including lexical-overlap heuristics in NLI and directional failures in relational knowledge . Knowledge graph methods such as TransE and RotatE explicitly represent asymmetric relations in a symbolic triple space . Dense bi-encoders can encode direction through instruction-conditioned query and document representations; successful reversal additionally requires the induced query change to align strongly with the document contrast. SwitchIR measures this requirement at the decision level by asking whether a model moves the ranking boundary between two topically plausible documents when only relational structure changes. SwitchIR isolates instruction-conditioned pairwise preference under a fixed query and two mutually hard-negative documents. The instruction determines which document should rank higher, while the topic and candidate pair remain unchanged. This minimal setting removes corpus-level document recovery and candidate-composition effects, and tests whether the retriever can distinguish the requested relation direction once both plausible candidates are available. Both candidates are exposed under every instruction condition, so category differences reflect preference resolution rather than candidate availability. A failure therefore indicates that the model cannot reliably rank the requested direction above its reversed counterpart. Each direction has a canonical and a meaning-preserving equivalent realization. Both candidates remain lexically plausible, so the retriever must match the directed relation in the instruction to the one expressed in the document. We instantiate Relational Flip across six relation types: distinguishes cause from effect; distinguishes the earlier event from the later event; distinguishes larger from smaller, or more from less; distinguishes a container from its contained item, or a higher from a lower role; distinguishes the agent of an action from its patient; and distinguishes giver from receiver, or source from destination. Together, the three categories separate success based on visible attributes, success under matched lexical content but reversed include/exclude roles, and success under directed relational structure. SwitchIR reveals a sharp divide in instruction-following retrieval. Retrievers reliably switch preferences under explicit filtering and attribute constraints, yet struggle when a similarly small change reverses the same entities' relational roles. Success on cue-rich tasks therefore does not imply reliable relational role binding. The models often encode the requested direction change, but the resulting document interaction is too weak to overturn their existing pair preference. Relational retrieval thus depends on both a direction-sensitive signal and sufficient interaction strength to cross the decision boundary. Diagnostic training confirms that relational switching is learnable: single-relation supervision remains relation-specific, whereas multi-relation supervision transfers much more strongly in the higher-capacity encoder. SwitchIR focuses on directional relational structure, where the same entities are assigned to reversed semantic roles. This controlled setting does not cover other forms of structurally specified relevance, such as nested constraints, multi-hop relations, or interactions among multiple conditions.
