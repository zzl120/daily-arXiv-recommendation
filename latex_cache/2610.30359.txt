 {IEEEtran} {{}} {} -.08em T -.1667em .7ex -.125emX}} } @umb.edu } } Fairness-aware recommender systems often encode one fairness objective during training or apply a fixed re-ranking rule after scoring. In deployment, however, the appropriate fairness action can vary across requests because the candidate pool, item metadata, exposure debt, user preference history, and utility budget all change at runtime. We propose FAIRGate, a model-agnostic delivery controller that places an agentic policy-selection layer between a base ranker and the final top-$K$ list. The agent does not define fairness objectives from scratch. It chooses from a predefined policy library covering long-tail exposure, item-group exposure balance, concentration control, calibration protection, and abstention. A deterministic executor then applies the selected policy through constrained repair, and a validator accepts the result only when relevance, feasibility, metadata, calibration, and audit constraints are satisfied. The final language-model component receives only the verified audit trace and produces evidence-linked narration after the ranking decision is fixed. This design gives the agent runtime responsibility while keeping fairness enforcement measurable, bounded, and replayable. responsible AI, recommender systems, agentic AI, exposure fairness, policy selection, auditability Recommender systems decide which items appear in a user's top-$K$ list. This decision shapes user choice and affects the visibility received by long-tail items, authors, creators, genres, and other item groups. Fairness interventions commonly enter the pipeline through training objectives, post-processing re-rankers, or explanations. These tools are important, but they often assume a fixed fairness action. In practice, one fixed action can be too rigid. A user with a diverse candidate pool may support long-tail repair, while another user with sparse history may need calibration protection or abstention because feasible alternatives are unavailable. This paper studies fairness as a runtime delivery-control problem. Given a base ranker output, the system must decide which fairness policy applies now, which repair is feasible, and whether the repaired list is ready to deliver. A purely deterministic priority order is reproducible, but it can treat distinct delivery contexts in the same way. A fully unconstrained language-model agent is flexible, but it can make unstable or unsupported fairness judgments. FAIRGate takes a bounded path: the agent chooses and plans from a fixed policy library using structured metrics, while deterministic validation enforces the selected policy before delivery. The design rests on a separation of roles. The base recommender estimates relevance. The policy-selection agent identifies the current fairness context and chooses applicable policies. The repair-planning agent proposes bounded actions. The deterministic executor and validator apply and verify those actions. The narration agent communicates only the verified trace. This makes the system agentic at the control layer, not arbitrary at the fairness layer. This paper makes three contributions. FAIRGate formalizes bounded agentic fairness control for recommendation delivery, where an agent selects among predefined exposure and calibration policies rather than inventing fairness criteria at inference time. We introduce a verified repair loop that executes agent-selected policies only when deterministic relevance, feasibility, metadata, calibration, and audit checks are satisfied. We define an audit-trace contract that converts policy selections, repair actions, validator outcomes, and abstention decisions into typed evidence keys for claim-level explanation verification. Together, these contributions move recommendation delivery closer to fairer practice by combining adaptive policy choice with enforceable safeguards. Fair ranking work formalizes exposure as position-weighted visibility and studies how exposure should relate to merit or group membership . Recommender systems add another challenge because repeated exposure to popular items can reduce catalog coverage and long-tail discovery. Abdollahpouri et al. study popularity-bias mitigation and metrics such as average recommendation popularity, average percentage of long-tail items, and average coverage of long-tail items . Provider and item-side fairness methods further study how visibility is distributed across creators, providers, and item groups . FAIRGate builds on this literature by treating exposure objectives as deployable policies that must be selected and validated for each delivery context. Recommendation fairness is not only a user-accuracy problem. It includes user utility, provider visibility, category representation, calibration, and the distribution of gains and losses across users . Calibration is important for runtime repair because exposure improvement can move the delivered list away from a user's historical content distribution. FAIRGate therefore includes calibration protection as a selectable policy and as a validator condition, rather than treating every exposure repair as automatically deliverable. LLMs have been used as zero-shot rankers, user simulators, and agentic components for recommender systems . These systems show the potential of language agents to reason over recommendation context, but they also raise governance questions when an LLM directly controls ranking or evaluation. LLM-as-judge studies report sensitivity to prompts, positions, and evaluation framing . FAIRGate uses LLMs for policy selection, repair planning, and narration, but constrains their authority through a policy library, deterministic execution, and post-hoc validation. Existing work gives fair ranking objectives, re-ranking algorithms, and LLM-based recommendation agents. Less work studies the final delivery decision as a bounded agentic control point. The gap is not only whether a list can be made fairer. It is whether the system can decide which fairness policy is relevant for the current request, apply it without exceeding a relevance budget, and explain the verified action without unsupported claims. FAIRGate targets this gap by moving adaptivity to policy selection while keeping enforcement deterministic. We evaluate FAIRGate through four questions. Does runtime policy selection improve long-tail and item-group exposure satisfaction compared with base rankers, fixed re-rankers, and deterministic fixed-policy gates under the same utility budget? Does validator-constrained repair preserve recommendation utility while improving selected exposure policies? Does the agent choose different policies for different runtime contexts, and do those choices explain gains over fixed policy orderings? Does audit-grounded narration reduce unsupported fairness claims compared with direct LLM explanations? A policy $P$ is a tuple $ $, where $q$ is a measurable objective, $c$ is a set of constraints, $a$ is an allowed repair action set, and $ $ is a validator. The policy library includes $P_T$ for long-tail floors, $P_G$ for item-group exposure balance, $P_C$ for concentration caps, $P_{Cal}$ for calibration protection, and $P_A$ for abstention when metadata or feasibility is insufficient. The agent selects a policy subset $A_u $ from this library using runtime state $x_u$, but the validator decides whether the resulting repair is deliverable. In the current implementation, group labels come from available item metadata, such as genre or popularity tier. They are used to evaluate exposure and calibration behavior in a controlled recommendation setting. FAIRGate is therefore a delivery-control framework over explicitly defined policy targets, not a claim that any single metadata grouping exhausts fairness in a deployment domain. The agentic layer is constrained by a typed input-output contract. Each agent receives only precomputed metrics, candidate summaries, and policy descriptors. Each agent returns a structured object that can be parsed and checked. The contract is designed to make runtime choice adaptive without allowing unbounded fairness invention. The dataset contains approximately one million ratings with movie genres, timestamps, and user attributes . Ratings of 4 or 5 are treated as positive implicit feedback. We use timestamp-based leave-last-one-out splitting, with each user's latest interaction used for test and the second-latest interaction used for validation. Popularity tiers, genre-group counts, and calibration targets are computed from training data only. MovieLens 1M supports a controlled evaluation of long-tail movie exposure, genre-group exposure, and calibration. Base rankers include TopPop, item-kNN, and BPR-MF . RecBole standardizes training and evaluation . FAIRGate operates on the top-$M=100$ candidate pool and delivers $K=10$ items. The main baselines are the base BPR-MF ranker, a static long-tail re-ranker, a deterministic fixed-order FAIRGate, an agentic variant without validation, and the full agentic FAIRGate. The key comparison is deterministic fixed-order FAIRGate versus full agentic FAIRGate because it isolates the value of runtime policy selection under the same policy library and utility budget. Utility metrics are NDCG@10, Recall@10, and relative NDCG loss. Exposure metrics are LT-Cov@10, exposure Gini, and policy-pass rate. Calibration uses Jensen-Shannon divergence between the user's historical genre distribution and the delivered list. Agent metrics are validator rejection rate and selected-policy agreement with a validation-derived oracle policy label. Narration metrics are unsupported claim rate, evidence coverage, audit-faithfulness rate, and sensitive-attribute leakage rate. Thresholds $ _T$, $ _G$, $ _C$, and $ $ are selected on validation data and frozen before test evaluation. The same user set and candidate pools are used across methods. For policy selection, the oracle label is defined as the validation policy that gives the largest exposure-policy gain while satisfying the utility budget. This label is not used to control the agent at test time. It serves only as an evaluation reference for RQ3. Narration evaluation is separate from ranking evaluation. Each explanation is decomposed into atomic claims. A claim is supported only when it maps to a typed audit key, such as policy selection, rule trigger, item movement, validator rejection, relaxation event, abstention reason, or post-gate metric. This prevents the narration agent from receiving credit for unsupported fairness language. The controller is implemented as a wrapper around a ranker that returns a scored candidate pool. The base model writes one record per user request containing candidate identifiers, relevance scores, popularity tier, genre-group labels, and content tags. A metric service computes the runtime state $x_u$ before the agent is called, so the LLM does not compute exposure or calibration from raw logs. For MovieLens 1M, the policy library includes $P_T$, $P_G$, $P_C$, $P_{Cal}$, and $P_A$, where $P_G$ is defined over genre-group exposure and $P_C$ enforces a concentration cap on the dominant group. The policy-selection step uses structured generation with schema validation and hard trigger enforcement. The agent is evaluated by whether it chooses a policy that improves the selected exposure objective under the validation-selected utility budget. The repair executor builds a replacement set from candidates outside the base top-$K$ whose relevance scores fall within a tolerance band of the item being replaced. It then selects the swap that gives the largest improvement in the selected policy objective while passing the calibration guard. The system logs the base list, selected policy object, validator report, and final explanation for each request. These artifacts make failed requests diagnosable as metadata gaps, missing feasible replacements, utility-budget conflicts, or calibration conflicts. We run the experiments on a reproducible random sample of 1000 MovieLens 1M users. This sampling choice is made to keep LLM-call cost and latency manageable while using the same sampled users across methods. This is restrictive in top-$K$ recommendation because feasible repair changes by request: some users have relevant long-tail candidates, others are calibration-sensitive, and some requests show concentration or metadata gaps. Agentic FAIRGate addresses this heterogeneity at the policy-selection layer, not the enforcement layer. The agent selects among predefined policies using runtime evidence, while deterministic validation still authorizes delivery. This distinction is central to the design. It allows the system to respond differently across recommendation contexts while keeping final decisions bounded by explicit policy semantics and deterministic validation. This also explains why Agentic FAIRGate should be compared against both static re-ranking and fixed-order gating. Static re-ranking tests whether a single exposure correction is enough. Fixed-order gating tests whether deterministic policy priority is enough. Agentic FAIRGate tests whether runtime policy choice adds value when multiple fairness actions are available but only some are appropriate for the current request. The evaluation therefore measures not only whether exposure metrics improve, but also whether policy choice, repair feasibility, and audit faithfulness work together as a delivery-control mechanism. The current evaluation uses MovieLens 1M, so the results should be read as evidence from a controlled movie-recommendation setting rather than as a general claim across all domains. Movie metadata supports genre-group and popularity analysis, but it does not provide a rich provider-side marketplace structure. A natural next step is to evaluate FAIRGate on datasets with clearer creator, seller, or provider labels, including book or marketplace datasets such as Goodbooks-10k when their metadata supports the selected policy targets. The policy library also shapes the reachable fairness outcomes. FAIRGate selects among predefined policies; it does not create new fairness objectives at runtime. This design improves controllability and auditability, but it also means that missing policies must be added by system designers before deployment. Thresholds and utility budgets are selected on validation data, and different domains may require different policy definitions, metadata standards, and escalation rules. The narration evaluation depends on claim parsing and audit-key matching. These checks are stricter than open-ended explanation grading, which is useful for auditability, but they may undercount partially supported natural-language claims. Future work can add stronger claim decomposition, human review of borderline claims, and domain-specific audit keys. Another useful direction is to study policy selection under distribution shift, where candidate-pool composition and popularity dynamics change over time. FAIRGate turns fairness delivery into a bounded runtime-control problem. The agent receives structured metrics, selects from a fixed policy library, and proposes repair plans for the current recommendation context. Deterministic validation then decides whether the repaired list is deliverable. This separation gives the agent adaptive responsibility while keeping enforcement measurable and replayable. The results show three main findings. First, Agentic FAIRGate improves LT-Cov@10 and policy-pass rate over fixed alternatives while preserving most ranking utility. Second, validator-constrained repair prevents the agentic layer from treating every exposure gain as deliverable. Third, audit-grounded narration sharply reduces unsupported fairness claims compared with direct LLM explanation. Together, these findings support a practical path toward fairer recommendation delivery: adaptive at the policy-selection layer, deterministic at the enforcement layer, and auditable at the communication layer. {00} A.
