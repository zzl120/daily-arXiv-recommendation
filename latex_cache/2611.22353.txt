See Tables~-- for more qualitative examples.} Dense retrieval is now standard infrastructure for open-domain question answering and retrieval-augmented generation . A common operational assumption follows: a highly ranked dense neighbor is at least passage-like evidence, even if it is not the best evidence. This paper tests that assumption directly. We ask whether a dense retriever can rank a visibly non-semantic string above the best judged query-relevant passage for the same query. We introduce -Semantic Strings Expose emanticity Gaps in D Retrieval}, a diagnostic probe for single-vector dense retrieval. Given a query and retriever, it initializes a fixed-length visible filler string and proposes one-character replacements. It accepts a replacement only when the candidate's dense similarity to the query improves, rejects candidates containing query terms, and finally inserts the optimized string into the full corpus for ranking. The output is not a prompt injection, a hidden Unicode trick, or a query-term-repetition passage: it is an overt, non-semantic character string. Figure~ illustrates the failure mode with examples of queries. The main comparison is deliberately relative to judged evidence. For each query, we compare the optimized NONSENSE score to the highest-scoring judged query-relevant passage under the same retriever. On SciFact, SciDocs, FIQA, and NFCorpus, NONSENSE frequently beats QRP across E5, BGE, and Contriever; on several pairs, success exceeds 0.95 and median corpus rank is 1. ArguAna/BGE is a useful boundary case, indicating that success depends on the model--dataset geometry rather than following automatically from the search procedure. Score curves show that the strings cross the QRP baseline after hundreds to thousands of proposals, then continue improving. In a nutshell, this paper makes the following contributions: a black-box, fixed-length character-replacement probe for dense retrieval that forbids query-term copying and produces visibly non-semantic strings; a QRP-relative evaluation showing that these strings frequently outscore the retriever's strongest judged evidence and reach near-top full-corpus ranks; and budget, lexical, and ablation analyses showing that the effect is not explained by random strings, initialization choice, string length, or explicit lexical overlap. Dense retrieval learns embedding spaces for nearest-neighbor search, from dual-encoder question answering to hard-negative training, distillation, and unsupervised contrastive learning . E5 and BGE are widely used embedding families ; late-interaction and reranking models are stronger but costlier alternatives . Sparse lexical retrieval such as BM25 remains a standard comparison point . Benchmarks such as MS MARCO, BEIR, KILT, SciFact, SciDocs, FIQA, NFCorpus, and ArguAna evaluate whether relevant documents are retrieved . NONSENSE asks the complementary question: can non-documents also become nearest neighbors? Adversarial NLP has shown that character-, token-, and black-box methods can manipulate classifiers and entailment systems , paralleling classic adversarial examples in vision . Behavioral testing motivates targeted probes beyond aggregate accuracy , and retrieval poisoning injects adversarial passages into corpora or RAG pipelines . Our goal is narrower: remove payload semantics and lexical overlap to isolate a representational weakness in retrieval scoring geometry. The closer analogy is to fooling images: off-manifold inputs that receive high model confidence despite lacking human-recognizable semantics . We ask whether dense similarity can likewise be high for text that is not a passage at all. Let $f_q$ and $f_p$ be the retriever's query and passage encoders. For a query $q$, NONSENSE maintains a fixed-length candidate string $s$, initialized by repeating ``'' to 256 characters in the default setting. At each generation, the algorithm samples one mutable position and replaces its character with a visible character drawn from the active alphabet; with frozen spaces, positions initialized as spaces are never mutated. Query terms are lowercased alphanumeric spans matched by ; any candidate containing a query term of length at least three is rejected before scoring. A replacement is accepted only if it improves cosine similarity $ , f_p )$. Thus the main experiment is fixed-length and replacement-only. After $50,000$ proposal steps, we insert the final evolved string into the full corpus and rank it by dense similarity. The key comparison is not only rank. NONSENSE shows that, in the evaluated dense retrievers, nearest-neighbor status and passage-like meaning can separate sharply. QRP-relative success asks whether a non-passage can beat the strongest judged evidence under the same model, score, and corpus; frequently, it can. Across five datasets and three retrievers, optimized non-semantic strings often outscore the retriever's own highest-scoring judged relevant passage and rank near the top of the full corpus. Since the probe uses only black-box scoring, repeated queries can in principle synthesize strings that, if inserted into a searchable corpus, populate the index with non-semantic nearest neighbors. The resulting signal is diagnostic but operationally relevant: dense retrieval systems should not assume that high similarity is sufficient semantic evidence. Robust pipelines should audit negative neighborhoods and complement dense scores with lexical filters, passage-validity checks, or semantic verifiers before downstream use. NONSENSE is an offline diagnostic for three English single-vector retrievers under exact dense scoring, leaving late-interaction, hybrid, reranked, ANN, multilingual, and RAG for future work. Optimized strings did not transfer reliably across retrievers, so the probe is currently model-specific. The strings are not human-facing evidence; they test whether dense similarity alone can rank non-semantic text above judged relevant passages. All main runs use 256 sampled queries per dataset and retriever, three seeds , and 50k proposal steps per query. The 256-query sample is drawn from the judged-query set for each dataset. This gives a fixed cross-dataset evaluation size while remaining below the smallest available BEIR test set in our study: SciFact has 300 test queries, NFCorpus 323, FIQA 648, SciDocs 1,000, and ArguAna 1,406. The choice also aligns with the batched GPU execution used by the optimizer, which evaluates one proposal per query in batches of 256. We evaluate E5-base-v2, BGE-base-en-v1.5, and Contriever with cosine-normalized dense similarity. E5 uses the standard query and passage prefixes. For each dataset--retriever pair, we precompute full-corpus passage embeddings and rank the final NONSENSE string after inserting it into the complete corpus. The datasets cover scientific fact verification , scientific document retrieval , financial question answering , argument retrieval , and medical information retrieval . Corpus sizes are listed in Table~. We use judged relevant documents from the dataset qrels to define QRP. When a query has multiple judged relevant passages, QRP is the one with the highest dense similarity under the evaluated retriever. The optimizer only queries the retriever as an embedding scorer. It does not use gradients, model weights, token-level saliency, or training data. Each proposal replaces one visible character, keeping the string length fixed, and is accepted only when the dense score improves. The query-term ban is applied before scoring a candidate, so accepted strings cannot succeed by copying obvious lexical evidence from the query. In the main setting, the edit alphabet is ASCII letters, digits, punctuation characters Runs were executed in batched GPU jobs on H200 GPU nodes. A main run optimizes 256 queries jointly for 50k generations, proposing one replacement per query per generation and encoding valid proposals in batches of 256. Across the 45 completed main jobs recorded for the full run in the SLURM logs, wall-clock time ranges from 106.3 to 189.5 minutes per dataset--retriever--seed run, with mean 125.9 minutes and median 117.5 minutes. The method is therefore efficient as an offline audit: each run optimizes 256 queries in parallel, uses only black-box scoring calls, and can be distributed over datasets, retrievers, and seeds. We report seed means in the main table because the optimizer is stochastic and the sampled query set depends on the seed. We keep the main metric set intentionally small: QRP score, NONSENSE score, QRP-relative success, and full-corpus mean/median rank. We omit BM25 from the main table because BM25 is a control rather than the evaluated dense model; the control-baseline appendix reports BM25@1 where lexical matching is the question. The controls are designed to rule out three simple explanations. First, the blank and random-string rows test whether arbitrary off-manifold text is already highly ranked. They are not: success is essentially zero and mean ranks are in the thousands. Second, matched random edits test whether the large edit budget alone creates strong candidates. It does not. Third, query-term repetition verifies that a lexical copying baseline can win, while also showing why it is a different phenomenon: it copies query terms and becomes a BM25 top hit. Optimized NONSENSE provides the diagnostic contrast: it wins dense similarity without query-term overlap and without lexical retrieval success. Table~ reports QRP-relative success at earlier optimization budgets. The effect is visible well before the final 50k proposals: the all-model success rate is 0.205 at 1k, 0.591 at 5k, and 0.729 at 10k. This does not make the method a low-cost online attack, but it shows that the audit does not rely solely on an endpoint after exhaustive search. Table~ audits lexical leakage in the final main runs. The implemented query-term tests never fire in the final optimized strings: both substring and exact alphanumeric rates are 0.000. BM25@1 is low but not identically zero outside the SciFact control setting, which is why we treat BM25 as a diagnostic rather than as part of the main dense-retrieval claim. Table~ is designed to distinguish dense-similarity success from readable evidence, lexical copying, and ordinary sparse retrieval. Query-term rates are computed after lowercasing and extracting alphanumeric spans with the same rule used by the optimizer. Exact Rate requires a banned query term to appear as a complete alphanumeric span, while Query-Term Rate also counts substring matches. Dictionary words are counted over alphabetic spans after lowercasing; the near-zero counts indicate that optimized strings rarely form ordinary English words. Alpha Tokens count alphabetic spans regardless of whether they are dictionary words, and Non-Alnum. reports the fraction of characters outside letters and digits. These diagnostics are lexical rather than mechanistic. They do not identify which tokenizer units, embedding axes, or pooling behaviors produce high dense similarity. Instead, they establish the narrower claim needed for the main result: optimized NONSENSE strings beat QRP without obvious query copying, BM25 success, or readable word formation. A fuller mechanistic account of tokenization patterns and embedding directions is left for future work. NONSENSE can be used as a stress test for a dense retriever before deployment. A practitioner can sample queries, run the black-box optimizer under the same encoder and preprocessing used in production, and compare optimized strings against the strongest judged or pseudo-judged relevant passages. High success is best interpreted as evidence that the embedding space contains non-passage nearest neighbors for those queries. Such systems may benefit from hybrid lexical-dense scoring, candidate-type filters, out-of-distribution detectors, cross-encoder verification, or reader-side evidence checks. The effect varies by model and dataset, with ArguAna/BGE providing a clear boundary case. This variation is useful: it suggests the probe can distinguish model--dataset pairs where off-manifold strings are easier or harder to place near queries. Future audits should test newer embedding models, larger and multilingual corpora, approximate-nearest-neighbor retrieval stacks, late-interaction and hybrid systems, semantic-verifier defenses, and mechanistic analyses of tokenizer and embedding-space behavior. Cross-encoder rerankers, hybrid lexical-dense scoring, and OOD filters are natural mitigation candidates for empirical evaluation. The results should be read as evidence about the geometry exposed by the evaluated scoring functions. QRP-relative success is intentionally stringent because QRP is selected by the same retriever being audited: the comparison asks whether the model's own strongest judged evidence can be displaced by a non-passage under identical scoring. The lexical diagnostics then separate this effect from ordinary term matching. Taken together, the main table, score curves, and controls indicate that dense similarity can assign high rank to inputs that satisfy the model objective while falling outside the document assumptions under which retrieval outputs are normally consumed.
