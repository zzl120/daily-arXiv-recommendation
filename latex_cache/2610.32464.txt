By we mean, operationally, any information outside the agent's current inference call that the system can write or modify and later use to condition the agent's behavior. This definition encompasses a vector store of extracted facts, a directory of notes, a library of executable skills, a relational log of past episodes, and parameters updated after deployment. The context window is not memory in this sense; it is where memory is , and the boundary between the two --- what is promoted into context, when, and in what form --- is itself one of the central research questions . Psychology and AI give the term different emphases. In psychology, memory is the faculty that lets an organism retain and recall experiences, knowledge, and skills~. In AI, memory is usually described in terms of persisting state, retaining conversational context, or learning continually without catastrophic forgetting~. Neither definition alone structures the questions of system design. We therefore adopt a system-level view with four components: objects , procedures , applications , and optimization . It classifies , but does not directly specify how a system should match, update, or evaluate each item. We therefore add a complementary computational lens. Using terminology from mathematics, we organize the contents of an agent's memory into three levels: $C$ are discrete pieces of information, such as the user's passport expiry, an API endpoint, or the outcome of a past episode. They are individually meaningful, often amenable to structured representation, and matched by content. $F$ are mappings from inputs to outputs, such as how to change a seat on an airline's site or debug a particular class of build failure. An episode can be recorded as a set of constants: what happened, what was tried, and what failed. Generalizing across recurring episodes to form a reusable procedure lifts constants into a function. Generalizing recurring corrections across procedures --- for example, repeated rejections of overnight options --- lifts them into an operator. Current systems commonly store episodes and extracted facts; some also build skills or consolidate preferences. How to perform these lifting operations, and when abstraction helps rather than hurts, remain open questions. Databases and knowledge bases provide schemas, keys, consistency, and versioning for structured constants. IR remains relevant to unstructured constants and becomes especially central for functions and operators. These objects are often expressed in natural language, can be noisy or inconsistent, and must be matched by applicability or scope inferred from the current task. The difference is emphasis: that taxonomy is organized by the purpose of retrieval; ours is organized by the algebraic structure of the content itself, which determines how an item must be matched, composed, and updated. The next memory to organize is the one inside the agent. Almost sixty years ago, Gerard Salton, the pioneer of Information Retrieval , described IR as a field ``concerned with the structure, analysis, organization, storage, searching, and retrieval of information''~. This definition applies no matter whether the system in question is a Boolean retrieval engine, a learning-to-rank pipeline, a recommender system, a question-answering service, or a conversational assistant. IR research has consistently examined how to organize information, match it to a need, and evaluate its quality. Yet, these questions usually carry an implicit assumption: who reads it, judges it, and uses it to finish a task. With geneartive AI today, this arrangement is changing. In a growing range of applications, people are asking AI assistants to read, filter, summarize, and act on information on their behalf. Metzler et al.~ anticipated part of this shift when they argued for replacing the index-and-rank pipeline with model-based ``domain experts.'' While much of the subsequent work in the IR community pursued one interpretation of this vision , a more intuitive and powerful version of it, i.e., the Large Language Model , has been studied and developed outside the IR community. Now, a second wave of shifts, i.e., the rise of AI agents, is already under way. When an agent performs a multi-step task for a user --- booking a trip, refactoring a module, or preparing a literature review --- information is no longer a person directly, but becomes the internal state for the system that does the work. The agent must decide when to collect information, what to keep or discard at each step, and how to let what it retrieves shape its behavior. Those decisions not only affect the agent's capabilities for problem solving, but also largely determine whether it can improve with experience~ or simply repeats previous mistakes. The problem has moved inside the agent, where it is often referred to as the . Therefore, in parallel with Salton's definition of IR, in this paper, we define the research on this problem as , which concerns with the structure, analysis, organization, storage, searching, retrieval, and of information in the memory of an intelligent agent. Our position is that AMM could be viewed as a new form of IR problems in the era of generative AI agents. On the one hand, a significant proportion of AMM studies have direct or implicit connections to problems that have been widely studied in the IR community. In fact, research on AMM from IR perspectives is already substantial. For example, studies on how to understand and store conversational state~, building libraries of reusable skills~, extracting and consolidating user facts~, or organizing notes into evolving knowledge networks~ have already been using retrieval techniques as core parts of their work. On the other hand, AMM can bring completely new challenges and ideas that significantly boarden the scope of IR research. The shifting from serving users to serving AI agents in AMM could reshape the way we think about IR. It brings new opportunities to move IR systems from tools that supply information to modules that directly support the development of continue learning agents and solve an important part of the puzzle to AGI. The methodology and research experience from decades of studies on information users and retrieval systems could give IR researchers unique advantages in tackling problems in AMM, particularly at its early stage. Recent surveys try to document the area of AMM systematically~, but they found that the theory and foundation of it is still consolidating. Diverse terminology and frameworks, many borrowed from the studies of human memory~, have been proposed to categorize how information should be organized and used in AMM. Without rigid theoretical or systematic grounding, these make most AMM studies look more like ah-hoc engineering efforts rather than scientific works. Existing AMM benchmarks often focus on narrow and specific task scenarios or agent behaviors without a well-defined evaluation protocol and roadmap. These limit the reusibility of AMM benchmarks and prevent the field to reach boarder audience. In contrast, IR community has long history and rish experience on user analysis and evaluation methodology. While the task formats may look different, the core behind scene is similar. For example, the way how Broder~ developed the taxonomy of Web search could be used in the analysis of agent's information needs; the Crandfield paradigms widely used in TREC~ and NTCIR~ could serve as a starting point to inspire new ideas for AMM benchmark construction. Experience and methodology used in IR studies could provide important guidelines to help us better craft the structure of AMM research. In this paper, we provide a systematic view to bridge AMM with IR. We first discuss the paradigms commonly used in traditional IR research, particularly the lens from the user perspective and the system perspective. Then we provide a system and computation oriented taxonomy of AMM from four perspectives, i.e. objects, produres, applications, and optimizations, through which we can better identify the connections and differences between AMM and estabilished IR problems. \\ IR made competing claims comparable through shared evaluation protocols, from the Cranfield experiments~ to TREC~ and NTCIR~, and progress in neural IR accelerated when large shared collections became available~. AMM benchmarks now exist~. Most concentrate on conversational fact recall, although some have begun to include streams of user feedback~; recent analysis also raises concerns about saturation, the validity of automated judges, backbone sensitivity, and incomplete cost reporting~. Taking the Cranfield paradigm as a starting point, we suggest five desiderata for the next generation of AMM benchmarks. A benchmark should test constants, functions, and operators separately and in composition. Recalling a stated fact and acquiring a workflow that transfers to a new site are different capabilities, and a single aggregate score obscures which capabilities a system has acquired. Most benchmarks test utilization on a preconstructed memory store. Learning (what does the system choose to store from a raw episode?) and forgetting need first-class tasks with deliberately misleading, outdated, or contradictory episodes, and deletion requests that must actually take effect. Optimization cannot be evaluated on static data. A benchmark that hopes to measure whether a system must supply a stream of tasks with outcomes, corrections, and delayed rewards. Multi-user, multi-session designs are needed to detect a failure with serious consequences in deployment: a preference learned from one user or one session leaking into contexts where it does not hold. Results should be replicated across at least two backbones, and token, latency, and storage costs reported alongside quality, so that cost--quality trade-offs are visible rather than hidden. New terminology can obscure useful connections to established work. For example, contrastive objectives used in representation learning~ share structural features with ranking losses developed earlier~. AMM research on deduplication, temporal validity, and feedback scope can likewise build on related IR literature. Table~ is our attempt at a translation table. The mapping instead allows researchers to build on a substantial body of prior work. As Section~ notes, IR advanced through a combination of system building and foundational work. Its foundational work provided explicit accounts of relevance and retrieval models derived from them~, giving later researchers clear assumptions to test and extend. As data and model capacity grew, learned systems increasingly absorbed factors that these theories had enumerated explicitly~, which accelerated progress but provided less conceptual scaffolding. Neither route currently serves AMM well. An end-to-end learning approach is difficult to apply because the system is not one differentiable model but an assembly of prompts, stores, policies, and a foundation model whose retraining is out of reach for most researchers. At the same time, system designs often rely on analogies to human memory --- humans have episodic memory, so the system has an episodic store --- without stating the conditions under which that design should help. A productive next step is to state hypotheses about memory systems, derive observable consequences, and test them. We therefore state three candidate principles as conjectures to be tested rather than as established results. A memory system should prioritize items for retention and retrieval according to their expected marginal contribution to future task reward, net of the associated costs --- storage, retrieval, context occupancy, and interference with other items. This formulation deliberately mirrors the probability ranking principle~, and it inherits both its appeal and its weaknesses: the expectation is over an unknown future task distribution, and marginal contribution is not additive across items. Lifting constants into functions and functions into operators increases transfer but also increases the risk of misapplication. For a given task distribution, there may therefore be an optimal level of abstraction. One testable corollary is that systems that aggressively summarize episodes into general rules should outperform verbatim storage on task distributions with high repetition, and underperform it on heterogeneous ones. A correction should be applied at the narrowest scope that explains it: the system should prefer a session-specific scope to a user-specific one, and a user-specific scope to a cohort-wide one. The scope should widen only with repeated, independent evidence. Violating this principle offers one plausible explanation for personalization failures in which an agent overgeneralizes a local correction. Each conjecture is testable with the benchmark designs of Section~. Evidence that refutes any of them would still sharpen the research agenda. A memory system can improve from experience through parametric updates to model weights and through non-parametric updates to the content and structure of its store. For non-parametric updates, something must play the role that gradient descent plays for parametric models. Today that role is usually filled by asking a language model to revise its own notes, an approach formalized as textual ``gradients''~. It is often effective in practice, but current accounts provide limited guidance about when repeated revision improves task performance, whether the process converges, or how to distinguish a useful update from mere rephrasing. These questions mark a substantial theoretical gap. We see four concrete directions. First, : under what conditions does iterated textual revision converge, and can guarantees of monotonic improvement be obtained under assumptions about feedback quality? Second, : attributing a delayed, sparse task outcome to decisions along a trajectory of writing, retention, retrieval, and use is a counterfactual estimation problem, for which IR's work on counterfactual and online learning to rank~ provides a relevant toolkit. Third, : some knowledge belongs in weights, whereas other knowledge belongs in text. The boundary is currently determined by convenience, and a principled account of consolidation --- what to compile into parameters, when, and how to reverse it --- is missing. Fourth, : the organization of the store is itself a decision variable, and changing it changes what can be retrieved. Finally, the user view of Section~ should be applied to the new user. Agent interaction logs invite analyses analogous to those applied to query logs~: what agents retrieve, when they retrieve it, how often retrieval proves unhelpful, and what a trajectory of memory operations looks like when a task goes wrong. Such analyses could lead to a taxonomy of agent information needs, in the way that Broder's taxonomy characterized web search~. Memory presentation deserves the attention that result-page design once received, since format, granularity, ordering, and attribution all change whether an item is used. Agents can also be trained to search their own memory more effectively, which is itself an IR problem. Human control remains essential. Because memory is personal, persistent, and consequential, people need to see, correct, and delete what an agent has remembered about them. Deletion must remain meaningful even after the content has been lifted into a skill or consolidated into parameters. Memory is also an attack surface: poisoning of memory stores~ and membership inference against them~ are emerging concerns. These are governance questions, but they are also retrieval questions, and they will not be answered by systems work alone. In the 1960s, the Cranfield experiments established the practice of comparing systems on a shared set of documents, queries, and relevance judgments~, and Salton's SMART project developed the vector space model, term weighting, and relevance feedback~. The 1970s and 1980s added formal foundations, including inverse document frequency~, the probability ranking principle~, and cognitive accounts of why people search~. When it came to the 1990s, with the rise of Internet, the importance of such ``memory systems'' has become even more significant. Vinton G. Cerf, one of the fathers of Internet, argued that search engines are the ``substitutes for human memory'' and the ``cognitive implants'' of human~. From 1992, the Text REtrieval Conference extended evaluation methodologies to much larger collections and to new tasks~. Ranking functions such as BM25~ and statistical language models~ were refined in that setting, which led to search products that shape the world~. In the same decade, collaborative filtering systems began to recommend items from the ratings of similar users rather than from an explicit query~, and reommendation systems started to attract more and more attention in both industry and academia. Ever since then, the shape of IR research remained relatively stable for nearly three decades. While advanced methods have greatly improved the performance of modern IR systems, the research agenda behind them remained mostly recognizable: analyze information, match it to a need, serve it to a user, and measure whether that worked. Thus, we can easily categorize classic IR research into two types: the studies that tackle IR more from user's perspectives, or the , and the studies that focus more on the IR systems themseleves, or the . The user view, closely allied with human--computer interaction and information science, focus one studies that help human users better seek and use information. This could be boardly described from three directions: understanding users, serving users, and evaluating IR systems for users. usually begins with observations on user behaviors. It aims to provide insights and models that better help us understand and interpret how users use IR systems. For example, an analysis of about one billion AltaVista queries found that web users typed short queries, mostly examined only the first ten results, and seldom modified their queries, which means that web users differed substantially from the user assumed in the IR literature of the time~; Studies of web search showed that many queries are not informational: a user may want to reach a particular site or to complete a transaction~. Eye-tracking studies found that clicks are informative but biased, among other factors by the position of a result in the ranking~, and click models were developed to separate relevance from such biases~. Methods for query understanding, such as spelling correction, intent classification, and query reformulation, build on these observations, as does user profiling for personalization and recommendation. draws on models of human cognition. It aims to craft systems that directly fit the behaviors and cognitive process of search or recommendation users. One of the most well known examples are the studies of search interfaces such as ranking-based search result page, result snippets, query suggestions, faceted navigation, etc.~. There are also famous studies that touch the interdiscipline of computer science and psychology, such as Belkin's anomalous-state-of-knowledge hypothesis ~, the modeling of human learning process with search engines~. translates assumptions and knowledge about human users into measurements that guide the development of IR systems. For instance, discounted cumulative gain tries to distinguish good ranking systems from bad ones by reducing the credit for relevant results at lower ranks because users are less likely to examine them~; Diversity measures reward rankings that cover the different intents behind an ambiguous query~ so that we can better estimate the effectiveness of retrieval systems in real serving environments; Fairness measures ask whether the exposure that a ranking allocates to items and their providers is proportional to their merit~. The system view studies how information should be represented, matched, and delivered at scale. means analyzing the collection before any query arrives. Link analysis treats a hyperlink as evidence of a page's importance and derives authority scores such as PageRank~. The cluster hypothesis, that closely associated documents tend to be relevant to the same requests, motivates clustering for retrieval and browsing~. Content quality can be estimated from features such as readability, layout, and ease of navigation, and used to promote better documents in the ranking~. has two goals. For effectiveness, retrieval models estimate how well an item satisfies a query, from the vector space model~ and probabilistic models such as BM25~ to neural rankers~. Recommendation models perform the same matching without an explicit query, for example by factorizing a matrix of user--item interactions~. For efficiency, an inverted index maps each term to the documents that contain it, so that a query touches only a small fraction of the collection~, and compression, caching, and dynamic pruning further reduce storage and response time. Query performance prediction estimates, without relevance judgments, whether a query is likely to be answered well~, which can guide whether to apply costly steps such as query expansion. covers cost as well as quality: time and space complexity, index size, latency and throughput, energy use and emissions~, and operation in low-resource settings with limited hardware or training data. The two views are not separate research areas; they often address the same problem from opposite directions. Relevance is a human judgment in the user view and a quantity to estimate in the system view; the probability ranking principle connects the two by showing that, under stated assumptions, ranking by the probability of relevance yields the best effectiveness obtainable from the available data~. Clicks are evidence about behavior in the user view and training signals in the system view; unbiased learning to rank uses models of position bias from the former to train rankers in the latter~. An ambiguous query can be handled from the user side, by an interface that suggests refinements or asks for clarification, or from the system side, by diversifying the ranking. Offline evaluation abstracts the user into relevance judgments so that systems can be compared cheaply and repeatably~, and user studies test whether those comparisons reflect what people experience. Both views ultimately serve the same end. Search, recommendation, advertising, question answering, and personal assistants differ in their interfaces and signals, but each is valuable to the extent that it helps people satisfy an information need or complete a task. Three observations from this history recur in the rest of the paper. First, task completion is the final goal, but conventional IR systems observe only part of the task, so the field built its methodology around measurable proxies; Section~ argues that this constraint weakens when the user is an agent. Second, IR advanced through a combination of system building and foundational work, including shared decision principles, reusable models, and test collections that made results comparable; agent-memory research would benefit from the same combination. Third, from the memex onward, both views assumed that the consumer of retrieved information is a person; Section~ examines what changes when the consumer is an agent. Viewing AMM as a new form of IR problem invites an obvious question: if it is IR, what is new, and if it is new, why is it IR? The main differences are that memory content is often rather than authored independently, and is frequently user-specific. This can make some established problems easier and others harder (there may be no crowd to aggregate over or anchor text, and personal data entail obligations that a web crawl does not). Here the change is substantial. IR's user models describe people: their needs, their cognitive limits, their behavior under uncertainty. AMM's consumer is a model. Without assuming humanlike cognition, we can still measure regularities that play a role similar to behavioral biases in click modeling. A model attends unevenly to long inputs, with strong position effects~. It may over-rely on retrieved content that contradicts its parametric knowledge, or under-use content that is correct but unconventionally formatted. Its propensity to retrieve at all depends on how the task is framed. This suggests an explicit research target: a for agents, the analogue of a click model, that predicts whether a given memory item, in a given position and format, will actually change behavior. Such models could serve the same three roles as click models: diagnostic instruments, components of offline evaluation, and sufficiently differentiable proxies to optimize against. Treating the agent as a user also creates useful experimental opportunities. A model can be run repeatedly, instrumented in detail, and replayed under controlled variations. Experiments that are difficult to conduct with human participants can become practical. This experimental advantage comes with the risk of non-stationarity: an agent's behavior changes with every model version, so studies must identify the backbone and, ideally, replicate their findings across backbones. Recent analyses report that memory-system performance varies substantially with the backbone~, so this is a practical rather than a hypothetical concern. The main consequence is methodological. IR's ultimate goal is to help someone finish a task, but conventional IR systems often see only part of it. A search engine observes a query, a ranking, and some clicks; it does not observe whether the report was written, the trip was booked, or the bug was fixed. The field's response became one of its central methodological conventions: hold the downstream task constant by assumption, and measure the intermediate quantity we believe predicts it --- relevance of a ranked list, evaluated on a reusable test collection~. Proxy measures are especially important when the final objective lies outside the system's view. In AMM, this constraint weakens. The consumer of the information is a system that we can instrument. We can observe which items were retrieved, whether they entered the context, whether the agent's action changed as a result, and whether the task succeeded. We can re-run the same model and task with a different memory state, enabling a more controlled counterfactual comparison than is usually possible after a person has experienced one condition~. Evaluation can therefore connect component behavior to downstream outcomes more directly. We call this shift the : downstream use becomes more measurable, while topical relevance becomes less informative on its own. This argument requires two qualifications. First, end-to-end task success memory quality with the agent's competence: a strong backbone can compensate for a bad memory, and a weak one can waste a good one. Second, outcome-only evaluation provides no diagnosis, so a failure cannot readily be attributed to writing, retention, retrieval, or use. The resolution is not to abandon component metrics but to change their status. In established IR, a ranking metric is a for an objective that is usually only partly observable. In AMM, more of the objective is observable, so component measurements can become that explain an outcome. This suggests evaluating memory systems as a stack: --- is the appropriate information stored at the right level of abstraction, without contradiction; --- is the stored item retrieved when it is needed and does it change behavior when retrieved; --- does the task succeed under a stated backbone; and --- what are the token, latency, and storage costs? Cost must be reported alongside quality, since an accuracy improvement obtained at five times the cost may not provide a net benefit. AMM is currently studied mostly in the NLP, ML, and agent communities, and much of that work already draws on retrieval. Our claim is that IR offers additional concepts and methods that should inform this work more systematically. IR aims to help people satisfy information needs and complete tasks . AMM aims to provide an agent with the information it needs to complete a task on a user's behalf. AMM adds an intermediary while preserving the same objective. Relevant methods include indexing, retrieval and ranking, recommendation, clustering, query reformulation, relevance feedback, compression, caching, and evaluation design. Structurally, a memory system can be viewed as a small, personal, write-heavy, feedback-rich retrieval system. IR has shown how a proxy that diverges from the objective can distort a system and how implicit feedback can mislead when position and selection biases are ignored~. It has also studied whether reported improvements survive comparison with fair baselines and how shared benchmarks can either focus a field or narrow it prematurely. These lessons can inform the design of agent-memory systems and evaluations. Two comparative advantages are worth naming. The first is . IR has spent decades building reproducible ways to study users and to evaluate systems that serve them, including simulation as a first-class methodology~. When the user becomes an agent, parts of that methodological tradition transfer naturally and complement current memory evaluation. The second is , from index structures and update policies to modeling feedback from noisy interactions. We stress that this is an argument for contribution, not for ownership. The shift toward generative information access is instructive: a vision articulated in IR~ was realized mainly through LLMs developed outside the IR community . AMM offers another opportunity for IR to shape a transition that draws directly on its core expertise. Information increasingly reaches people only after a model has processed it. A search engine summarizes results in an answer box, a conversational assistant synthesizes several sources into a single reply, and an agent asked to book a trip reads airline pages and returns only the finished itinerary~. Retrieval has not disappeared, since retrieval-augmented generation~ made it a standard component of LLM systems, but the retrieved text is now often read by a model before, or instead of, a person. We describe the resulting change along two dimensions. The first concerns the : when an agent rather than a person reads the retrieved information, both how that information is processed and how it should be presented change . The second concerns the of IR: IR can remain an external tool that the agent calls, as it has always been for people, or it can become an internal module that shapes the agent's behavior . The two changes are parallel rather than alternative, and AMM is the setting in which both apply . Much of what IR knows about its users describes people rather than information . When the consumer becomes an agent, this knowledge must be re-examined rather than assumed to transfer. IR has long studied human cognition in order to serve people better. The anomalous-state-of-knowledge hypothesis explains why people struggle to specify what they need~, and eye-tracking and click studies show that people examine results from the top and trust higher-ranked ones~. Agents process information in ways that such models do not predict. In a study of retrieval-augmented question answering, high-scoring retrieved passages that did not contain the answer reduced accuracy, whereas adding random passages improved it by up to 35\ A person who finds the sentence ``ignore your previous instructions'' on a web page does not treat it as a command addressed to them, whereas an LLM-integrated application may follow instructions injected into the content it retrieves~. Some human constraints also disappear: an agent can read all hundred results of a query or issue dozens of reformulations, at a cost measured in tokens rather than in attention or patience. The ``cognitive'' behavior of agents therefore needs to be studied in its own right, through observational and controlled studies of how agents seek, read, and use information, as studies of human searchers once informed the design of search systems . Search interfaces were designed for human perception. A result page shows about ten results in a vertical list ordered by estimated relevance, each with a title and a snippet that helps a person decide whether to click~. The design assumes that people scan from the top and that attention declines with rank, and discounted cumulative gain encodes the same assumption in evaluation~. An agent does not read a list sequentially. A language model processes its entire context at once, but it uses that context unevenly: accuracy tends to be highest when relevant information appears near the beginning or the end of the input and lowest when it appears in the middle~. Under such a U-shaped profile, placing the best item first is not necessarily the best arrangement, and a rank-discounted metric may misstate how useful a ranking is to an agent. The interface need not be visual either. An agent receives results as text or structured data through a tool call, so presentation becomes a question of selection, format, granularity, and length rather than layout. RECOMP, for example, compresses retrieved documents into short summaries that are trained to improve the downstream accuracy of the reading model rather than to be read by a person~. Interfaces for agents therefore call for new designs rather than adaptations of result pages built for people. The second change concerns where IR operates relative to its consumer. For people, IR has always been external. A search engine or recommender supplies information, but what a person does with it is up to that person; the system can rank, highlight, and suggest, but it cannot write into the person's memory or determine the next action. Because people cannot be redesigned, IR systems adapted to them: to their vocabulary, their biases, and their interfaces. Most current work on IR for agents keeps this external position. WebGPT answers questions by issuing commands to a text-based web browser~, ReAct interleaves reasoning steps with search actions~, and Toolformer learns when to call a search engine among other tools~. One difference from the human case already appears here: the consumer can also adapt to the tool. Search-R1 uses reinforcement learning to teach a model when and what to search~, an adjustment that IR could encourage in human users but not impose. The relation nonetheless remains external: the retrieval system returns results and has no direct control over how they are used. An agent also allows a position that a person does not: IR can be built inside the consumer. Here the memory system decides what the agent stores from each episode, what it retrieves at each step, and how the retrieved content conditions the next action, so IR becomes a module of intelligence as well as a tool for it. Memory in this position can shape many aspects of an agent. It can shape the agent's : in Generative Agents, each character starts from a set of seed memories, and the reflections synthesized from its memory stream inform how it plans and responds~. It can shape its : a travel assistant that remembers that a user rejects red-eye flights should override its default of minimizing cost . It can accumulate : Reflexion stores verbal reflections on failed attempts and conditions later attempts on them~. And it can guide : Voyager stores executable skills acquired in Minecraft and retrieves them to solve new tasks~. In each case, retrieved information does not merely inform the agent; it changes what the agent does. This position also makes memory currently the most practical route to continual learning for deployed agents: retraining the underlying model for every user, task, and correction is rarely feasible, so what the system learns after deployment often depends on what it writes down~. The two changes are parallel rather than competing. The consumer change applies even when IR stays external: a search tool that serves an agent must still account for how the agent processes results and in what form they should be delivered. The position change adds a possibility rather than replacing one. If people and agents are both viewed as managing information through external resources and internal memory, IR can act only on the external side for people but on both sides for agents. AMM is the combination in which both changes apply: the consumer is an agent, and IR operates inside it. There, the processing and presentation questions of Section~ reappear as questions about what to promote from memory into the agent's context, in what order, and in what form, and the questions of Section~ become questions about how memory should change behavior. The remainder of this paper develops this setting.
