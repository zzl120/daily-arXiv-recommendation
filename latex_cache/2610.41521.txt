As large language model agents engage in multi-session interactions, they need to carry useful knowledge forward from earlier exchanges. Although larger context windows expose more history, they do not inherently support selective retention . Persistent memory has therefore become a key component of long-term LLM-agent architectures, allowing relevant information to be retained and reused across sessions . {figures/Figure1_KITE.pdf} 's retrieval with explicit constraints.} In practice, many memory systems retrieve past information through nearest-neighbor search over embeddings . Although effective for semantic matching, single-vector similarity ranking alone does not ensure that the retrieved memories satisfy the conditions of the query . As Figure~ illustrates, semantically close memories may outrank evidence that answers the question . In addition, fixed top-$k$ selection returns candidates without explicitly indicating when none satisfies the query . Beyond retrieval, storing $N$ memories as $d$-dimensional dense vectors requires $O $ space, while encoder changes often require rebuilding the entire embedding index . These limitations of embedding-based retrieval motivate a formulation of memory retrieval as an explicit decision procedure that evaluates query conditions rather than relying on similarity ranking alone . By exposing matching conditions and intermediate results, this approach makes retrieval decisions inspectable and supports the diagnosis of retrieval failures . However, as conversations evolve, the same concepts may be represented by different surface labels or graph nodes, complicating consistent indexing . Moreover, questions may refer to stored information through different wording or implicit cues, making relevant evidence harder to retrieve . These challenges call for a governed knowledge index that evolves with the ongoing conversation, together with query compilation that is grounded in the same knowledge representation. To this end, we propose } , a vector-free framework for long-horizon conversational memory that organizes multi-session interactions into structured knowledge indexed by a governed topic vocabulary. Each memory entry is enriched with typed contextual and temporal facets, providing complementary access paths beyond topics. The validated plan is then executed through explicit lookup and ranking operations, making evidence selection deterministic. also supplements the primary path with sparse lexical retrieval to recover surface details missed during memory construction. Experiments on LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S show that achieves accuracies of 93.5\ The key contributions of this paper are as follows: We formulate memory retrieval as a decision procedure that evaluates explicit query conditions against indexed evidence. Validated plans are then executed through fixed lookup and ranking operations, making evidence selection deterministic per plan and inspectable. These mechanisms establish a practical alternative to similarity-based retrieval while maintaining high accuracy with compact reader contexts. We organize memories using a topic vocabulary that evolves across multi-session conversations under controlled expansion and consolidation, with typed facets for finer-grained access to evidence. This design limits topic fragmentation and keeps memories coherently organized as conversations grow. We align question interpretation with memory organization via a shared knowledge index. Mapping question concepts to existing index keys grounds retrieval plans in stored topics and entities, reducing the semantic gap between natural language and indexed knowledge. Extending the context window of language models is a direct approach to supporting long-horizon interaction . However, longer contexts increase inference costs, while model performance remains susceptible to position-dependent degradation . Persistent-memory systems instead preserve information across sessions, beyond the limits of the active context window. For example, MemGPT manages information transfer between the model context and the external storage. In addition, MemoryBank incorporates a time-dependent forgetting mechanism, whereas Mem0 extracts salient facts into an editable memory store and supports add, update, and delete operations. Beyond individual memory operations, several works investigate broader mechanisms for managing information accumulated over long interactions. A prominent direction explores system-level memory architectures with hierarchical storage and lifecycle control . Meanwhile, MEM1 consolidates interaction history into a compact internal state that supports continued reasoning. Together, they show that long-term memory depends not only on persistence, but also on how accumulated information is organized for later use. Recent studies have explored structured representations for organizing conversational memory. A-MEM builds interconnected notes whose attributes and links evolve as new memories arrive. Similarly, Zep organizes conversational knowledge as a temporal knowledge graph that tracks facts along with their validity over time. Alongside these relational approaches, Memanto structures memories through predefined semantic types, while EverMemOS consolidates episodic MemCells into thematic MemScenes. In general, these approaches provide richer structures for organizing related memories over time. As these structures grow, a related challenge is maintaining consistent representations of recurring concepts. EDC canonicalizes extracted relations into a consolidated schema, including settings without a predefined target schema. KGGen addresses the same problem from a graph-construction perspective by clustering entities and relations to reduce fragmentation. These works highlight the importance of keeping related concepts aligned and reducing redundant representations, providing a more stable basis for subsequent retrieval. Once memories are organized, retrieval must connect each question to the representations in which evidence is stored. Sparse lexical methods such as BM25 rank candidates by scoring matches between query terms and stored text. However, questions may express the same information in different words, making relevant memories harder to find through lexical matching alone. QueryLink addresses this mismatch by building semantic representations of questions and memories at different levels of detail for similarity-based retrieval. Complementary work makes memory access explicit through executable operations. ChatDB treats databases as symbolic memory, allowing an LLM to generate SQL instructions for memory retrieval and manipulation. More generally, Text2Mem translates natural-language memory commands into validated operations with a common execution interface. For more structured knowledge, KAG combines logical-form planning with hybrid retrieval over knowledge graphs and text, while supporting schema-free knowledge extraction. Executable programs can further support reasoning after retrieval, as in TReMu , which performs temporal calculations over retrieved timeline summaries. Taken together, these approaches establish executable operations as a general-purpose mechanism for explicit access and reasoning over stored evidence. This emphasis on explicit operations also aligns with diagnostic work on intermediate evidence. RAGChecker separates retrieval from generation to localize pipeline failures. In long-term memory, MemTrace distinguishes whether source evidence is reachable from whether it is successfully used. These evaluations highlight the value of making the evidence process inspectable rather than relying on final-answer accuracy alone. {figures/Figure2_KITE.pdf} . Rather than indexing raw dialogue directly, converts each conversation session into structured memory entries. Each entry can be represented as m= x_m, _m,T_m,A_m , where $x_m$ is the self-contained memory content and $ _m$ its provenance, including the source session and supporting utterances. For each session, the extraction model processes the dialogue with the current topic vocabulary as context, producing source-linked entries with facet annotations. Relative time expressions are also resolved against the date of the corresponding session whenever possible, while candidate memory entries without valid provenance are discarded. Rather than assuming a complete ontology in advance, derives its topic vocabulary from the accumulated conversation history. Starting from a small set of canonical roots, the extraction model reuses existing topics when possible and gradually proposes more specific children when new concepts emerge. To ensure consistency, new topics must fit within the existing hierarchy, which helps maintain a stable index over time. Vocabulary expansion is further controlled by keeping proposed topics provisional until they recur across multiple sessions and time periods, rather than admitting each new topic as soon as it first appears. As the vocabulary evolves, potentially redundant sibling topics are consolidated, with their memory entries redirected to the selected canonical topic. Structured memory is designed to capture reusable concepts and retrieval cues, but this abstraction may omit surface-form details preserved in the source text, such as rare names or numerical values. To retain access to such information, augments the indexed path with a deterministic sparse lexical channel. The ranked evidence from both paths is merged under a fixed fusion rule, without dense embeddings or neural reranking. A retrieval attempt may fail to produce a structured match without failing to find anything at all. In that case the result is recorded as a fallback, and the lexical channel may still provide evidence to the reader, marked as such rather than as a match. We introduced , a vector-free framework that formulates long-term conversational memory retrieval as an explicit decision procedure. To this end, organizes conversations into structured memories within a governed knowledge index keyed by topic. At query time, the same index guides the compilation of each question into a retrieval plan, aligning its interpretation with the structure of stored memories. The plan is then executed deterministically to select evidence that satisfies explicit contextual and temporal conditions. In this way, retrieval is driven by whether the available information meets the question's requirements, rather than by semantic similarity alone.
