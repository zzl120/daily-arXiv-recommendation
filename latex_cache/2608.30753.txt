Dual-encoder models retrieve candidates efficiently with approximate nearest-neighbor search , but often miss the complex, fine-grained relevance signals modeled by heavy downstream rerankers . Bridging this gap via online fine-tuning is challenging in industrial settings, where optimization must operate with a . Re-indexing billions of item vectors after every model update is computationally prohibitive, so optimization is constrained to the query encoder alone. We formulate this alignment task as a Reinforcement Learning problem, where the query encoder acts as an agent and receives reward signals from the downstream reranker . However, we identify a critical failure mode: standard Policy Gradient algorithms lead to severe performance deterioration, a phenomenon we term as . Our investigation suggests that the point-wise optimization of RL---specifically pushing away queries from low-reward items---acts destructively when the document space is immutable. Unlike contrastive learning where negatives are pushed relative to positives , pure RL pushing in a frozen space often displaces query embeddings into semantically ambiguous regions, shattering the distribution established by pre-training . To resolve this, we propose , a selective update mechanism for applying positive-advantage-only RL under the frozen-index constraint. We hypothesize that strictly pulling queries toward candidates with allows for fine-grained local alignment, while masking negative signals prevents structural disruption. We identify the geometric degradation caused by standard RL when fine-tuning query encoders against a frozen index. We propose PAO, a selective update mechanism that harmonizes retrieval-ranking consistency without compromising embedding space generalization. Extensive experiments on industrial and public datasets confirm PAO's superiority in both standard metrics and semantic alignment. {figure1_pao_overview.png} Dense retrieval has been advanced by pre-trained language models and contrastive objectives , with representative systems improving negative mining, embedding structure, and retrieval efficiency . However, most methods assume that both query and document encoders can be updated, or that document embeddings can be rebuilt after training. This assumption is often infeasible in industrial retrieval, where the document index is frozen and only the query encoder can be adapted online. Retriever-reranker alignment is commonly approached through knowledge distillation, where cross-encoder or reranker scores supervise a dual encoder . Such methods typically distill static teacher distributions over fixed candidate sets, whereas our setting uses feedback on the candidates retrieved by the current query policy. RL has also been studied for search and ranking , and recent work explores reinforcement learning for dense retrieval or reranker optimization . In contrast, we focus on query-only RL under a frozen document index, where negative policy-gradient updates can distort the embedding geometry. The method optimizes retrievability but cannot generate new knowledge; if relevant documents are absent from the index, PAO serves limited utility. Additionally, our framework assumes the Reward Model is a reliable proxy for user satisfaction. In scenarios where the ranker exhibits bias , PAO may efficiently propagate these biases into the retrieval stage. Addressing this reward hacking via multi-objective optimization remains a direction for future work. This paper presents a novel online fine-tuning framework for retrieval models under the strict constraint of a frozen document index. We demonstrate that the strategy effectively prevents the geometry collapse associated with standard RL by avoiding destructive updates from negative samples. Beyond immediate performance gains, PAO suggests a potential path toward closed-loop retriever-ranker adaptation: query-side retriever updates may expose harder and more relevant candidates to the ranker, while future ranker updates may provide sharper reward signals for subsequent retriever fine-tuning.
