To limit error accumulation, current methods verify each generated sequence remains predictive of the user's real interactions and discard those that drift away from it. Verification does not, however, determine which verified sequences should train the next model. With every verified sequence used for training, source sequences yielding more verified sequences or longer continuations have more influence, although neither quantity indicates how much those sequences will help the next model. We formulate the decision of which verified sequences are used to train the next model as and introduce { Disagreement-Aware Recursive Self-Improving Recommendation }. DA-RSIR caps each source sequence's contribution and ranks its verified sequences by how much the model's predictions disagree over their augmented interactions. It uses a score derived from Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement and estimated with Monte Carlo dropout. DA-RSIR requires no extra labels, teacher model, or quality scorer. Across four datasets, three recommender models, and two metrics, it improves on the retain-all approach in all $24$ comparisons and attains the highest mean in $23$ of $24$ overall; the aggregate improvement is statistically significant on both metrics. A single DA-RSIR round exceeds the retain-all approach's best gain over five recursive rounds. These findings establish post-verification acquisition as a separate control point in recursive self-improvement, separating which sequences pass verification from which verified sequences are used to train the next model. Sequential recommender systems predict a user's next interaction from their chronologically ordered history , but their effectiveness remains constrained by sparse and imbalanced interaction data . Augmentation and self-supervised methods add training data by masking, reordering, or otherwise perturbing observed interaction sequences , while generative methods synthesize interaction sequences that were never observed . Recursive self-improvement goes one step further: the recommender generates synthetic sequences, a new model is trained on the enlarged corpus, and the process repeats without additional labels or an external teacher. Training a model on its own data can distort what it learns . Keeping original data alongside synthetic data limits this effect , but a model trained on its own predictions can still reinforce its mistakes . Recursive Self-Improving Recommendation applies a fidelity check during generation . A synthetic sequence grows from a source sequence one interaction at a time, and generation continues only while an unused later item from the source remains near the top of the model's predictions. In the first round, sources contain observed training-prefix interactions; at later rounds, they may also be synthetic sequences acquired earlier. This fidelity check therefore evaluates consistency with the current source sequence, not the downstream value of a candidate for successor model training. RSIR nevertheless retains every verified sequence, so source sequences yielding more candidates contribute more training examples, while longer continuations contribute more next-item prediction targets. Retaining every verified sequence is consequently an acquisition policy rather than a neutral consequence of verification. In our first-round experiments, RSIR yields higher mean Recall@10 than the same model trained only on real data in all twelve model--dataset settings, but lower mean NDCG@10 in four. This motivates treating candidate selection after verification as an explicit design choice. We introduce , a post-verification acquisition framework that leaves RSIR's generation and verification unchanged. DA-RSIR limits each source sequence's contribution and ranks its verified sequences using disagreement estimated with MC dropout . Multiple forward passes with dropout measure how much the model's predictions disagree about the item at each generated position, corresponding to that item's contribution to the BALD score . DA-RSIR then samples representatives from the low-, middle-, and high-disagreement ranges. Acquisition requires no additional labels, external teacher, or separately trained quality model. We evaluate DA-RSIR on four datasets and three recommender models under retraining and fine-tuning. In the main comparison, retraining with DA-RSIR achieves a higher mean than retain-all RSIR in all $24$ model--dataset--metric comparisons. Sequential recommendation has progressed through recurrent and convolutional encoders, unidirectional self-attention , bidirectional masked-item modeling , self-supervised pretraining , contrastive views of the same history , frequency-enhanced hybrid attention , and generative sequential transduction . Because performance is often limited by sparse interaction histories , data-augmentation methods construct additional training examples through sequence transformations or learned generators . These methods determine how additional examples are generated; DA-RSIR instead operates after generation and verification, selecting which verified model-generated sequences become supervision for the successor model. Learning from model-produced supervision spans semi-supervised classification and iterative self-training in language models , but repeated use of model outputs can reinforce errors through confirmation bias or distort the learned distribution . Methods therefore filter generated supervision before training: FixMatch retains only high-confidence pseudo-labels , STaR uses rationales associated with correct answers , and Self-Instruct removes invalid and near-duplicate generations . Training protocol also matters: retaining real data alongside synthetic data can prevent collapse in the settings studied . RSIR applies fidelity verification to recommendation by stopping a continuation when no later item from its source sequence remains highly ranked , yet retains every sequence that passes verification and basic filtering. Verification therefore determines which sequences pass the source-conditioned fidelity check, not which verified sequences should train the next model. DA-RSIR leaves verification unchanged and makes this selection explicit through post-verification acquisition. Active learning selects informative examples from a pool for labeling, with BALD scoring them by mutual information between predictions and model parameters . Monte Carlo dropout provides a tractable approximation in neural networks . Batch acquisition must account for redundancy: selecting the highest-scoring examples independently can produce redundant batches that even underperform uniform acquisition . This motivates avoiding a top-score-only acquisition rule rather than treating high disagreement as universally preferable. A parallel literature selects or values fixed-corpus examples using influence functions , Shapley values , target-task gradient similarity , or pruning criteria designed to improve scaling with retained dataset size . DA-RSIR instead selects among model-generated sequences that specify the next interaction at each generated position and have passed source-conditioned fidelity verification. Posterior disagreement complements this verification by guiding selection among these verified synthetic sequences. During scoring, the current model is fixed, gradient computation is disabled, and dropout remains active. We perform $S$ stochastic forward passes, drawing a new dropout configuration for each pass and retaining it across all generated positions of the sequence so that each pass corresponds to a coherent approximate parameter draw. Let $p^{ }_{z,t} $ denote the probability assigned to item $j$ at position $t$ in pass $r$. Before softmax, we mask the padding item and items already present in $z_{<t}$. The mean predictive probability is $_{z,t} =S^{-1} _{r=1}^{S}p^{ }_{z,t} $. BALD measures the mutual information between predictions and model parameters at a given context . It is large when the stochastic predictions are individually confident but disagree on the next item, and small when they agree or are uncertain in similar ways. For each generated position, DA-RSIR uses $b_{z,t} $, the generated target's contribution to the MC BALD estimate. This target-specific term is not a mutual-information estimate on its own: it depends on both the probability assigned to $i_t$ and its variation across stochastic passes. Consequently, a sequence score formed from these terms need not produce the same candidate ordering as full BALD. We average these contributions across the generated positions to obtain the sequence-level disagreement score U_{} = {|z|-1} _{t=2}^{|z|} b_{z,t} . Averaging prevents the score from growing automatically with the number of generated positions, although some sequence-length dependence may remain. For deterministic tie-breaking, we first use the maximum target-specific disagreement across generated positions and then the mean posterior-predictive log-likelihood of the generated targets. Any remaining ties are resolved using the generation-attempt index and a deterministic representation of the complete sequence. All quantities are computed only after fidelity verification, so they cannot affect candidate generation or verification decisions. Generation attempts for source sequence $s$ are assigned by attempt index to $B$ disjoint proposal groups of $M$ attempts each, giving $BM$ attempts per source sequence. Grouping is fixed before disagreement scoring, so membership cannot depend on the realized scores, and each surviving candidate retains its group identity. This provides multiple proposal sets for the same source sequence while keeping comparisons within the same behavioral context. We treat $B$ as a design parameter selected using validation performance and include $B=1$ as the source-sequence-conditioned configuration without proposal grouping. This section provides additional analyses of two DA-RSIR design choices: the number of proposal groups $B$ and Monte Carlo dropout samples $S$. We examine how each choice affects performance and assess the sensitivity of DA-RSIR to these settings. Figure~ varies the number of proposal groups $B$ while holding the acquired count fixed at $12{,}897$ sequences from a shared proposal bank, isolating grouping from acquisition volume. Performance varies non-monotonically with $B$. Under retraining, $B=2$ attains the highest NDCG@10 and Recall@10. Under fine-tuning, $B=2$ attains the highest Recall@10, while $B=5$ yields a marginally higher NDCG@10. Every tested $B$ improves Recall@10 over Base, whereas retraining with $B=3$ and $B=4$ returns approximately to the Base NDCG@10 level.
