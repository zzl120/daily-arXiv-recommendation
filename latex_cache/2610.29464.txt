In this paper, we introduce Directional Posterior Feedback Fusion , a framework for feedback-based query reformulation in dense retrieval. Moving beyond conventional pointwise fusion, it models feedback as directional evidence in the tangent space of the original query, allowing each feedback item to constrain only the correction direction it supports while preserving complementary information from other feedback. Experiments across diverse retrieval settings demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of DIFF and validate the benefits of directional modeling and confidence-aware fusion. Future work includes extending the directional formulation beyond local tangent-space modeling to capture more complex geometry in embedding spaces, and investigating richer mechanisms for estimating feedback reliability or jointly exploiting heterogeneous feedback sources. Dense retrieval represents queries and documents as continuous semantic vectors and performs retrieval according to their similarity in a shared embedding space~. Early dual-encoder approaches such as DPR demonstrated the effectiveness of independently encoding queries and passages for efficient maximum-inner-product search~, while subsequent work improved dense retrievers through better negative sampling~ and large-scale pretraining~. More recent embedding models further improve generalization across heterogeneous retrieval tasks through large-scale weak supervision and instruction-aware representation learning~. Despite these advances, retrieval effectiveness still depends critically on the quality of the query representation: short or under-specified queries may not fully express the underlying information need. Our work is complementary to improvements in retriever training: we keep the pretrained dense retriever fixed and study how its query representation can be effectively refined using external feedback. Query reformulation aims to enrich or modify the original query using additional information and can broadly be viewed through two sources of feedback~. Pseudo-relevance feedback assumes that top-ranked documents from an initial retrieval contain relevant information and uses them to refine the query. Classical approaches operate in the lexical space through term reweighting or expansion~, while recent methods extend PRF to neural and dense retrieval by learning feedback-aware query encoders or directly manipulating query and feedback representations in the embedding space~. In parallel, generative relevance feedback leverages the parametric knowledge of LLMs to generate auxiliary information without relying exclusively on the initial retrieval results. Representative approaches generate hypothetical documents, pseudo-documents or answers, query expansions, or other forms of relevant content that are subsequently incorporated into retrieval, such as HyDE~, Query2Doc~, CoT~, Lamer~, and GPRF~. These two lines of work have substantially expanded , from retrieved documents to LLM-generated knowledge. However, once feedback is available, an equally important question is , especially for dense retrievers that operate in the semantic embedding space beyond the explicit vocabulary space. Existing approaches often simply aggregate or interpolate feedback representations~, implicitly treating them as target points for query movement. In contrast, our DIFF models feedback vectors as confidence-weighted directional constraints rather than complete target representations. It also serves as a general reformulation mechanism on top of different feedback sources without retraining the underlying dense retriever. Table~ presents the overall retrieval performance across all BEIR subsets under different dense retrievers and feedback sources. Overall, DIFF delivers the strongest and most consistent performance. This trend holds for both PRF and all three forms of GRF, as well as across different embedding models, suggesting that the proposed directional formulation is not tied to a particular retriever or feedback construction strategy. These results also highlight the importance of how feedback is utilized. Generating high-quality feedback with LLMs alone is insufficient, and how to incorporate such feedback is equally important. Specifically, simple vector averaging or concatenation does not consistently improve retrieval performance and can even degrade it, particularly when the feedback contains noisy information . Methods that more carefully control the contribution of feedback generally exhibit more robust behavior. DIFF further improves upon these approaches by treating feedback as directional evidence instead of complete target representations and by adapting its influence according to both individual feedback quality and set-level confidence. In Appendix~, we further report the per-query changes in NDCG@10 relative to the original retrieval results, indicating that DIFF behaves conservatively under noisy feedback and allows more beneficial updates when the feedback is more reliable. Its consistent behavior across PRF and GRF supports our motivation that a feedback item may provide useful evidence for a particular correction direction without constituting a complete replacement for the original query representation. Among the competing methods, AnchorQE is the closest to DIFF in overall performance, but requires additional retrieval operations to estimate feedback contributions. These operations provide additional retrieval signals beyond the given feedback, particularly in the GRF setting where feedback is generated independently of the document collection. Moreover, these additional retrieval operations introduce substantial computational overhead, as further demonstrated in the efficiency analysis in Appendix~. In contrast, DIFF requires no additional retrieval operations and achieves stronger overall performance. It suggests that effectively modeling feedback geometry and reliability can be more beneficial than incorporating additional retrieval signals for feedback weighting. Taken together, these results demonstrate that DIFF provides a robust feedback-utilization mechanism across heterogeneous retrieval conditions. Rather than relying on a specific feedback source or additional retrieval signals, it consistently extracts useful query corrections from the feedback already available. We first examine DIFF's central modeling choice: replacing directional posterior fusion with point-target fusion, where we directly aggregate the correction vectors as $ = _i _i _i u_i$. This variant leads to one of the largest performance drops under both feedback sources. Point-target fusion directly pulls the query toward feedback vectors and therefore implicitly suppresses components not expressed by each target. In contrast, DIFF imposes a more selective directional constraint, requiring the inferred correction to agree with each feedback item only along the direction it supports. The remaining degrees of freedom can consequently accommodate complementary evidence from other feedback items. The consistent advantage of DIFF over point-target fusion empirically supports this distinction between fitting feedback as a target and using feedback as directional evidence. The remaining ablations demonstrate the importance of controlling feedback reliability at both the individual and set levels. Assigning equal precision to all feedback causes more evident degradation under PRF than under HyDE, consistent with PRF containing noisier and more heterogeneous pseudo-relevant documents, whereas LLM-generated feedback is generally more consistent and focused on the input query. This suggests that confidence estimation becomes particularly valuable when feedback quality is uneven. Moreover, despite this degradation, the confidence-free variant still substantially outperforms simple point-target fusion methods such as VPRF and Concat, further demonstrating the advantage of directional fitting over directly aggregating feedback representations. Within the hierarchy, removing local quality prevents DIFF from distinguishing the relative reliability of individual feedback items, while replacing robust normalization with raw query-feedback similarity also reduces performance, supporting query-wise calibration of feedback quality. Set-level confidence provides complementary control over whether the feedback collectively warrants a substantial update. Removing either its geometric consistency or average local-quality component leads to degradation, and removing the set-level gate altogether is consistently inferior to the full model. Overall, these results show that the hierarchical design allows DIFF to account for both which feedback items should be trusted and how strongly the feedback set as a whole should influence the query, complementing the directional formulation with robustness to noisy or inconsistent feedback. Given a normalized query embedding $ q R^D$ and $K$ normalized feedback embeddings $ F=\{ f_i\}_{i=1}^{K}$, our goal is to construct a reformulated query $ q'$ that incorporates useful information from the feedback while avoiding excessive deviation from the original query. As discussed above, we do not treat feedback embeddings as complete target representations. Instead, we convert them into directional observations in the tangent space $T_{ q}$ and jointly infer a query correction $ $ . Specifically, each feedback item is represented by a triplet $ $, where $ u_i T_{ q}$ denotes the direction supported by the feedback, $ _i$ specifies the target projection along this direction, and $ _i 0$ represents the confidence of this directional observation. Each feedback item therefore provides a noisy constraint $ u_i^ _i$. We jointly fuse these observations by the objective: ^*= _{ T_{ q}}{2}\| \|^2 +{2} _{i=1}^{K} _i ^2, where $ >0$ controls the strength of the original-query prior. The first term favors a conservative correction around the original query, whereas the second term fits the directional evidence according to its estimated reliability. Theorem~ in makes this precise: summing the above decomposition over all feedback items shows that pointwise fusion equals directional fusion plus the penalty $ 12 ^ P $ with $P= _i _i $, which penalizes every direction in proportion to the total confidence of the feedback items that do not support it and is therefore what suppresses complementary corrections. The directional representation above determines what correction each feedback item proposes, but not how much influence it should have on the reformulated query. Actually, feedback is inherently noisy and imperfect~: some feedback items may be less aligned with the original information need, while even individually plausible items may collectively form an ambiguous or inconsistent correction signal. We therefore consider feedback reliability from two complementary perspectives. At the local item-level, we assess the relative quality of each feedback item with respect to the original query, so that stronger feedback contributes more to posterior inference. At the set-level, we assess whether the feedback items collectively provide a sufficiently coherent and reliable signal to justify modifying the query. The two levels respectively control the relative contribution of individual observations and the overall strength of the feedback set. Accordingly, we factor the precision or confidence of the $i$-th directional observation as _i = g\,a_i, where $a_i$ denotes its local quality and $g$ denotes the confidence of the entire feedback set. Dense retrieval has become a fundamental paradigm for modern information retrieval~. It represents queries and documents as dense vectors and retrieves relevant documents according to their similarity in a shared semantic embedding space, thus bridging the gap between users' information needs and vast document collections~. However, its effectiveness depends critically on whether the query embedding adequately captures the user’s underlying information need~. User queries can be short, ambiguous, or underspecified, and only partially describe the relevant information~. Query reformulation therefore offers a natural way to bridge this gap by enriching the initial query representation with additional evidence. A common strategy is to obtain such evidence from feedback. Classical pseudo-relevance feedback ~ assumes that top-ranked documents from an initial retrieval contain useful information and uses them to refine the query representation. More recently, large language models ~ have further broadened the possible sources of feedback by generating hypothetical documents, pseudo-answers, query expansions, or other auxiliary content~. Despite their different origins, these approaches share a common question: In this work, we separate this question from how the feedback is obtained and focus on converting feedback vectors into a controlled and robust query update. Existing vector-based feedback methods commonly construct the reformulated query by averaging feedback embeddings, linearly interpolating them with the original query, or otherwise moving the query toward an aggregate feedback representation~. Although intuitive, such pointwise fusion implicitly treats each feedback vector as evidence for a complete target representation . This assumption can be unnecessarily restrictive. A feedback item may support only one aspect of the information need rather than specify what the entire reformulated query should be. Consequently, dimensions or directions not expressed by that feedback should not automatically be interpreted as evidence against changing the query along those directions. To address this limitation, we shift from pointwise fitting to directional constraints. Instead of asking the reformulated query to approach each feedback vector as a whole, we let each feedback constrain only the query movement along the direction it supports, as shown in the right part of Figure~. Specifically, we operate in the tangent space of the unit sphere at the original query representation, where feedback can be expressed as local directional evidence and multiple pieces of evidence can be jointly incorporated through a constrained optimization objective. This formulation allows complementary feedback to govern different directions of the query update, while a prior toward the original query keeps the reformulation conservative when feedback evidence is insufficient. {fig/fig.pdf} Based on this formulation, we propose rectional Posterior eedback usion , a query reformulation framework for dense retrieval. DIFF first converts feedback vectors into directional evidence in the tangent space of the original query, and then estimates their contributions using lightweight local- and set-level confidence control. The resulting directional observations are jointly fused through a prior-regularized posterior objective with an efficient closed-form solution, after which the inferred correction is mapped back to the unit sphere. DIFF operates directly on fixed pretrained embeddings without updating the retriever or training an additional reformulation model, making it applicable to feedback from different sources. Experiments across multiple retrieval benchmarks and dense retrievers show that DIFF achieves the best overall retrieval performance in diverse retrieval settings. In conclusion, our contributions are threefold: We introduce a directional perspective on vector feedback for dense query reformulation. Rather than treating feedback embeddings as complete target points, we formulate each feedback item as a local directional constraint, allowing complementary evidence to contribute along different directions of the query update. We propose DIFF, a posterior fusion framework that jointly incorporates directional evidence with local feedback quality and set-level confidence, while explicitly regularizing the update toward the original query. We develop a training-free and feedback-source-agnostic reformulation method that operates directly on pretrained embeddings and admits an efficient closed-form solution, enabling its application to both retrieval-based and generated feedback. Dense retrieval has become a fundamental paradigm for modern information retrieval, supporting efficient semantic search across a wide range of applications. Its effectiveness, however, relies critically on query embeddings that adequately capture users’ information needs, which can be difficult for short, ambiguous, or underspecified queries. Feedback-based query reformulation approaches like pseudo-relevance feedback alleviate this by incorporating additional evidence from retrieved or generated content. However, existing embedding-space approaches commonly aggregate or interpolate feedback vectors, implicitly treating them as complete target representations. Such pointwise fusion can unnecessarily suppress query corrections along directions not expressed by individual feedback items, even when these directions are supported by complementary evidence. To address this limitation, we propose Directional Posterior Feedback Fusion , a query reformulation framework that models feedback as directional evidence rather than target points. DIFF represents feedback as confidence-weighted directional evidence in the tangent space of the original query and jointly infers a controlled query update through prior-regularized posterior fusion, without retraining the underlying dense retriever. Experiments across multiple dense retrievers and different feedback sources show that DIFF achieves the best overall retrieval performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of explicitly modeling how feedback should constrain query updates.
