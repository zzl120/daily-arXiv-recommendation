Textual explanations are particularly appealing because they convey justifications in natural, human-understandable language. Existing approaches have evolved from predefined templates~ to neural generation~ and, more recently, LLM-based generation~. However, these generative methods may produce generic or unsupported rationales~. Evaluating their factual grounding with semantic similarity metrics~ or LLM-based judges~, as well as assessing the relevance of individual explanatory factors, remains challenging. Such concerns extend beyond recommendation, motivating evidence-grounded approaches in question answering~ and retrieval-based tool selection in LLM agents~. In explainable recommendation, a complementary paradigm is : rather than generating free-form explanations, approaches such as ~ and ~ rank concise, review-derived explanatory statements and return the top-$k$ for each user--item interaction. This formulation enables individual explanatory aspects to be prioritized and supports reproducible evaluation using established ranking metrics~. Yet ranking explanations does not inherently solve their personalization. Under evaluation, candidates are restricted to the target item's statements, making the challenge to identify which aspects best match a particular user. Prior work shows that simple user-history popularity signals remain competitive with sophisticated rankers in this setting~. We build upon the statement extraction and clustering pipeline introduced by ~, with two refinements aimed at improving the explanatory relevance and semantic consistency of the resulting statements. First, we revise the extraction prompt to explicitly identify statements that explain the match between a user's preferences and an item's characteristics, rather than merely describing item attributes. Second, we strengthen the final clustering stage with reranker-based verification to reduce erroneous merges between semantically distinct clusters. Specifically, we select $q$ representative statements from each cluster to capture its semantic diversity in the embedding space. For each pair of candidate clusters, we evaluate all cross-cluster pairs of representatives and merge the clusters only if the reranker identifies every pair as semantically equivalent. We use {Qwen3.8-27B}~} for statement extraction, {Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B}~} for semantic representations, and {Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B}~} for pairwise paraphrase verification. All prompts and hyperparameters are provided in the accompanying code. After statement extraction and clustering, we discard interactions without valid explanatory statements and apply iterative $5$-core filtering, retaining only users and items with at least five interactions. We review existing approaches to generating and ranking explanations and advances in dense representation learning. *{Text-based Explainable Recommendation} Text-based explainable recommendation has evolved from template-based methods~ to neural generation using RNNs~, Transformers~, and more recently pretrained language models and LLMs~. Despite advances in fluency, evaluation remains challenging: lexical metrics reward surface overlap~, semantic metrics do not ensure factual consistency~, and LLM-based judges can be biased and difficult to reproduce~. Moreover, paragraph-level evaluation obscures the relevance of individual explanatory factors. To address these limitations, prior work~ shifts from free-form generation to ranking short explanatory passages, called , selecting the top-$k$ candidates as explanations. This enables standardized evaluation using established ranking metrics~. Meaningful evaluation further requires statements to be explanatory, atomic, and semantically unique~. *{Statement Ranking Methods} Existing approaches range from statistical baselines to collaborative and semantically enhanced models. Popularity baselines~ rank statements by their frequency in user histories , item histories , or the training corpus . Collaborative approaches exploit user--item--statement interactions: and aggregate evidence from Jaccard-based neighborhoods, while and rely on ternary and pairwise latent factorization, respectively~. ~ combines separately learned user--statement and item--statement scores. To incorporate textual semantics, ~ combines latent factors with BERT~ embeddings, whereas and initialize latent factors from contextualized statement representations, with the latter introducing task-specific fine-tuning~. While these models rely on Bayesian Personalized Ranking objective~, ~ adopts a contrastive NT-BXent loss while retaining ID-based representations. Explanation ranking distinguishes ranking over all statements from ranking restricted to the target item's statements~. While the former resembles large-scale retrieval, the latter more directly assesses personalization. Results on the benchmark~ show that popularity baselines remain competitive globally, while matches or outperforms several state-of-the-art models at the item level, motivating our focus on this setting. *{Statement Ranking Datasets} Real-world recommendation datasets contain free-form reviews rather than structured explanatory statements. ~ constructs explanation-ranking datasets by extracting review sentences and grouping near-duplicates through lexical $n$-gram similarity and locality-sensitive hashing~. However, lexical matching may retain non-explanatory content and overlook semantic paraphrases. ~ addresses these limitations through LLM-based statement extraction and a three-stage semantic clustering pipeline combining approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, reranker-based filtering, and graph-based refinement. We build upon this pipeline with two refinements: an extraction prompt emphasizing the user--item match and stricter reranker-based verification of cluster merges. These changes aim to improve explanatory relevance and semantic consistency. *{Neural Retrieval Models} Neural retrieval represents queries and candidates in a shared embedding space, enabling efficient similarity-based ranking with precomputable candidate representations. Early dual-encoder approaches, including {DSSM}~, {Sentence-BERT}~, and {DPR}~, established this paradigm. Subsequent work improved dense retrieval through contrastive learning and hard negative mining~, large-scale weak supervision~, and instruction-conditioned representations~. More recently, {RepLLaMA}~, {LLM2Vec}~, and {Qwen3-Embedding}~ have extended dense retrieval to LLM-based representations. In recommendation, neural candidate retrieval has been widely adopted~, while {UniSRec}~ and {RecFormer}~ leverage textual item information and interaction histories to model user preferences. Recent LLM-based two-tower architectures further extend this paradigm to personalized item retrieval~.
