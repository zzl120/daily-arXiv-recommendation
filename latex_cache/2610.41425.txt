Despite their success, the mechanisms underlying their relevance predictions remain poorly understood, particularly how they process to match queries and documents. This lack of interpretability limits their optimization, can lead to suboptimal retrieval, especially out-of-domain, and raises efficiency concerns for over-parameterized models. To address these challenges, this paper investigates two under-explored aspects of cross-encoder behavior. First, to understand the relevance prediction process, we trace the flow of information at the different stages of the process and between different input segments. We study the nature of the signals that compose this flow and that allow cross-encoders to predict the relevance of a passage to a query. Our work particularly focuses on the matching performed by the cross-encoders between queries and documents. Second, we explore how cross-encoders behave with out-of-domain documents and/or queries. Through a series of interpretability experiments, we evaluate three BERT-based cross-encoder models, varying in size and pre-training strategies, across both in-domain and out-of-domain datasets. Overall, this study both consolidates what is known about how cross-encoders model relevance and extends it by exploring the role of matching signals. With the rise of transformer-based models , currently culminating in Large Language Models , information retrieval has changed drastically since the BM25 era . Current transformer-based IR models can be categorized according to how they model the interaction between queries and documents: implicitly , explicitly or a mix of both . With their ability to model semantics and produce contextualized representations, these models have overcome the query-document exact match limitation of traditional lexical methods. This advancement, however, has come with an important trade-off: interpretability. Unlike lexical approaches such as BM25 -- where relevance can be directly traced to term-statistics -- neural models are mostly black-boxes. For end users, this opacity may be acceptable, provided that the retrieved results or the LLM-generated outputs are relevant . For researchers and practitioners, however, this opacity hinders both systematic improvement and the diagnosis of failure cases. In particular, cross-encoders are widely used in the re-ranking stage of retrieval pipelines -- whether in search engines or RAG frameworks -- despite limited understanding of their internal mechanisms and failure modes. While we know that these models are over-parameterized and could be made more efficient by removing unnecessary layers or attention heads , our limited comprehension prevents us from designing more efficient and/or effective alternatives. This inability to diagnose failure cases has motivated a surge in and studies, not limited to IR. From attribution methods to probing , many techniques have been developed to interpret and explain the behavior of neural networks. In IR, efforts have largely concentrated on elucidating the nature of the signals involved when computing document relevance, though with mixed outcomes . Some works study the agreement of neural IR models with established IR axioms or use probing to search for specific encoded features , while other studies have highlighted the roles of specific attention heads in detecting matching signals . However, we still lack a understanding of how these signals are detected or processed, let alone incorporated to form the . In addition, few works have attempted to provide a holistic view of the entire relevance prediction process, as most of them generally focus on a specific aspect. This also raises the question of whether any single interpretability technique can bridge this gap. Notable exceptions in IR are studies of the processing stages within MonoBERT , but even these lack supporting evidence for the hypotheses they formulate. More recently, ~ identified a mechanism similar to a semantic variant of BM25 within a MiniLM-based cross-encoder, while ~ used mechanistic interpretability to interpret the roles of some attention heads for duplicate tokens inside TAS-B . These works provide an end-to-end picture of a specific mechanism inside neural IR models, yet they also highlight another overlooked aspect of interpretability studies: their generalization to other models and datasets. In response to these limitations, a parallel line of research has emerged, shifting focus from behavioral analysis to uncovering the internal mechanisms by which neural IR models arrive at relevance predictions. Originally motivated by BERTology , this line of work investigates the different processing stages within models such as MonoBERT . More recently, the rise of mechanistic interpretability , which aims to characterize the behavior of individual components and the structure of representational subspaces, has inspired a second wave of investigations. In particular, ~ identified a mechanism similar to a semantic variant of BM25 within cross-encoder architectures. In parallel, ~ used mechanistic interpretability to interpret the roles of some attention heads for duplicate tokens inside the TAS-B bi-encoder. Cross-encoders stand as the most effective models for relevance estimation, while their internal mechanisms remain poorly understood. This paradox makes them a high-potential target for interpretability research. Better understanding how they work could help overcome their two main limitations in search pipelines: computational inefficiency and dependence on a first-stage retriever. To address these limitations, our experimental plan combines 1) multiple interpretability techniques, since signals in transformer-based models are entangled ; 2) multiple BERT-based cross-encoders of different sizes and pre-training strategies; and 3) multiple datasets, both from the training domain and from domains unknown to the models. This last point is motivated by the release of the BEIR benchmark which highlighted disparities between the in-domain and out-of-domain performance of neural IR models. Although smaller for cross-encoders than for bi-encoders, this gap, together with their reliance on semantic knowledge inherited from pre-training, raises the question of whether cross-encoders behave consistently In-Domain and Out-of-Domain . Thanks to the points 1) and 3) of this experimental plan, i.e., multiple techniques applied across multiple datasets, we further gain the ability to draw stronger conclusions, as we can cross observations. Our objective is to consolidate and extend previous findings on the role of attention heads and the nature of signals processed by cross-encoders. Beyond unifying these findings, we seek to shed light on the full process through which these models arrive at a relevance prediction. Specifically, we address the following research questions: : What is the contribution of the different models' components in predicting relevance? : How does information flow within the model, particularly through its most important components? : Which signals flow through the model and contribute to predicting relevance or non-relevance? : How is the relevance signal constructed within the representation of cross-encoders? These main research questions structure our work. We study each one for different models and across different domains to answer a series of orthogonal research questions that strengthen our findings: : To what extent are these mechanisms consistent across different sizes of cross-encoders? : How robust are these findings to domain shifts? To identify the most crucial components involved in the relevance prediction , we follow our previous work in ~, leveraging the Neuron Integrated Gradients method . Some components are important domains, while others are important in OOD, particularly for large models. In particular, our method highlights the key role of a few attention heads and FFN neurons in the relevance prediction process. As a ``naive'' usage of NIG cannot provide enough details on the nature of the contributions from the critical components, we investigate the flow of information inside the model . In Section , we combine insights drawn from improving the granularity of the original NIG technique, extending the ablation analysis from ~, and using a variant of the Information Bottleneck Attribution . While NIG and ablations allow for the extraction of the most salient informational directions throughout the model, the Information Bottleneck yields a more holistic view of the information flow. Altogether, they map the pathways through which information flows between input segments. By answering and , we notice the key role of some high-attribution attention heads in query-to-document and document-to-query transfers, a behavior we link to the detection of matching signals across them. To derive the nature of these matching signals , we analyze in Section the attention patterns of every attention head by contrasting their attributions when exposed to relevant pairs versus non-relevant ones. Our objective here is twofold: firstly, to uncover the nature of the query-document interactions detected by high-attribution heads, and secondly, to discover heads specialized in detecting matching signals. Furthermore, we extend this experiment with a mechanistic interpretability study to better understand the mechanism at stake that allows these heads to match together query tokens and document tokens. Our results confirm that heads specialized in detecting matching signals between queries and documents exist in the model. By analyzing their attention patterns, we further characterize the nature of the matching signals they capture and report two distinct stages. From early to middle layers, these interactions match occurrences of the same token in the query and the document , while in the last layers, matching occurs between semantically-contextualized tokens that are close to each other. These heads are also among the most important components of cross-encoders, which confirms the significance of matching signals, and our mechanistic study explains the behavior of most of them: their Query-Key circuit favors cross-attention over self-attention, and associates highly similar source and target tokens. After discovering the nature of the signals that are leveraged by cross-encoders to predict relevance, we look into the process behind the construction of the ``relevance'' signal itself . We target key stages of the process, i.e., when the information stored inside the query tokens is transferred into the representation, and investigate the geometry of the hidden state's subspaces to understand if a ``relevance'' subspace exists. Our analysis reveals differences between MiniLM, MonoBERT-base and MonoBERT-large, in both the construction and the nature of their respective ``relevance'' subspace. In addition, we report that relevance signals emerge in the token only in the final layers, which supports the view that it performs a ``no-op'' function in earlier layers, akin to the tokens . Taken together, our findings both reinforce and refine prior insights into cross-encoder behavior. In particular, we are able, not only to confirm, but also to specify, the flow of information between input parts inside BERT-based cross-encoders. Crucially, we go beyond the existing work by testing the consistency of this finding across multiple architectures and domains . In doing so, we help move the field toward a more unified and generalizable understanding of how cross-encoders compute relevance. To interpret neural models, researchers rely on a large variety of interpretability techniques that can be categorized into distinct families. In IR, some architectures such as SPLADE or ColBERT are ``more explainable'' than cross-encoders which must rely on the interpretability approaches we describe next. One of the most widespread ways to access the internal knowledge of neural networks is through probing . Probing studies rely on training external models from hidden states on auxiliary but related tasks , revealing the specific abilities that the model learned implicitly during its training to solve the task. Probing is popular because it is easy to use: given a dataset for the auxiliary task, one can test whether a model's activations encode the corresponding information. In IR, probing studies often rely on ``diagnostic datasets'' related to specific IR axioms . However, because probing relies on external classifiers , its results are only correlational and can be hard to interpret, especially with overly expressive nonlinear probes. In addition, probing methods cannot be used to target specific neurons as they use each hidden representation as a whole and do not provide any explanation of the interplay between these abilities, as well as their relative importance in the model output. Other simple introspection techniques include the study of the attention process in transformer-based architectures. Similarly to probing, these techniques have been criticized for providing explanations uncorrelated to the predictions of the model . This claim has been challenged since , and researchers now address this issue by combining attention patterns analysis with other interpretability methods to strengthen their conclusions~. A way to test the importance of specific components is through ablation studies. By comparing the performance of the model with and without a specific part, one can directly assess its importance. Ablations belong to the extended family of attribution approaches , as they compare the outputs of the original model with those of the ablated model. Similarly, mechanistic interpretability approaches'' }, such as activation patching and path patching , introduce perturbations inside the computational graph of the model to deduce the importance of specific components. While they led to significant breakthroughs , including in IR , mechanistic interpretability approaches and perturbation-based ones are hard to scale in an exhaustive study like ours. Even for more straightforward approaches, such as ablations, perturbations need to be conducted in isolation and put into perspective with the perturbations of the surrounding components. On larger models than BERT, this can make them intractable. More tractable attribution methods are the ones. They usually have more desirable theoretical properties than perturbation-based methods, such as sensitivity or additivity . Their main drawback is the difficulty of giving a human-understandable meaning to their attribution. Among such techniques, the Integrated Gradients and Neuron Integrated Gradients respectively allow tracing back the contributions of input parts and individual neurons to the predictions of the model. However, these approaches fail to provide insights into the nature of these contributions. Notably, attribution patching alleviates this limitation for mechanistic interpretability approaches by making use of gradient-based approximation , but it can lead to the appearance of false negatives , potentially harming its conclusions. While attribution methods help to understand the relative importance of input parts or components of the model , they fall short of providing a holistic view of information flow without being applied repeatedly across the architecture. In contrast, the Information Bottleneck allows for automatically determining the most important parts in the input. This approach estimates the minimal information flow between an input $X$ and an output $Y$, necessary to preserve $Y$, limiting the components to their most important features. As it acts on all the components at once, it further accounts for their relations, alleviating one of the main limitations of attribution methods -- but at the cost of some potential instability during training. Following our previous work involving NIG to study the most important components inside MonoBERT in both in-domain and out-of-domain settings, we repeat each experiment in both setups and cross the results to differentiate the in- and out-of-domain behavior. Extending this study, we further develop a method based on NIG, which differentiates the different parts inside the input to study the most important directions of the attention inside the model as well as the most important components in the FFN. So far, we reviewed methods designed to models' behaviors, i.e., decipher their inner mechanisms. By design, interpretability is tied to the studied model. On the other hand, refers to a different line of work, whose methods produce post-hoc explanations, are generally model-agnostic, and specifically target the role of the input features . In IR, notable works include applications of SHAP or LIME to explain pointwise predictions and even listwise rankings . We consider this line of work complementary to ours, as its focus is mostly on providing post-hoc explanations of a model's behavior, rather than on its . Numerous works investigate the behaviors and tendencies of BERT , a field of research called BERTology . In particular, studies have shown that earlier layers tend to encode syntactic knowledge, such as parts of speech, syntactic structures . BERT models have also been found to encode IDF information inside their parameters, in particular inside their embeddings , and some of their behaviors appear to depend on token frequency, with attention heads in early layers attending primarily to rare tokens . In addition to syntax and lexical properties, BERT's embeddings are also known to be highly contextualized , i.e., each token representation encodes information about its surroundings. In later layers, contextualization allows the model to capture the words' meaning . Combining these insights, it is possible to draw a high-level structure underlying the different steps of a forward pass in a transformer model. In the first layers, neurons detect surface signals and shallow patterns in the input. At the same time, the input is contextualized, and moving on to the middle layers, neurons specialize in higher-level concepts. Finally, in the last layers, neurons use the signals extracted earlier to take actions and promote features in the residual stream. In the context of BERT-based models specifically, this is encapsulated by the fact that pre-trained BERT models can encode knowledge related to different NLP tasks and organize them dynamically into a pipeline . These insights obviously overlap with IR interpretability studies, and ours in particular, as most observed behaviors and conclusions are transferable across all NLP tasks. Yet, some specificities of IR, like the modeling of relevance, are not properly captured by generic NLP studies. IR thus developed its interpretability field, summarized in the next section. Soon after BERT was successfully applied to IR , studies aimed at understanding how neural IR models work and how they compare to traditional IR approaches emerged. In particular, ~ and ~ both study the attention process of MonoBERT. state that MonoBERT predicts relevance by first contextualizing queries and documents, then capturing interaction signals, and finally composing them. With the Information Bottleneck attribution method , find that MonoBERT not only relies on lexical matching but also captures semantic relationships between the contextualized representations of the query and the document tokens. They also provide further details on the role of , which starts to gather relevance information in the very last layers of the model. On this matter, ~ go even further and show that across most of the model's layers, , , and punctuations attract a large proportion of attention but carry little relevance information, echoing a similar behavior to the ``no-op'' operation . Combined with the finding that the IDF is encoded in a low-rank component of the model's embedding matrix , further show that the model output can be reconstructed using these signals. Aside from BERT-based ranking models, some works have also started to explore the mechanisms of LLMs for ranking. If we further extend the scope to different architectures, other works have also explored the behavior of learned sparse retrieval models , late-interaction models , and bi-encoders , in particular the matching behaviors of these models. For example, ~ discover the key roles of some attention heads in detecting duplicate instances of the same tokens while studying TAS-B , a dual embedding model, using activation patching . Over the years, a series of works have improved the quality of the attributions from Neuron Integrated Gradients. Notably, Integrated Decision Gradients samples mostly in the region where the model decision changes . We use their algorithm to sample the steps and obtain better attributions. Usually, ``neurons'' correspond to the activation of any layer/block in the model. Previous works used an even more restricted scope and used ``neurons'' to refer to outputs of linear transformations and demonstrated their importance in various mechanisms . While ~ followed the same definition and applied NIG to the outputs of the linear transformations in MonoBERT, we argue that not all of them are equally interesting and can provide meaningful interpretations. As pointed out in , activations in transformer-based models do not necessarily possess a , which makes them hard to interpret. ~ list ``tokens, attention patterns and FFN activations'' as ``the only vectors with privileged bases'' based on their mathematical definitions, but later works discovered that some features in the residual stream are also . Motivated by these works, we further restrict the scope of vectors and study the attention patterns and the FFN neurons . As IR differs from other domains in which NIG has been applied, it requires some adaptations. To answer and draw fair comparisons between attributions obtained for different datasets, we aggregate the contribution of each activation over tokens. Since the same neuron is applied to every token of the query-document pair, we sum its conductance over tokens: a ``neuron'' in our experiments thus includes its corresponding outputs over all tokens. For the FFN neurons, this aggregated conductance can be thought of as the importance of the neuron within the Transformer. For the attention heads, we obtain a score characterizing their importance within the model. As pointed out by ~, gradient-based methods produce results that are input-dependent. Among the attention heads we have found to play a crucial role in the process of modeling and predicting relevance in cross-encoder models, we can guess what the role of most of them is. Indeed, as highlighted by prior studies as well as our findings in the previous Section , attention heads in cross-encoder models play a key role in 1) contextualizing the query and/or document tokens , 2) transferring information between the query tokens and document tokens and 3) transferring information from the entire input to the . Roles 1) and 3) are as important as 2), but they are better understood. Role 2), usually seen as the of query-document matches, remains opaque, both in the information it surfaces and in its mechanism. In our work, we focused on three cross-encoders, MiniLM and MonoBERT , in both its base and large versions, that differ mostly by their pre-training regime and their size . To answer : the stages of the relevance prediction process are similar across the studied models. This has been confirmed by our work around and , in Sections and , where we observed very similar flows of information across the input parts as well as attention heads specialized in detecting matching signals. Where the models differ, is principally in the details of these different steps. For example, we identified one major difference in the stage of gathering relevance signals and constructing a relevance subspace in Section . MonoBERT-large gathers raw relevance signals from the document into the query, then into the , where it refines them and builds a relevance subspace. From NIG, we obtain a relatively high-level picture of the information flow throughout the model. In general, interpretability studies constitute solid foundations to improve neural models. By revealing key mechanisms or how specific computations are implemented, it becomes possible to leverage architectural improvements and deduce the importance of design choices. For instance in this work, we confirmed the prevalence of the following three steps in the three cross-encoders we studied, to predict the relevance of a document to a query: Query and document independent contextualization across the first layers ; Query and document interactions, especially through the attention ; Transmission of the relevance signals to the token for the final prediction, from the query . In a recent work , we directly leverage this insight to design an optimized cross-encoder where only the most important directions, i.e., that preserve the previous steps, are kept. Thanks to the present interpretability study, we have been able to correctly identify and remove superfluous interactions from cross-encoders. This led to the improvement of the efficiency/effectiveness trade-off, with much lower latencies and also better OOD performance. In addition, our work also raised some interesting questions, such as why MiniLM seems to have learned to search for ``non-relevance'' signals, instead of relevance ones, or how exactly attention heads learn to detect query-document matches. While we leave them unanswered at this stage, we believe they are interesting directions for future work. In this study, cross-encoders based on encoder-decoder or decoder-only architectures are not considered, which restricts the scope of our findings to encoder-only, and more specifically BERT-based, cross-encoders. Regarding the latter, and although comparisons would have been of interest, decoder-only models are not primarily designed for the IR task, as suggested by a recent study comparing encoder-only and decoder-only models of equal size across various NLP tasks . Regarding the former, while extending our experiments to encoder-decoder models such as RankT5 would have yielded valuable insights, it would have simultaneously introduced additional layers of complexity into our analysis. First, prompt design is non-trivial and has been shown to heavily influence cross-encoder predictions . Second, the cross-attention heads and the decoder side introduce an additional layer that requires explanation and remains largely under-explored. For these reasons, our efforts were focused on encoder-only cross-encoders. The extension of this work to more recent encoders, such as DeBERTa-v3 , ELECTRA , or ModernBERT , is left for future work. Given the scale of our experimental setup, the decision was made to restrict our scope to BERT-based cross-encoders. Building on our discussion of the impact of pre-training and layer depth , we believe the series of steps we identified is shared across encoders beyond BERT. Nevertheless, each model may implement a given step differently, and it therefore cannot be asserted that all encoder-only cross-encoders share a single, uniform behavior. A further limitation, already noted in the Design Implications section , concerns the accuracy of the Information Bottleneck results, particularly for the MonoBERT models. Because the method learns masks that depends on a carefully selected threshold to preserve performance while removing as many directions as possible, its results are hard to interpret in isolation. By combining IB with experiments based on NIG and ablations, and given that their results largely overlap, this limitation can be mitigated; however, further work is needed to design a more robust experimental plan specific to the Information Bottleneck. In this work, we focused on better explaining the most effective IR models, namely the cross-encoders. This lack of knowledge is a long-standing issue in the IR community that previous works have already attempted to tackle. After reviewing the current state of knowledge on interpreting cross-encoders, we identified that the main limitations are a restricted scope of study and of applications . In contrast, we comprehensively study the relevance prediction process inside three cross-encoders over multiple datasets, both in-domain and out-of-domain. The different steps of this process have been roughly hypothesized in the past . Thanks to multiple experiments and the correlation of their results, we have been able to identify their nature and offer insights into their functioning: Initially, tokens lack contextualization, so the first mission of cross-encoders, similarly to transformer-based models, is to contextualize them to gain a semantic understanding of the query-passage pair. At this stage, self-contextualization plays a major role, but we further showed that cross-contextualization also occurs at this stage . By studying the attention process more in-depth , we showed that through early to middle layers, some attention heads are specialized in detecting matches between the query and the document. Moving forward in the relevance prediction process and the model's layers, we discovered other attention heads specialized in detecting query-document matches, but this time . This happens later in cross-encoders, as the input tokens need to be contextualized first. For both types of matching, we proposed an explanation for their detection . Once these matching signals have been detected, cross-encoders refine and gather them inside a . This step is decomposed into two different sub-steps, as cross-encoders first gather relevance signals in the query tokens, before transferring them to the . Importantly, we noticed that while MonoBERT models base their predictions on evidence that the passage is ``relevant'', MiniLM may instead be looking for the opposite, i.e., evidence that the passage is ``non-relevant''. At this stage, we noticed distinct strategies across the models, probably due to the different pre-training strategies. While MonoBERT models construct a relevance subspace inside the token and need multiple layers to accumulate the ``relevance'' signals and refine them, MiniLM constructs multiple relevance subspaces at the level of the query and document tokens . It suggests that MiniLM refines the ``relevance'' signals it has detected before sending them to the , while MonoBERT gathers inside it ``raw'' signals. Note that unlike its larger counterpart, MonoBERT-base also tends to construct relevance subspaces at the level of the query and document tokens, in a similar fashion to MiniLM. Since both models have the same depth, we hypothesize that the number of layers is a bottleneck for building a relevance subspace inside the alone, which smaller models avoid by spreading the task across tokens. Finally, the ``relevance'' signals are refined/aggregated inside the before the final relevance prediction. By comparing three BERT-based cross-encoders, of different sizes and trained following different strategies, we reinforced the hypothesis that this multi-stage process is shared by most BERT-based cross-encoders . In addition, we also showed that this process is consistent across domains, which might explain their better generalization ability compared to other neural IR models. Our work, however, underlined how difficult it is for these models to deal with hard negatives, especially in OOD. It suggests that cross-encoders may over-rely on matches when they fail to capture the semantics of a new domain. Following this in-depth comparison of MiniLM and MonoBERT, we can assert that they have comparable performance, despite major differences in their behaviors. Some of our findings further indicate that MonoBERT might be over-parameterized . Given its slightly better performance and the greater robustness of its mechanism across domains, MiniLM-L12-v2 is a more reasonable choice than MonoBERT-base or large, for both the efficiency-effectiveness trade-off and generalization. Overall, our work provides a robust foundation for future interpretability studies in IR. We demonstrated the importance of including various datasets and considering more than a single model to put results into perspective and strengthen conclusions. Some of the insights we unveiled, such as the fact that MiniLM may rely on ``non-relevance'' signals and how some attention heads can detect query-document matches, also constitute interesting directions for future work.
