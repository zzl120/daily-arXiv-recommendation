Their ability to process and generate natural language has made them natural candidates for integration into recommender systems~ ~, traditionally built on collaborative filtering or content-based methods that model user--item interactions through learned embeddings~. LLM-based RS leverage this linguistic competence to represent user preferences and items directly in natural language, enabling rich, flexible descriptions of preferences and products within a single prompting interface~. Among the paradigms explored for LLM-based RS, list-wise ranking, where the model receives a set of candidate items and directly outputs a ranked ordering or a single selected item, has attracted particular attention for its simplicity and its natural fit with instruction-following LLMs~. This paradigm, however, introduces a structural vulnerability: by presenting all candidates simultaneously as plain text, it exposes the model to systematic sensitivities to surface-level properties of item descriptions. Recommender systems differ in how many items they surface and how they interact with the user. In conversational recommender systems, which elicit preferences through multi-turn dialogue, LLMs now play three distinct roles: as the recommendation engine itself, as supporting components that augment specific parts of the pipeline , and as tools for user simulation and evaluation~. Top-1 and single turn LLM-based RS, the focus of this work, use the LLM in the first of these roles but in a single turn: they receive a set of $k$ candidate items retrieved from a large catalog and select a single recommended product in response to a natural language query, a paradigm that directly mirrors high-stakes real-world deployments such as e-commerce product search, voice-driven shopping assistants, and conversational product discovery interfaces~. In these settings, the model commits to a single recommendation with no opportunity for iterative preference refinement, making the quality of that single decision entirely dependent on how the model processes the candidate descriptions presented in its input prompt. This also makes the setting particularly consequential: unlike multi-turn systems where an erroneous first recommendation can be corrected through dialogue~, a systematic flaw in how the model reads those descriptions significantly degrades the user experience. Understanding what drives the recommender's choice among candidates and whether its decisions reflect only query-product relevance or are also shaped by other properties of the descriptions is therefore a question of direct practical importance. This question has a concrete operational counterpart. Organizations deploying LLM-based shopping assistants routinely replace the underlying model, update the prompt or ingest new catalog content, and each of these changes may silently alter which products are recommended. Auditing such changes with standard offline evaluation requires relevance judgments for every query--product pair, which are expensive to collect, rarely available for live catalogs, and quickly outdated. In marketplaces where product descriptions are written by third-party sellers, an undetected sensitivity to how descriptions are written also distorts competition between sellers and creates incentives to optimize listings for the recommender rather than for customers. Platform operators therefore need audit procedures that work with black-box access to the deployed system, on its own catalog and queries, and without annotation effort, a need that is reinforced by emerging regulation requiring transparency and risk assessment of recommender systems on large online platforms~. Prior work has documented that LLMs acting as evaluators or rankers exhibit a range of : systematic tendencies to favor candidates based on where they appear in the prompt, how they are worded, or how long their descriptions are, independently of their actual quality~. In the recommendation setting, where the model selects among products described in natural language, these biases are particularly consequential: a model that systematically favors longer or more elaborate descriptions may consistently recommend products that are not the most relevant, simply because their descriptions happen to be more verbose. Despite growing evidence of such effects in LLM-as-judge settings, their presence and magnitude in LLM-based RS remain largely uncharacterized. In particular, , the tendency to assign higher scores to longer or more elaborate inputs independently of their relevance, has received considerably less rigorous treatment than positional bias, and no statistically grounded methodology exists to detect it in a recommendation context without access to ground-truth relevance labels. This motivates the following research questions: To address these questions, we propose a statistically grounded, label-free methodology for detecting and characterizing structural biases in LLM-based RS, and instantiate it on verbosity bias as a concrete use case. The methodology uses paraphrasing models as a controlled rewriting mechanism: by applying semantics-preserving paraphrasers to candidate descriptions and randomizing both their assignment and their prompt position, we construct a setting in which any systematic deviation from uniform paraphraser selection, after controlling for semantic deviations, constitutes evidence of structural bias. A four-step statistical pipeline then detects the presence of bias, quantifies the independent contribution of each surface-level feature after controlling for relevance, determines the direction of each feature's effect, and characterizes how surface-level and relevance signals interact when they conflict. Our main contributions are the following: We show that, when paraphrasers are assigned to products at random, a recommender that is invariant to paraphrasing must select every paraphraser with equal probability. This randomization guarantee yields a valid test for structural bias that requires no relevance annotations.} We propose a label-free evaluation framework for structural bias in LLM-based RS that decomposes selection behavior into interpretable surface-level features. New feature extractors can be integrated with a single configuration change.} We detect and quantify verbosity bias across 9 recommender LLMs, showing that description length has a positive effect on product selection in all of them after controlling for the measured query-product relevance signals, with a magnitude that varies across model families.} We package the methodology as a step-by-step audit for deployed LLM-based recommenders that requires only black-box access, reuses paraphrases across audits, and comes with sample-size guidance, so that practitioners can monitor structural biases whenever the model, prompt or catalog changes.} We apply our methodology to a dataset of $20{,}000$ sampled user queries over a subset of the Amazon ML Challenge 2023 catalog, evaluating nine recommender LLMs spanning five model families. Seven of the nine models select paraphrased descriptions non-uniformly, showing that semantically equivalent rewritings of the same products are not treated as interchangeable. In all nine models, description length has a positive effect on selection after controlling for the measured relevance and semantic similarity signals. Relevance remains the primary driver of the recommenders' decisions: when verbosity and relevance point in opposite directions, the more relevant description is still preferred, but the preference gap is on average less than half that observed when both signals agree, indicating that verbosity acts as a secondary signal that partially counteracts relevance. The strength of this effect varies across model families: the GPT-OSS models are the least sensitive to description length, whereas the DeepSeek-R1 distillations are the most sensitive. Recommender systems are designed to surface relevant items from a large catalog by modeling user preferences, typically through collaborative filtering, content-based methods, or hybrid approaches~. At inference time, RS rank candidates using one of three paradigms: pointwise approaches score each item independently, pairwise approaches compare items in isolation, and listwise approaches rank the full candidate set jointly. Recent work has shifted toward LLM-based RS~, which leverage LLMs as the recommendation core. Unlike traditional methods, LLM-based RS can process rich natural language descriptions of both users and items, enabling zero-shot generalization and flexible integration into conversational pipelines~, and they are increasingly combined with retrieval augmentation and collaborative signals~. In this setting, LLMs most commonly operate in a listwise fashion, receiving the full candidate list as a prompt and producing a ranked output directly, a paradigm that, as we discuss below, is particularly exposed to structural biases arising from how items are presented in the input. Biases in LLM-based RS can be broadly grouped into two categories. Fairness and social biases arise when model outputs systematically disadvantage users or items along protected attributes such as gender, age, or ethnicity~. In LLM-based RS, such biases can also enter through LLM-generated text: Zhang et al.~ show that user and item profiles generated by LLMs to support recommendation inherit the model's propensity biases, which they mitigate through counterfactual inference. Structural biases, by contrast, stem from how LLMs process input at inference time, including sensitivity to presentation order, text length, or surface wording, independently of demographic factors~. We acknowledge that these categories are not perfectly separable: positional bias, for instance, may partly reflect human tendencies learned during pretraining~. This paper focuses exclusively on structural biases, which remain undercharacterized in LLM-based RS despite their direct implications for recommendation quality. A growing body of work has documented that LLMs exhibit systematic behavioral biases when used as evaluators or rankers, independent of the semantic content of the inputs. Recent surveys and bias-quantification frameworks identify a wide range of such limitations, including positional, verbosity, authority, sentiment, and distraction biases, among others~. These biases can be understood as sharing a common mechanism: LLMs appear to use surface-level textual features as proxies for quality, a shortcut that is particularly consequential when the model acts as a recommender selecting among retrieved products described in natural language. In this paper we mainly focus on two of these biases, namely positional and verbosity biases. Positional bias refers to the tendency of LLMs to favour candidates based on where they appear in the input prompt rather than on their intrinsic relevance. Ground-truth-dependent studies have documented it in question answering, where Liu et al.~ revealed primacy and recency effects on datasets with known answers, and in recommendation, where Hou et al.~ showed that LLM-based rankers evaluated on standard benchmarks are sensitive to candidate order. Label-free approaches avoid relevance annotations by requiring the output to be invariant to input order, either by aggregating outputs over shuffled inputs into an order-independent consensus, as in permutation self-consistency~, or by enforcing invariance inside the model, as Li et al.~ do by averaging attention weights across permutations. Both treat any change in the output as an error, which presumes a single correct answer. Recommendation lacks such a ground truth: Peláez-González et al.~ model it as a choice within a set of equally relevant items, so switching between them is not by itself evidence of bias, and propose statistically grounded methodologies to detect positional bias in this setting. Positional bias in LLM-based RS thus already has a dedicated detection framework, but other structural biases do not, and detecting them requires more than measuring output sensitivity to input perturbations, since recommendations lack a single ground-truth answer against which such changes could be judged. Verbosity bias refers to the tendency of LLMs to assign higher scores to longer or more elaborate inputs independently of their actual quality. On the ground-truth side, Dubois et al.~ showed that the AlpacaEval benchmark systematically favours longer outputs and proposed a length-controlled variant anchored to human preference labels, using regression to estimate the counterfactual win rate had both responses been the same length. Label-free probes for verbosity bias rely on controlled inflation of inputs rather than human annotations. Zheng et al.~ proposed a , which inflates answers by prepending a rephrased version of the original list without adding new information, and observing whether an LLM judge assigns a higher score to the inflated version. Ye et al.~ likewise include verbosity bias in their consistency-based taxonomy, confirming that length-inflated answers systematically receive higher scores independently of content quality. Closest to our setting, Tang et al.~ introduce BiasRecBench, which evaluates LLMs that select one option from a small candidate list in paper review, e-commerce and recruitment, and includes verbosity among the injected biases. Their evaluation relies on constructed ground truth: candidate lists are synthesized with a calibrated quality gap between an optimal option and sub-optimal ones, and bias is measured as the loss of accuracy when a sub-optimal option is rewritten to be more verbose. Despite this evidence, verbosity bias has received considerably less attention than positional bias in the context of LLM-based RS, where item descriptions naturally vary in length, and to the best of our knowledge it has only been evaluated there against constructed ground-truth labels. Despite the growing evidence that both positional and verbosity biases affect LLM outputs, their rigorous characterization in LLM-based RS remains underexplored. A central difficulty is that variability in the recommendation is not by itself evidence of bias: it may reflect legitimate differences in relevance, and equally relevant items may be selected interchangeably without any bias being involved. This distinguishes recommendation from question answering, where a single answer is correct and invariance can be required for each individual input~. Existing methodologies resolve this ambiguity through external references: LLM-as-judge settings assess free-form responses against a reference answer, and evaluation frameworks for LLM-based RS such as CFaiRLLM~ compare recommendations against ground-truth user preferences. Such references are not necessarily available in recommendation settings, where items are distinguished only by their natural language descriptions. While positional bias detection has already received a statistically grounded treatment in this setting~, no equivalent methodology exists for verbosity bias. Concretely, the remaining challenge is to detect and characterize verbosity-related selection patterns in existing recommendation datasets without ground-truth relevance labels, and to assess how these patterns vary across recommender models after accounting for measured relevance signals. This paper addresses these open questions by proposing statistically grounded, label-free methodologies for detecting and quantifying verbosity bias in LLM-based RS. The framework therefore accommodates any number of paraphrasers and candidates. This section introduces a four-step methodology for detecting and characterizing structural biases in LLM-based RS. The first step tests for the presence of bias by assessing whether the empirical distribution of paraphraser selections deviates significantly from uniformity. The second step quantifies the contribution of each surface-level and semantic feature to the recommender's decision rule, measuring how much each feature contributes to the fit of a choice model. The third step determines the direction of each feature's effect, establishing whether higher values of a given feature increase or decrease the probability of selection. The fourth step analyzes the interaction between the semantic feature and the surface-level feature under study, decomposing selection probabilities by their joint conditions to reveal how the two signals combine or conflict in driving the recommender's decisions. Having established in Proposition~ that an unbiased recommender must select paraphrasers uniformly, we now test whether observed selections actually follow this distribution. A significant departure is our first signal that something beyond semantics is driving choices. This paper introduced a label-free, statistically grounded methodology for detecting structural biases in LLM-based RS and applied it to verbosity bias, i.e., the tendency to prefer items with longer descriptions, across nine recommender LLMs from five model families. The methodology rests on a simple guarantee: if the recommender is insensitive to surface-level features and the paraphrasers preserve meaning, the selected paraphraser must be uniformly distributed . Any departure from uniformity is therefore a measurable signal, and it can be obtained without any relevance annotation. Our three research questions concerning verbosity bias in LLM-based RS receive clear answers. asked whether LLM-based RS exhibit verbosity bias in product selection: seven of the nine recommenders select paraphrased descriptions non-uniformly, showing that semantically equivalent rewritings of the same products are not treated as interchangeable, and the subsequent steps of the methodology indicate that description length is one of the factors behind this non-uniformity. asked whether verbosity independently influences product selection beyond query--product relevance signals. The answer is affirmative: the conditional logit analysis of Steps~2 and~3 shows that description length retains a non-negligible unique $R^2$ and a positive unique coefficient in all nine models after controlling for all relevance and semantic similarity signals, so longer descriptions are favored beyond what the measured relevance signals explain. asked how verbosity and relevance interact when they provide conflicting signals. The conditional analysis of conflicting signals of Step~4 shows that relevance remains the primary driver of the recommender's decisions: when length and relevance point in opposite directions, the more relevant description is still preferred. However, the preference gap in these conflicting cases is on average less than half that observed when both signals agree, indicating that verbosity acts as a secondary signal that partially counteracts relevance rather than as noise. Moreover, when length is the only differing signal, the longer description is preferred by all nine recommenders, although by a small margin. The overall picture is therefore reassuring and cautionary at the same time. The evaluated recommenders behave as recommenders should, in that relevance dominates their choices. Yet all of them also respond, to different degrees, to how much is written about a product. The magnitude of this effect is model-dependent. The GPT-OSS models are the least sensitive, with the smallest unique length effects in Steps~2--3 and among the smallest responses to isolated length differences in Step~4. The DeepSeek-R1 distillations are the most sensitive. This indicates that verbosity sensitivity is not an inevitable property of LLM-based recommendation but varies with model design and training. This is consistent with broader evidence that reliability properties of LLMs, including stability to semantically equivalent rephrasings of the prompt, do not improve uniformly with scale or instruction tuning and differ across model families~. Such properties must therefore be audited empirically for each deployed model rather than assumed. These findings have direct implications for the different stakeholders of LLM-based recommendation. Verbosity sensitivity varies by a factor of almost three across the evaluated models , so it should be part of model selection alongside accuracy and cost. Because the audit of Algorithm~ needs only black-box access, reusable paraphrases and a few thousand queries, it can be integrated into the validation pipeline and rerun whenever the model, the prompt template or the catalog changes, in the same way as regression tests in software deployment. When descriptions are written by sellers, a recommender that rewards longer descriptions creates an incentive to pad listings regardless of product quality, and disadvantages sellers who describe their products concisely. Detecting this sensitivity is a prerequisite for ensuring that recommendations reflect product fit rather than writing style, and the audit provides quantitative evidence for platform transparency and risk-assessment obligations~. The results point to inexpensive mitigations that can be evaluated with the same framework: normalizing or templating descriptions before they reach the recommender , preferring the less length-sensitive models when descriptions vary widely in length, and adapting post-hoc calibration techniques developed for length bias in LLM evaluation~. Because the audit outputs statistically testable measures, it can confirm whether a mitigation actually reduces the bias rather than merely changing the recommendations. Our empirical findings concern single-turn product recommendation with synthetic user queries, $k=5$ candidates and deterministic decoding, and Steps~2--4 estimate associations between description features and selection rather than causal effects. The methodology itself, however, is agnostic to the recommendation domain, the number of candidates and paraphrasers, and the features under study: it applies to any recommender that selects among textual candidates. This generality opens several directions for future work. The framework can be instantiated on other surface-level properties, such as lexical choices , sentiment, formatting, or stylistic markers of LLM-generated text. This would give a more complete map of the structural biases that shape LLM-based recommendation. The audit can be extended to recommenders that enrich the candidate list with LLM-generated descriptions, collaborative signals or reasoning LLMs, such as RALLRec+~. These designs change both the text the recommender reads and how it decides, so they may amplify or reduce structural biases. This question is especially relevant given that the reasoning-distilled DeepSeek-R1 models were the most length-sensitive recommenders in our study. Because the framework yields quantitative, statistically testable measures of bias, it can serve as an evaluation protocol for mitigation strategies, from description normalization to bias-aware prompting and post-hoc recalibration. A natural first candidate is the approach of , which estimates the length-dependent component of reward-model scores and removes it post hoc; adapting it to the selection scores of LLM-based recommenders and measuring its effect with our framework is a promising direction. Combining the label-free analysis with human relevance judgments or interaction data would quantify how often verbosity bias changes the recommendation to a less relevant item, and thereby measure its practical cost to users. Any additional information carried by a longer description should be reflected both in the recommender's choice and in the cross-encoder relevance score, so a preference for longer descriptions that remains after controlling for relevance is most plausibly explained by verbosity bias. This reasoning relies on the relevance signal itself being insensitive to length. Future work could build relevance signals that are agnostic to verbosity by construction, for instance by decomposing each description into atomic facts, removing redundant ones, and computing relevance from the resulting set of facts, so that restating or padding the same content leaves the score unchanged. The framework proposed in this paper allows new features of this kind to be analyzed automatically. Overall, this work shows that structural biases in LLM-based recommender systems can be detected and characterized rigorously without relevance labels. Verbosity is one bias that is now measurable, and the same framework is ready to be applied to others.
