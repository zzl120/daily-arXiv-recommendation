These two aspects can be evaluated either in isolation, using a measure for each aspect; or jointly with a single `relevance-aware' fairness measure, i.e., a measure that quantifies fairness with respect to relevance. Yet, neither approach provides a simultaneous evaluation of both relevance and fairness: the first approach may result in two different rankings of models, each corresponding to an evaluation aspect; the second approach often provides an imbalanced evaluation of the two aspects. To address this gap, we propose Distance to Pareto Frontier , a new approach to jointly evaluate RS fairness and relevance. DPFR is based on a Pareto frontier of relevance and fairness scores from existing measures. The frontier is generated from user-item interaction data and represents various Pareto-optimal trade-offs between the two aspects. Thus, the distance from a point in the frontier provides a joint score of fairness and relevance. As DPFR can be used with existing measures, it is modular and intuitive. Our experiments with 4 recommenders and 3 fairness-aware re-rankers on 6 real-world datasets reveal inconsistent associations between existing measures and DPFR, showing that they are not reliable proxies for evaluations based on Pareto optimality. We further examine the robustness of DPFR as a joint evaluation approach for fairness and relevance by conducting experiments with artificial recommendations and with various fairness-relevance trade-offs, as well as by analysing DPFR's agreement to simple measures of fairness and relevance. These analyses show that DPFR is more robust than existing joint evaluation approaches. Finally, we provide guidelines for evaluating fairness and relevance jointly with DPFR. These two evaluation concepts, RS relevance and fairness, are sometimes studied together , however, they are inherently different from one another. Relevance evaluation aims to capture the proximity of a recommended item to the user need . Fairness evaluation, on the other hand, aims to capture how impartial a recommendation is, in the sense that no items or users should be subjected to consistently favourable or unfavourable recommendations, compared to the other items or users in the RS. We focus solely on measures, which quantify the exposure of items in the recommendations. Exposure typically refers to the frequency of item appearance in the recommendation list across all users~. Individual item fairness is important in ensuring that each item in the system has a chance to be recommended to any user. The inherent difference between relevance and fairness makes their simultaneous evaluation challenging. One approach is to use a single evaluation measure that accounts for both fairness and relevance; however, such tend to be ad hoc, unstable, and oftentimes imbalanced in their quantification of relevance versus fairness . An alternative approach is to measure relevance and fairness separately, and then aggregate the scores into a single numerical score. There are at least three issues with this approach. Firstly, it is not trivial to combine scores that have different distributions and different scales, which is often the case with existing relevance and fairness evaluation measures. Secondly, it is not trivial to combine scores produced by measures that may not even be computed on the same input. This is often the case with relevance and fairness measures, because relevance scores are computed for individual users and then averaged, while fairness measures for individual items are typically based on individual item recommendation frequency. Finally, even if one merges relevance and fairness scores into a single numerical score, it is not clear how this single score should be interpreted, for instance how well this score reflects the distance from an ideal balance of relevance and fairness, or from an acceptable trade-off of relevance and fairness. We contribute a novel approach of evaluating relevance and fairness, which uses Pareto-optimality , a well-studied concept in multi-objective optimisation problems across domains, including RSs . A recommendation is said to be Pareto-optimal if there is no other possible recommendation with the same relevance score that achieves better fairness. Practically, this means that when a recommendation is Pareto-optimal, it is impossible to have other recommendations that empirically perform better in terms of fairness unless relevance is compromised. Note that the opposite is also true: a recommendation is Pareto-optimal if there is no other recommendation with the same fairness score and a better score. Our approach, called , combines existing measures and measures in the following way: first, we build a Pareto Frontier that maximises relevance; then we find the best fairness achievable under this relevance constraint; and finally, we jointly quantify fairness and relevance as the distance from an optimal solution. The evaluation process with DPFR is illustrated in . Our approach has several advantages. While it can be used with existing evaluation measures of relevance and fairness, it avoids the problems arising from directly combining their numerical scores. Instead, it computes the distance of these scores to a desired fairness-relevance balance, which is empirically optimal. Furthermore, the weight of fairness w.r.t.~relevance in this desired fairness-relevance balance is controlled by a parameter . The above aspects make our approach modular, tractable, and intuitive in its interpretation as a quantification of relevance and fairness. In this work, we ask the following research questions : How does our generated PF compare to the true PF ? How do existing single-aspect and joint evaluation measures of relevance and fairness relate to DPFR? How does the choice of distance measure affect DPFR? How does the choice of $ $, the fairness-relevance trade-off weight, affect DPFR? How does DPFR relate to simple measures of effectiveness and fairness that are not used for computing DPFR? How can DPFR be efficiently approximated without significant loss in effectiveness? We present thorough experiments with various RS models, re-ranking approaches, and datasets, using different evaluation measures of relevance and fairness. Our experiments reveal a noticeable gap between current joint evaluation approaches for relevance and fairness and our Pareto-optimal joint evaluation. This gap increases for larger datasets and when using rank-based relevance measures , as opposed to set-based relevance measures . This work extends our previous publication on this topic by contributing further analyses and discussions on the robustness of existing approaches for joint fairness-relevance evaluation, including DPFR. Specifically: We evaluate the closeness of our algorithm's generated PF to the true PF, which is the set of Pareto-optimal solutions obtained through exhaustive enumerations ; We conduct experiments with an artificial recommender to complement experiments with recommenders trained on real-world data ; We discuss and empirically investigate the use of various distance measures for computing DPFR ; We study the effect of varying the fairness-relevance trade-off weight ; and We analyse their correlation to simple measures of relevance and fairness . Our results show that DPFR can quantify fairness and relevance jointly in a more robust way, compared to existing joint evaluation approaches. Beyond robustness-related analyses, we also discuss theoretical considerations as to why existing multi-aspect evaluation approaches do not suit the specific evaluation problem that we tackle . Finally, we contribute a set of guidelines on when and how to use DPFR, summarising several consideration factors for selecting single- and multi-aspect evaluation approaches . In RSs, fairness is usually defined as an equal treatment of users and/or items, i.e., avoiding discrimination of users and/or items~. Fairness definitions are commonly categorised based on the subjects, e.g., items or users, and the partition granularity of the subjects, e.g., individuals or groups~. Item fairness is typically defined as the exposure disparity across items or types of items. The exposure of an item is estimated based on how frequently the item is recommended and at which rank positions. The more frequently an item is recommended and the closer the item is to the top of the ranking, the higher the amount of exposure associated with that item. Item fairness can also be defined by accounting for exposure and relevance simultaneously. The intuition is that an RS should not only provide equal exposure to items, but also recommend items that are relevant to the users. While item fairness is defined on exposure disparity, user fairness is usually defined on utility disparity, where utility is estimated with the effectiveness of the RS. In an ideally fair case, all users, or groups of users, should receive recommendations with the same level of effectiveness. Fairness granularity, i.e., individual vs.~groups, refers to how items or users are considered in the fairness definition. As the name suggests, individual fairness considers each item or user individually, and requires that equal treatment is provided to similar items or users~. Similarity can be computed based on features for items~ or the users' interaction history for users~. For example, given two items with similar features, an unfair RS will provide high exposure to only one of the two items. Analogously, given two users with similar past interactions, an unfair RS will provide effective/relevant recommendations to only one of the two users. Group fairness simplifies the problem by grouping items and users, so that each group of items or users will receive equal treatment~. Groups are usually formed with the attributes of items and users, e.g., popularity~, or sensitive attributes, such as gender and ethnicity~. In group fairness, an example of an unfair RS, is a system that provides more exposure to the group of highly popular items, or is more effective for a group of users, e.g., white male users. This paper focuses on individual item fairness. Next, we review measures in this category, focusing solely on item exposure. We consider all measures that could be corrected for the non-realisability limitation according to~. The non-realisability limitation occurs when a measure cannot empirically reach its theoretical minimum/maximum score. Correcting a measure for non-realisability ensures that there exists a set of top-$k$ recommendation lists such that the measure can empirically reach its theoretical minimum and maximum scores, e.g., 0 and 1, respectively. } Jain index quantifies fairness in terms of recommendation opportunity. It measures how consistently an item is exposed within the top-$k$, with respect to the number of times other items are recommended in the top-$k$. The higher the Jain index, the fairer the RS, i.e., the more consistently the exposure is allocated across items. } Qualification Fairness is equivalent to coverage, e.g., the number of unique items recommended in the top-$k$ divided by the total number of items. When $ = 1$, each item is recommended at least once in the top-$k$, therefore, the higher the Qualification Fairness, the fairer the RS. } Entropy quantifies how uniform the exposure probability is across items. The exposure probability is defined as the number of times an item is recommended in the top-$k$, divided by the number of recommendation slots across all users. The more uniform the exposure probability, the higher the entropy, and the fairer the RS. } Fraction of Satisfied Items is based on the maximin share, i.e., a threshold on the number of times an item needs to be recommended. The maximin share is computed as the greatest integer less than or equal to the ratio between the total number of recommendation slots within the top-$k$ across all users and the number of items. Basically, it is the minimum number of times an item needs to be recommended to ensure that all items have equal exposure within the top-$k$. Then, Fraction of Satisfied Items is the proportion of items with exposure above or equal to the maximin share. The higher the FSat, the fairer the RS, and when all items are equally exposed, $ = 1$. } Gini Index measures the inequality of the exposure allocation across items. If a single item receives all the exposure, i.e., it is always recommended in the top-$k$, then $ = 1$; if $ = 0$, then all items are exposed uniformly within the top-$k$. Thus, Gini Index measures unfairness; the higher the score, the more unfair the RS.\\ All the above-mentioned measures quantify fairness in terms of exposure only. Next, we present measures that jointly consider exposure and item relevance when quantifying fairness, which we broadly refer to as relevance-aware fairness measures . These measures are originally defined for a series of rankings, or recommendation rounds, with a stochastic ranking policy. In this paper, we consider a single recommendation round. } Item Better-Off is based on the principle of ``dominance over a uniform ranking''. First, an impact score is computed for each item based on its exposure in the top-$k$ and its relevance to the users. Then, IBO is the percentage of items for which the current exposure allocation increases the impact score by at least $10\ The higher the IBO, the higher the gain in terms of impact score over the uniform exposure, thus the fairer the RS. Item Worse-Off ~ has a specular definition: it is the percentage of items for which the current exposure allocation decreases the impact score by at least $10\ Therefore, IWO measures unfairness; the higher the IWO, the more unfair the RS. } Mean Max Envy is based on the concept of envy-freeness. Given an item, its exposure allocation is swapped with that of all the other items, one item at a time; then, the difference in the impact score before and after the swap is computed. The impact score is the same as in IBO/IWO. MME is the average, over the items, of the maximum difference in the impact scores obtained after performing all swaps. The intuition is that, given two items $i$ and $j$, if the impact score of $i$ increases when allocated the exposure of $j$, then the item $i$ would benefit more if allocated the exposure of $j$. MME is a measure of unfairness: the lower the MME, the lower the impact difference when swapping exposure allocations, and the fairer the RS. } Inequity of Amortized Attention is defined as the average absolute difference between item exposure and relevance. Exposure is computed with a linear examination function, i.e., a decay is assigned linearly with the item rank position. Exposure and relevance are min-max normalised so they both range in $ $, and can be subtracted. If an item has high relevance but low exposure, the difference between exposure and relevance will be high. Therefore, IAA is an unfairness measure, where a high score means that relevant items are less exposed than non-relevant ones, thus, the system is unfair. } Individual-user-to-individual-item fairness quantifies unfairness by comparing the exposure distribution of an RS against an ideal distribution. The ideal distribution assumes that relevant items get equal expected exposure. II-F measures unfairness; the higher the score, the further the RS exposure distribution from the ideal distribution, hence, the more unfair the RS. } All-users-to-individual-item fairness is similar to II-F, it compares the exposure distribution of an RS against the same ideal distribution used by II-F. The two measures differ in the way they aggregate the scores/distributions: II-F compares the RS and ideal distributions for each user individually and then aggregates the scores, while AI-F first estimates the RS and ideal distributions by aggregating across users, and then compares the two aggregated distributions. Several approaches have been proposed to handle multi-aspect or multi-dimensional evaluation~. propose two measures to evaluate the relevance and credibility of ranked lists, motivated by issues such as misinformation. One measure is computed based on the error between the item's actual rank and its ideal rank, i.e., its rank in the best possible ordering of items. The other measure is an extension of Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain ~ that accounts for both item relevance and credibility. presents a framework to evaluate rankings when relevance is defined by several aspects. It is motivated by consumer health search, where not only it is important to retrieve relevant items, but those items should also be easily understood by users. The proposed framework takes the harmonic mean of Rank-biased Precision ~ computed for topical relevance and understandability independently. proposes a principled framework for multi-aspect evaluation, which can work for any type and number of aspects. The framework defines an order relation among items labelled with different aspects and then computes standard Information Retrieval effectiveness measures, e.g., NDCG. extends NDCG to consider multidimensional relevance, item usability, and the content overlap among items in the ranking. The practical feasibility and validity of this measure is evaluated experimentally in~. In RSs, propose Expected Popularity Complement , a metric to evaluate item novelty based on three concepts: choice, discovery, and relevance. Relevance is approximated through user ratings, while choice and discovery are automatically computed based on a browsing model and what has been seen, respectively. None of the multi-aspect evaluation approaches mentioned above can be applied to the case of relevance and fairness. This is because they assume that each item can be labelled or scored for each aspect independently of other users or items. However, this is not possible for fairness, which is defined in relation to other users/items and can not be assessed in isolation. For example, the ``fairness'' of an item or a group of items is often defined based on exposure, i.e., how frequently an item is exposed at the top of the ranking across all users. Another line of work applies an aggregate measure on the scores of existing single-aspect measures: compute the harmonic mean of recommendation relevance and novelty to have a single measure that represents both; likewise, compute the harmonic mean of NDCG, $ $-NDCG~, and Attention-Weighted Rank Fairness ~ to aggregate relevance, diversity, and fairness. A different way of aggregating single-aspect measures is to combine or fuse the rankings of models/recommenders based on various evaluation aspects. For example, one may rank models from the best to worst relevance according to their NDCG score and separately rank them from the fairest to the unfairest based on their Gini score. A rank fusion method, e.g., COMBMNZ can then be applied to combine these rankings, resulting in a ranking of models that represents the best to worst models when considering both relevance and fairness. While rank fusion methods are often applied on rankings of items/documents, they can technically be applied on any rankings, including model rankings. Our proposed evaluation approach is similar to the above approaches, as it uses common relevance and individual item fairness measures in RSs. Yet, our approach has an added advantage over these existing approaches: it allows a comparison to the empirically optimal relevance-fairness scores, which are not obtainable by simply averaging single-aspect scores or fusing rankings. Furthermore, when looking into the different rank fusion approaches, several issues render them unsuitable for our goal. COMBMAX and COMBMIN would rank models based on the maximum/minimum of the relevance and fairness scores. As these two measure types typically have different ranges of observed scores , using COMBMAX/COMBMIN may collapse to a single aspect. On the other hand, COMBSUM and COMBMNZ may give equivalent results to the arithmetic mean: ranking models by the sum of their relevance and fairness scores yields the same ranking as averaging them. As the list of models to be ranked is identical across the two aspects, COMBMNZ would also give the same ranking as COMBSUM and averaging. COMBMED will also produce the same ranking as averaging, as the median of two scores equals their arithmetic mean. Rank-based fusion methods like Borda Fuse and Reciprocal Rank Fusion work by mapping a single-aspect score to another score based on the model's rank position. The resulting score cannot be used to quantify the gap in model performance when considering multiple aspects concurrently, which is what our approach aims to achieve. Aside from the relevance-aware fairness measures for individual items , several other evaluation measures also directly combine effectiveness and fairness. The Fairness-Aware IR measure~ combines group item fairness with standard IR effectiveness measures. It is defined as the ratio between the scores of the IR measure and of the fairness measure cumulated over the rank positions. Fairness is computed with the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the item group distribution in the ranking and the desired target distribution. Our approach is different because it is defined for individual item fairness; it does not require a desired target distribution, which can be hard to choose; it considers the empirically achievable optimal solution, while the target distribution may not be an achievable solution; and it can incorporate any individual item fairness measure. Fairness through Envy and Inferiority Reduction ~ and Pairwise User unFairness ~ are measures that combine individual user fairness and effectiveness. Instead, our approach focuses on individual item fairness, and we propose an evaluation approach that is compatible with existing fairness and effectiveness/relevance measures. Beyond the strict domain of relevance-fairness joint evaluation, there exist composite measures that integrate two or more aspects in a single metric. Similar to the relevance-aware fairness measures, these measures do not require a score for each item and for each aspect. Instead, each item is assessed in relation to other items. For example, relevance-aware diversity measures are defined in terms of similarity to other items in the same ranking. generalise the average intra-list distance metric by integrating a rank-aware and a relevance component into the measure. The proposed measure thus accounts for position bias and aims for effective diversification of recommendation. extend NDCG to consider relevance and diversity. The proposed measure is tailored to RSs and is based on the intuition that users prefer relevant and diverse items over relevant but similar items. As these measures do not evaluate fairness, they cannot be compared to our approach. Finally, some multi-aspect evaluation approaches have been proposed for Retrieval Augmented Generation ~, but they take as input the textual output produced by a generative model instead of a ranking of items. The robustness of evaluation measures for rankings in IR and RS has been assessed through multiple lenses, among others, in terms of how incomplete data or biased data affect their results . Traditionally, metric robustness to data incompleteness is evaluated by comparing IR system results based on an incomplete set of relevance data to those based on a less incomplete one, e.g., . First, they synthetically remove relevance judgments, producing several sets of judgments with varying levels of incompleteness. They then evaluate various runs using IR measures computed on these judgments and rank these runs based on their scores. Robustness is assessed in terms of how stable the ranking of runs is against data incompleteness. More recent work has also investigated such robustness of ranking measures in RS evaluation . evaluate the robustness of their relevance-aware diversity measure against random missing items. analyse how the removal of random items and users from the test split affects the ranking stability of RS effectiveness measures. They also study robustness against non-random missing data, focusing on biased distribution of ratings across items and users. From the test set, they remove popular items and users with the most interactions. The results show that, among other measures, Precision tends to be the most robust against both random and non-random missing data. Since our proposed evaluation approach, DPFR, is instantiated with existing relevance and fairness measures, we posit that its robustness would largely depend on these single-aspect measures. While the robustness of the relevance measures in RS has been assessed in prior work , to our knowledge, no such work has been done for individual item fairness measures. The challenge of jointly evaluating RS effectiveness and fairness is exacerbated by the fact that effectiveness and fairness are not completely independent. A trade-off between effectiveness and fairness has been empirically shown, with effectiveness towards users decreasing when fairness towards items increases~. Therefore, multi-aspect optimisation has been proposed as a solution to find the balance between effectiveness and fairness. The well-known Pareto optimality has been used in the optimisation functions of RSs to learn the trade-off between fairness and relevance, and recommend items to users. For example,~ present a recommendation algorithm that uses multi-objective reinforcement learning and the Pareto-optimal solution to control the trade-off between effectiveness and fairness~. use the concept of Pareto efficiency, a.k.a.~Pareto-optimal solution, to merge ranked lists generated by different optimisation aspects, e.g., effectiveness, novelty, diversity, and to create a weighted combination of algorithms optimised for different aspects. Computing the true PF can be unfeasible due to its complexity~. Therefore, some prior work could only show that their proposed algorithm was better in terms of Pareto dominance , without computing the actual Pareto-optimal frontier~. For example,~ show with the PF that their recommender model for fairness and effectiveness is better than state-of-the-art baselines, but they do not compute to what extent the proposed model is far from the Pareto-optimal solution. Other work used scores computed with a single or several models to generate the PF~, instead of using the optimal scores . For example,~ propose an approach to generate Pareto-optimal recommendations balancing Click Through Rate and Gross Merchandise Volume . The proposed approach uses the scores of the training losses and assumes that those loss functions are differentiable. generate the PF with the scores of different objective functions as inputs, and define an efficient way to select the best Pareto-optimal solution from the PF. Using such optimal solution during training leads to better performance in search and recommendation. Our work is inherently different from the above, both in terms of the purpose and also in the way we compute the PF. First, our aim is to use the PF as the basis of a theoretically grounded solution for the of rankings, not for multi-objective optimisation of RSs. Second, because or aim is to evaluate and compare rankings, we compute the PF using the ground truth, i.e., the labels in the test set, as opposed to using the models' scores, e.g., training loss, which represent an estimation of the ground truth and are prone to errors. Therefore, our PF is model agnostic and can be used to compare different RSs. Finally, our work is different from~, where the Pareto-optimal solution is the point in the PF with minimum distance from the utopia point, i.e., the ideal optimisation target. Such a utopia point might not be achievable depending on the datasets or measure characteristics~, instead, since we generate the PF from the test data, the PF will always be a set of Pareto-optimal solutions that are actually achievable. Given some user-item preference data , individual item fairness is usually computed across items while relevance scores are computed per user and then averaged. Our aim is to find the empirically maximum fairness towards individual items based on the given test set, such that the recommendation satisfies Pareto-optimality w.r.t.~fairness scores across all items and an average relevance score across users, e.g., MAP@$10=0.9$. We do this by measuring how far a model performance is from these Pareto-optimal solutions. The process of comparing all possible recommendations for users and items to find the complete set of Pareto-optimal solutions is computationally expensive and therefore infeasible, and there is no analytical solution either. We address this by proposing an algorithm that iteratively builds upon a maximally relevant initial recommendation list. Our algorithm iteratively finds Pareto-optimal recommendations by prioritising relevance over fairness, because, in practice, recommendations are usually optimised for relevance . This prioritisation is known as lexicographic optimisation, in which one objective is optimised first, and the other is optimised while keeping the first objective at its optimal value . The distance computation consists of two steps: determining a reference point in the PF; and computing the distance from the reference point to the model scores. For each pair of and measures, we find a reference point using a tunable parameter $ $; $ =0$ means giving maximum priority to relevance , and $ =1$ means giving maximum priority to fairness . Note that giving maximum priority to one aspect does not mean that the other aspect is completely discarded in the joint evaluation. For example, at $ =0$, the score will still influence the joint evaluation, but its impact will not be as large as it would be at a higher $ $. We explore what $ $ practically means in . Below, we present how to compute the reference point. We first use the following equation to find the length of a subset $T$ of the PF: $ lenPF = _{t=1}^{|T|-1} d_E $. Given that $P$ is the set of all Pareto-optimal solutions, $x^t = $ is the pair of Pareto-optimal solutions $ $ with the $t$-th highest $x_r$ in $P$, and $d_E$ is the Euclidean distance. The overall PF length is $lenPF $ or simply $lenPF$. The reference point is $s_{ } = x^{t'}$, where $t'$ is computed as follows: $ t' = _{j } |lenPF -\ lenPF | $. $T^{j}$ is a subset of $P$ containing the $j$ highest $x_r$ scores. So, the reference point is a point in the PF whose cumulative traversal distance is closest to the $ $-weighted PF length. In other words, the reference point $s_ $ is based on the PF length from the pair with the best score to the one with the best score, multiplied by $ $. As the PFs may have different densities of points along the frontiers, the reference point is not computed based on a percentile to avoid bias towards the denser part. Next, the distance between the reference point $s_ $ and each model's scores is computed with a distance measure $d$ that accommodates 2D-vectors . The model with the closest distance is the best in terms of both relevance and fairness, given the weight $ $. We call this . Generating the PF, as described in , is computationally costly. As an efficient alternative, we propose computing a subset of the PF. This can be done by selecting a fixed number of Pareto-optimal solutions to compute, $p$ . However, in order to reliably approximate the PF, these solutions should be selected so that they are spread according to the PF distribution, as opposed to, for instance, only computing the first $p$ points of the PF. The spread of the points is important, because the reference point in DPFR is computed based on the overall estimated PF. In the estimated PF, the first point corresponds to the measure scores of the initial recommendation given by the Oracle, and the rest are spread evenly throughout the PF generation. To select at which point of the algorithm the measures should be computed, we first estimate the total number of replacements needed by examining the distribution of recommended items frequency. We do this by computing the individual frequency count of all items in the recommendation, and subtracting the ideal upper bound of item count $ km/n $ from each count. The number of expected replacements is computed as the sum of the difference between the item frequency count and the ideal upper bound of item count in . Items with recommendation frequency counts less than the upper bound are excluded. With the estimated total number of replacements $numRep$, we compute the measures every $numRep\ $ replacements done by , such that the measures are computed a total of $p-1$ times during the replacement process + 1 time before the replacement starts. As a result, these $p$ points are spread evenly in terms of distance in the PF, which is important because DPFR is a distance-based measurement. How close is our generated PF to the true PF? We explain the complexity of addressing this question and how we resolve it in our experimental setup. *{Complexity of computing the true PF} Generally, computing the true PF on real-world datasets is regarded impractical due to its complexity~, and this applies to RS datasets as well. There are ${k}$ possible ways of recommending a set of $k$ items out of $n$ items to a single user, not even considering the order of items. Suppose that we use a dataset with a relatively small number of items, e.g., Jester with $n=100$ and a common choice of $k=10$, there are ${10} $ 17 trillion possible combinations of items for a single user alone. With a machine that can complete 100,000 iterations per minute, it would take around 323 years to go through all combinations, which is not feasible. One may also think of enumerating all the possible instead of the possible items, as two distinct recommendation lists can map to the same or score. However, this comes with other issues. Particularly, without knowing the exact item combinations, it is hard to identify which score can be paired with which score for a particular dataset, and vice versa. *{Experimental setup} To analyse the closeness of our generated PF to the true PFs, we brute-force the true PF for a small subset of each dataset, and then compare it to our algorithm's generated PF. Next, we detail the process of performing exhaustive search for the true PFs and how the closeness is evaluated. Taking into account the complexity of computing the true PF discussed above, we ask: how big of a dataset subset can be used such that the computation is still feasible? With 5 users, 5 items, and $k=3$, there are ${3}^5=100{,}000$ combinations, which can be completed in about a minute. Increasing either the number of users or items to 10 would be infeasible, as it would result in more than 10 billion combinations, which would take about 69 days to complete. Hence, to maintain reasonable computational cost while maintaining robustness, we take 100 samples of 2--5 users for each dataset and ensure that there are no duplicates of samples. The number of users should be at least 2, as evaluating item fairness for just one user is not meaningful. For the set of items, we take the union of test set items across all users within a sample. However, if users are sampled randomly, they altogether may have more than 5 items. Therefore, we need to constrain the sample to users whose test sets collectively have 4--5 unique, relevant items. To select the 2--5 users given the above constraint, we first get the $n_{pop}$ most popular items in the full dataset's test sets , as they are more likely to be shared across multiple users than less popular items. Note that the number of $n_{pop}$ differs for each dataset. Depending on the dataset's test item distribution, if $n_{pop}$ is too low, there may not be 100 unique samples of 2--5 users that satisfy the constraints. Through trial and error with manual increments, we set $n_{pop}=5$ for Amazon-lb, QK-video, and Jester; $n_{pop}=15$ for ML-20M; $n_{pop}=25$ for ML-10M; and $n_{pop}=255$ for Lastfm. We then get a set of users whose test set is a subset of the $n_{pop}$ most popular items. From this set of users, we randomly sample 5 and verify that these users altogether satisfy the constraint of having 4--5 unique test items; if not, we perform re-sampling. If no sample fulfils the constraint after 100 iterations, the number of users to be sampled is decreased by 1 , and the sampling process is repeated. For each sample, we enumerate all possible sets of recommendations. To get the maximum score for a given set of items, we rank the items based on decreasing relevance, based on the user's test set. For each possible recommendation, we then compute the NDCG@3 and Ent@3, to represent and measures, respectively; other or measures are not computed to reduce computational cost. This results in at most 100,000 non-unique pairs of NDCG-Ent scores. To get the true PF for each sample, we keep only the Pareto-optimal pairs of NDCG-Ent scores, i.e., pairs that fulfill both conditions across all score pairs: the best Ent score for a given NDCG; and the best NDCG for a given Ent score . This is based on the findings that single-aspect measures do not give equivalent ranking of models to DPFR at $ \{0,1\}$ . DPFR is intended as a joint evaluation measure that accounts for both relevance and fairness. Secondly, to select $ $, we encourage analysing how $ $ affects DPFR's correlation to the single-aspect measures used in its computation . If the intention is to select an $ $ that balances fairness and relevance, one can select $ $ where the and measures' correlation to DPFR are the most similar. In the case where the generated PF is short, or the recommenders' scores have low variance in one evaluation aspect, $ $ may not have much effect on the final ranking given by DPFR. As an alternative of selecting $ $, one can always choose to directly use the points in the PF. Suppose that the goal is to have a recommender model with an NDCG score of at least $0.8$. One can then search the PF for the best possible score that can be obtained for NDCG $ 0.8$ and use the - scores as a reference point for DPFR computation. *{Proxies for DPFR} We acknowledge that DPFR is not the most efficient joint fairness-relevance evaluation approach. As such, we have provided Efficient DPFR , which requires less computational time and serves as a good proxy for DPFR . In restricted cases where either the recommenders' or scores do not vary much, simple average's alignment to our Pareto-optimal based DPFR may surprisingly be better than weighted average's or other measures', though still worse than Efficient DPFR's. However, it can be hard to determine how low the score variance should be, for the simple average to successfully estimate the ranking of models given by DPFR. Another case where simple average may align with DPFR is when the dataset distribution does not have much trade-off between the achievable fairness and relevance . Nevertheless, obtaining this information would require generating the PF first. Even then, more research is needed to understand how the PF length affects the alignment of simple average to DPFR. For all the above-mentioned reasons, we recommend using Efficient DPFR over simple average to estimate DPFR. We have proposed DPFR, a novel joint evaluation approach for Recommender System fairness and relevance. DPFR has several advantages. Firstly, it can be used with various pairs of existing single-aspect measures. Additionally, it also follows the Pareto optimality concept, facilitating a grounded joint evaluation based on how close the recommendation is to the best possible fairness-relevance trade-offs, according to the ground truth. As such, DPFR is modular, tractable, and intuitive. Through a series of experiments, we find that no existing joint evaluation approaches can be a reliable proxy for DPFR. Further, our results show that DPFR is the most robust among existing joint evaluation approaches, as it is the least likely to be skewed towards one evaluation aspect. We conclude with a series of guidelines to guide the use of DPFR, by providing tips on how to best select the measure pairs and the reference point in the DPFR computation, as well as advising how to efficiently and effectively approximate DPFR. In this work, we designed DPFR for individual item fairness. Future work could extend DPFR to other RS fairness concepts and possibly to other RS evaluation aspects that are adjacent to fairness, such as diversity and popularity bias.
