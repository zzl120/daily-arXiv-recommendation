Thus, the model does not need an output layer of the size of the catalog, and a new item receives an identifier from its content alone. Following TIGER, most research has focused on generating these codes, learning them with collaborative signals~, and several companies now serve this design in production~. Decoding has also received attention; however, most of this work changes the model to suit the decoder, with multi-token heads~, trained drafters or self-drafting heads~, or scorers retrained for beam search~. A model directly served after training is still decoded with constrained beam search over the trie of valid SIDs~, and its large-scale variants adapt the width~, split the beam~, or rescore prefixes~, still running one dependent pass per SID level. This comes at a measurable cost: a single head decoded with beam search produces better rankings and content quality, whereas multi-token prediction provides higher throughput~. We therefore ask the following question: can we design a decoder that returns the exact output of a single head while avoiding its sequential cost? Beam search spends memory and latency, the two constrained resources of large-scale production systems, in a rigid way: on every request it runs $L$ passes of $W$ prefixes each and outputs a list only after the last pass. Yet most of these passes do not change the final output. The SID codebooks address far more cells than most catalog sizes, so below the first levels most prefixes contain single items. Beam search nevertheless spends a full sequential pass on each of the remaining codes of such prefixes. This approach also constrains the design of the codes themselves. Since every level adds a dependent pass, SIDs are usually kept short~, and recent industrial work moves capacity from depth to a larger codebook~. A larger codebook, however, makes the code space dwarf the catalog even more. Whether deeper trees would capture the embedding space better remains open. Moreover, beam search cannot tell which of its output lists differ from the exact top-$K$ of the model. Its output list is exact when $W$ exceeds the number of prefixes above the $K$-th best item at every level, which beam search cannot verify at runtime. In , for example, a beam of width $K$ prunes the prefix that holds the second-best item, and nothing in the computation of beam search reveals that its list is therefore wrong. Both limitations above, namely the fixed cost of $L$ passes and the missing certificate, stem from the decoder rather than from the model itself. Hence, we keep the model and its codes unchanged and redesign only the decoder. By design, beam search assumes that nothing is known about the final output before its first pass. However, most large-scale recommender systems in the industry can easily provide a draft of the answer. Most contemporary production stacks run a first-stage retriever before the ranker, and candidates are often computed offline using batched jobs. Alternatively, a batch job can store the top-$N$ items of the model itself, or of a cheaper, heavily quantized copy of the model that is exported at training time. The batch job can compute a high-quality draft for each user from an interaction history that is a few interactions old. At request time, the decoder can then retrieve such drafts through KV stores. Furthermore, one pass with a tree-attention mask~ can score the whole draft, just as speculative decoders verify a draft tree~. In this paper, we introduce (Semantic-ID Decoding with Exactness Certificates from Approximate Retrieval drafts), a decoder that exploits such drafts. Because the decoding is constrained to the trie of valid SIDs~, the pass that scores the draft also bounds every item outside it. Hence, can prove that its list is the exact top-$K$ ), which no existing speculative decoder for SIDs does~, and when the certification fails, a fallback expands only the prefixes that can further improve the output list. Thus, given enough memory per pass, the output is exact for every draft. The draft quality itself decides only how many passes are required. Moreover, uses the structure of the catalog trie, which beam search ignores, to announce how close to exact its output will be as early as after the first pass. Finally, since does not pay one pass per SID level on every request, we argue that it opens new avenues for deeper SID trees, which can capture richer semantic structures from the item embeddings. It does not change what is recommended, but only how fast and how reliably the model's ranking is produced. Lower decoding cost makes it cheaper to serve such recommenders, and a certificate makes their behavior under a budget auditable, which we view as positive. The societal consequences of recommendation systems themselves, including their effects on exposure and diversity, are the responsibility of the model and of the stack that deploys it, and are not altered by the decoder. For the purpose of this paper, we use the Beauty category of Amazon Reviews 2014~, which is commonly used to evaluate SID-based generative retrieval approaches. The dataset has 22,363 users and 12,101 items, and we split it with the leave-one-out protocol. The SIDs come from an RQ-VAE with three codebooks of 256 codes each, followed by a collision code, so that every SID has $L = 4$ codes. We train a four-layer decoder-only transformer on the histories of the users, written as sequences of SID codes. It should be noted that only modifies the decoder stage, and therefore we do not focus on optimizing the hyperparameters of the transformer to obtain the most performant model. However, the ranking quality of our trained model is within the confidence intervals of the published works using this setup. For every test user, we run one tree pass over all 21,091 internal nodes of the catalog trie to obtain the exact distribution of the model's probability mass. Every decoder is then a simple deterministic replay of these stored distributions. Thus, we can test every claim on every request, and compare the decoders on identical scores. We use with $K = 10$ as the reference target, since it is the output of exhaustive scoring of the model. The goal of is to return the output of exhaustive scoring; no decoder can do better than the model that it decodes. Thus, every comparison in this section uses as the reference for both decoders. In order to vary the sharpness of the model without changing the decoders, we apply a temperature $T$ to every conditional distribution of the trained model. The trained model corresponds to $T = 1$, and a larger $T$ gives a flatter model. Note that a temperature has a different effect here than on a single softmax that is commonly used in large language models. For a single softmax, a temperature rescales the distribution but does not change the order of its outcomes. Here, however, each of the $L$ conditionals is tempered at its own prefix and renormalized over the children of that prefix, so the ranking of the items changes. As $T$ grows, the probability of an item tends to the product of one over the number of children of each prefix on its path. The flatter models therefore rank the items differently from the trained model and serve as a useful proxy for a model trained on the noisy data that is common in large-scale recommender systems, since the conditionals of such a model are similarly spread out. Our baseline is constrained beam search with the same model and the same history cache, i.e., the setup used by TIGER. We do not compare against the speculative decoders for SIDs, because each of them requires an artifact beyond the trained model. AtSpeed~ requires a trained drafter, NEZHA~ requires a trained self-drafting head, and SpecGR~ requires a tuned acceptance threshold. Building upon the decoder of , this section shows what the structure of the catalog trie contributes to . First, we observe that most deep prefixes of a SID trie hold a single item. Then, we use this structure to save tokens in every pass and to resolve some open prefixes in a single round. In a SID trie, most deep prefixes hold a single item, and this follows from the size of the code space rather than from any one catalog or quantizer. The $L-1$ content codes, drawn from codebooks of size $B$, address $B^{L-1}$ cells, which is orders of magnitude more than the thousands to millions of items that a catalog holds (e.g., 16.7 million cells with the three codebooks of 256 codes of TIGER~). Moreover, every quantizer in use, whether a residual quantizer~ or a hierarchical clustering of the item embeddings~, spreads the items of a prefix over its codebook at every level, so that the number of items per prefix falls geometrically with depth. Indeed, the collision code of TIGER exists because the content codes alone leave almost every item in a prefix of its own~. Thus, below the first level, most prefixes hold a single item. At such a prefix, the model has one admissible child, so its constrained conditional is one and the item below inherits the probability of the prefix. Consequently, these prefixes need no pass and no token. We call a prefix when it has at least two admissible children. The of a prefix $b$, denoted by $S $, is the set of branching prefixes below $b$ together with the non-branching prefixes on the paths that connect them to $b$, which we call . Note that the connectors are needed because a node can only be fed together with its ancestors. We use $ = |S |$ to denote the number of nodes in the skeleton, i.e., the number of tokens that a pass has to feed to resolve every item below $b$ at once. Both $S $ and $ $ depend only on the catalog trie, so they are known before any request arrives, and the outputs at the skeleton determine the probability of every item below $b$. Since most deep prefixes are chains, the skeleton of a cluster is a small fraction of its prefixes, and the skeleton of the whole catalog is a small fraction of the trie. We exploit the chains in three places of , and in each of them we feed only nodes that are known to be needed. First, in the first pass, a candidate prefix whose subtree is a chain contributes nothing to the scores, because the conditional at its parent gives its probability, and every node on the chain passes this probability unchanged to the item. Hence, the first pass feeds only the branching candidate prefixes and the connectors on the candidate paths. Second, a frontier prefix whose subtree is a chain is resolved without a token for the same reason, since its item receives the probability of the prefix, which the pass has already returned. Third, an open prefix with a single admissible child makes that child open too, since the child inherits its probability. Thus, a fallback round feeds the whole unary run down to the next branching prefix at once, instead of one node per round. These rules leave the certificate, the exactness, and the bound of $L$ passes untouched. A fallback round of feeds the open prefixes and reads the conditionals of their children, which become the frontier of the next round. Hence, the fallback descends one level per round, and this is where the bound of $L-1$ rounds comes from. When the memory per pass is unbounded, the tree pass can instead feed an open prefix together with its whole skeleton, exactly as the first pass feeds the whole candidate trie. The ancestors of every fed node are then in the sequence, as tree attention requires, and consequently every item below the open prefix has an exact probability after the pass. Hence, if the first fallback round feeds every open prefix $b O_0$ together with its skeleton, at a cost of $ _{b O_0} $ tokens, nothing below $O_0$ remains on the frontier, and every other frontier prefix stays closed, since $s_K$ does not decrease. Thus, the open set is empty after the second pass, and is certified, regardless of the draft and of the model. In other words, the skeleton round saves rounds at the cost of additional tokens. Under a bound on the nodes of a pass, however, the skeletons of all open prefixes may not fit into one pass. In the memory-bound regime of serving, a pass of a few thousand tokens costs little more than a pass of fifty, so the trade favors the skeleton whenever the open set is a moderate number of clusters. Even in the worst case over all drafts, with every cluster open, the round feeds the skeleton of the catalog, which is a fraction of the nodes that an exhaustive pass over the trie would feed. Unlike the chain rules, the skeleton round feeds nodes before knowing that they are open, so it is not part of the default decoder. Industry-scale deployments typically use these models to retrieve multiple candidates per request with constrained beam search. However, this incurs a fixed cost of $L$ sequential model calls per request, and accuracy degrades as the SID generator becomes less sharp, without a guarantee on how close the output is to the exact top-$K$. Production systems therefore cannot detect degraded retrieval for individual requests. We propose , which uses approximate draft candidates and a tree-attention pass to score the entire draft in a single model call, memory permitting. This pass bounds the probability of every unexplored SID prefix and certifies the exact top-$K$ if the draft contains them. Otherwise, a branch-and-bound fallback expands only the parts of the trie that can still change the output, reaching certification within at most $L$ calls for any draft. Moreover, under a budget of $M$ tokens per call and $Y$ calls, which for $Y = L$ matches the resources of a beam search of width $M$, announces a factor $1 + $ within which its list will be exact at every rank. Depending on model sharpness, returns the exact top-$K$ using a fraction of beam search's calls or memory. Our work connects to several streams of research, which we discuss below. One line of research frames retrieval as the generation of document identifiers: the Differentiable Search Index~ introduced the idea, and the Neural Corpus Indexer~ and query-generation variants~ developed it for documents. Subsequent work studied its limits at corpus scale~, its reading as dense retrieval over prefixes~, and ranking objectives over identifiers~. TIGER~ brought Semantic IDs to recommendation with codes from an RQ-VAE~, in the manner of residual quantization in neural codecs~, and decoded them with constrained beam search. Item identifiers for language-model recommenders were studied by . Another line of research learns the tokenizer from collaborative signals~. Moreover, Semantic IDs were shown to improve generalization in ranking models~. The depth of the SID tree is still an open choice: an extra level can improve recommendation up to a point~, a shallower SID has improved offline and online metrics~, and a deeper TIGER degraded under beam search~, although depth changes the serving cost and the model at the same time. Industrial systems, such as PLUM~, GLIDE~, OneRec~, COBRA~, and the generative recommenders of Meta~, all decode with beam search over the SID trie, and a practitioner's study catalogs their design choices~. Although these works improve what the model ranks, they are orthogonal to ours, as they leave the decoding of the ranking unchanged. In comparison, we leave the model and its tokenizer unchanged and change only the decoder. Our approach applies to any of these tokenizers. showed that a single head with beam search gives better ranking and content quality than multi-token prediction~, which parallel SID decoders~ adopt for throughput. showed that constrained beam search can reach high top-1 precision while recalling branches poorly at top-$k$. Before Semantic IDs, tree-based deep models~ and Deep Retrieval~ retrieved with beam search over learned node scores, in the lineage of hierarchical softmax~, and showed that beam search over such trees is not Bayes-optimal and trained the scorers to compensate. These works adapt the model to the decoder; in contrast, we take the opposite route, in which the model stays as trained and the decoder becomes exact and certified. Constrained decoding limits each next token to the choices that are allowed. GENRE~ did so with a trie of entity names, and PICARD and grammar-constrained decoding did so with a grammar~. Decoding Semantic IDs is the simplest such case, because every item is a path of the same length in a trie, and the probability of a prefix is the total probability of the items below it. Hence, a prefix is never more likely than its own parent; exact search methods rely on this fact. These methods include exact search for the single best sequence~ and best-first beam search~. However, they search for one sequence of unbounded length, where exactness is expensive. In contrast, we search for the top-$K$ list over a trie of fixed depth $L$, where one pass over a good draft often suffices to certify the list, and $L$ passes always do. A similar idea appears in classical retrieval, where WAND and block-max WAND~ certify a top-$K$ list using score upper bounds that are computed offline. In contrast, our upper bounds come from the same forward pass that scores the draft, and the fallback expands only the prefixes that violate them. Speculative decoding~ drafts tokens cheaply and checks them against the target in one pass. Tree attention extends this to a whole draft tree~, and subsequent work added draft heads on the target~, lookahead and self-speculation~, staged and big-little drafters~, retrieval-based drafts~, and draft trees chosen under a token budget~. Perhaps closest to our objective are the speculative decoders for generative recommendation: AtSpeed~ adapts speculative decoding to beam search and trains an aligned drafter, NEZHA~ adds a self-drafting head, SpecGR~ drafts by retrieval and accepts by a likelihood threshold, and STARK~ shares the KV cache across beams. These decoders accelerate generation, but none of them certifies its list as we do. In contrast, we use the existing draft without training, score the whole draft in one pass, and either certify the resulting output list or correct it with the fallback. Our certificate has the form of the stopping rule of the threshold algorithm for top-$K$ aggregation~, i.e., the $K$-th best score seen against a bound on everything unseen. Moreover, the request-dependent cost set by the hidden probability is of the kind that Fagin's instance optimality describes. Similarly, any decoder that certifies its list must score every prefix whose probability exceeds the $K$-th best item's score, which parallels the nodes that best-first search must expand under any admissible heuristic~. The additive depth term in the budgeted round bound has the shape of the lower bound for exploring a tree whose structure is not known in advance~, which no exploration rule can remove. The catalog trie removes that uncertainty, because the decoder knows in advance which prefixes exist~. Hence, quantifies how much knowing the trie helps. Finally, the factor that we announce is the same kind of guarantee as in anytime and contract search~, which return a solution within a stated factor of the best one; our plan certifies that factor rather than estimating it. Sequoia~ also chooses its speculation tree under a token budget, but by expected acceptance, whereas our plans are worst-case. Constrained beam search, in TIGER and in the industrial systems of alike, needs a width of at least $K$ to return $K$ items, and in practice it runs a width well above $K$. A draft of at least $K$ items, which a first-stage retriever or a batch job produces without any model call at request time, is therefore a mild assumption. The method uses three properties: outputs of fixed length over a prefix-closed vocabulary, constrained scores whose prefix probabilities bound the items below, and a draft of plausible outputs. Any generative retriever over document identifiers has the first two, and most have a first-stage retriever that supplies the third. What is specific to SIDs, then, is the small $L$, which makes the fallback bound of $L$ passes a strong statement, and the unary structure of RQ-VAE tries, which makes the skeleton small. The catalog trie would have thousands of level-1 clusters and a skeleton far larger than a pass can hold, so the unlimited-memory results no longer apply, and the budgeted results of describe the decoder. The draft then has to be good enough to cover the model's probability, which the stored sequence does by construction, and the memory per pass has to cover the open clusters. This appendix explains what in that computation depends on the request, what can be stored ahead of time, and how the minimum is found. The round bound $R^* $ of is built from two kinds of quantities. The counts $c_j $ and the skeleton sizes at each level below a prefix $f$ depend only on the catalog trie. They are computed once for every internal node, with the skeleton sizes of each level sorted in decreasing order and stored as prefix sums, so that the sum of the $k$ largest is one array lookup. The frontier $F_0$, its probabilities $p $ and the score $s_K $ depend on the request and are known after the first pass. The threshold $t$ enters $R^* $ in only two places: it decides which prefixes are open, $p > t$, and it sets the integer $k_f = $ of each open prefix. For a fixed $f$, the cost $S_ $ of is therefore a function of the integer $k_f$ alone. Moreover, this function stops changing once $k_f$ is large enough. Once $k_f$ reaches $K_f = _j c_j $, every term $ , k_f)$ equals $c_j $ and the sum of the $k_f$ largest skeleton sizes is the sum of the whole level, so a larger $k_f$ changes nothing.
