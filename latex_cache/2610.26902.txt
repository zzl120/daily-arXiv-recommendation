We present Model Sanity Service , a production framework that addresses this operational gap by embedding LLM-based relevance scoring into a recurring monitoring loop with targeted sampling, strategy-aware prompting, structured alerting, and diagnostic follow-up prompting that categorizes failures into actionable root causes. MOSS has been running in production at a large U.S. retailer for over six months across recommendation, search, and offer surfaces, and has surfaced two confirmed serving defects. In practice, issues such as category mismatches, intent violations, or degraded relevance may persist until they impact users, while manual evaluation does not scale to the volume and diversity of production outputs . Recent work shows that LLMs can approximate human judgments in relevance assessment and information retrieval tasks . However, most such systems are designed for offline labeling or fixed evaluation sets. They do not directly address the needs of continuous monitoring: targeted sampling, model-specific thresholding, structured alerting, failure diagnosis, and operational triage in live recommender systems. We present Model Sanity Service , a production framework for monitoring and diagnosing semantic quality in product discovery systems. MOSS integrates LLM-based evaluation into a full monitoring loop that supports recommendations, search, and offers. A key feature is a two-stage design: the first stage scores relevance, while a follow-up prompt categorizes low-scoring cases into failure modes such as category mismatch, intent mismatch, and attribute inconsistency. This turns isolated scores into reviewer-facing diagnostics and aggregate root-cause signals. We make four contributions: We introduce MOSS, a production framework for continuous semantic-quality monitoring that combines sampling, task-aware LLM scoring, structured alerting, and log-based analysis. We propose a two-stage evaluation design that extends scoring with structured diagnostic prompting, enabling systematic failure-mode analysis and triage. Recommendation quality in practice is often assessed through aggregate metrics such as CTR, conversion, and revenue, while semantic relevance relies on human review. Prior work has studied recommender evaluation limitations , LLMs as judges , and LLM-based relevance assessment in search and product discovery . Industrial systems face additional constraints around scale, serving complexity, calibration, and operational evaluation . Several lines of work are adjacent to MOSS but address different problems. Recommender observability systems monitor behavioral metrics rather than semantic relevance. LLM-judge failure modes have been documented ; MOSS mitigates some by using structured numeric scores rather than pairwise preferences. Evaluator calibration and human-in-the-loop triage are well-studied but have not been applied to production recommender monitoring; MOSS's alert-then-review workflow is a lightweight instance of both patterns. MOSS differs from offline labeling pipelines by embedding LLM relevance assessment into a recurring monitoring workflow with alert thresholds, diagnostic prompting, human triage, and production incident analysis. Recent work also notes that LLM-based recommender methods can exhibit biases such as popularity bias ; this motivates treating MOSS alerts as triage signals rather than ground truth. MOSS monitors semantic quality of production ranking outputs. In each run, it samples representative contexts, retrieves the corresponding ranked results, and uses an LLM with task-specific prompts to generate structured relevance scores and explanations. The resulting judgments are aggregated into monitoring metrics and alerts, while detailed logs remain available for inspection and recurring-pattern analysis. Figure~ summarizes the workflow. When an initial relevance score falls below a configurable threshold, MOSS triggers a second-stage diagnostic prompt. This prompt receives the original context , the initial score, and the judge's reasoning. The LLM is instructed to classify the failure into predefined categories and returns a structured JSON response containing a primary cause, optional secondary causes, a brief explanation, and a suggested next step. This second stage transforms individual low scores into structured failure-mode labels that support aggregate root-cause analysis across strategies, categories, and serving contexts. To complement our automated scoring pipeline, we introduce an interactive AI agent that enables users to inspect individual LLM-based evaluations and uncover broader patterns across score distributions. While the scoring framework provides standardized relevance scores and justifications at scale, practitioners often need to understand why certain recommendations underperform and what aggregate trends may indicate misalignment. The AI agent supports this exploratory analysis through a structured yet flexible dialogue interface. The agent maintains conversational state and orchestrates the retrieval and interpretation of evaluation scores and metadata, as shown in Figure~. After loading a selected set of scores and their associated metadata , it enters an interactive analysis phase in which users issue free-form analytical queries. The AI agent supports two complementary modes: Users can request explanations for specific high or low performing scores, surfacing the LLM's rationale alongside relevant metadata to diagnose issues such as misinterpreted attributes, intent mismatches, or brittle strategies. The agent can aggregate scores across strategies or user-defined subsets, enabling questions such as ``Are there common patterns among the lowest-scoring evaluations?'' or ``Which outliers exist within this strategy?'' By interpreting the scores and their accompanying details through LLM-augmented reasoning, the agent reveals trends that would otherwise require manual slicing or external analytical tooling. By adding a conversational analytical layer to our evaluation framework, the agent transforms static score outputs into interactive analytical artifacts. It enables users across technical backgrounds to diagnose misaligned recommendations, validate strong ones, and understand system behavior at both granular and holistic levels. To guard against these failure modes, we extend MOSS with a drift-detection module which runs alongside the monitoring loop. To detect drift, every MOSS run appends one JSONL record per strategy to a persistent history file. Each record in the file captures the run identifier, timestamp, judge identity, strategy, per-strategy mean score, per-strategy median score, pair-level alert rate , and pair count. At the end of each run, the drift check reads the history, computes per-strategy baselines over two windows, and compares the current run against them. The abrupt drift check compares the current run's mean score and pair-level alert rate against a 7-day weighted baseline . At the 4-hour cadence, this window covers roughly 42 runs, which helps to smooth outliers while still catching regressions expeditiously. A score drop of at least 1.5 points or an alert-rate rise of at least 15 percentage points triggers a warning, while 2.5 points or 25 pp triggers a critical alert. A rise of 2.0 points also fires a ``suspicious rise'' alert, since abrupt upward moves in a stable monitor are more likely a data issue than a genuine quality improvement. The gradual drift check targets slow erosion by comparing the 7-day baseline against a 30-day reference baseline. The score gap and the alert-rate gap capture sustained decline even when no single run trips the abrupt thresholds. A 1.0-point score gap or 10 pp alert-rate gap fires a warning, while 2.0 points or 20 pp fires a critical alert. This check activates only once the 30-day window contains records outside the 7-day window, in practice about a week after deployment. The two checks run independently per strategy per run and can both fire together. The drift checks add only a few reads and one append per run and are treated as non-critical: exceptions are logged but never fail the run. Alerts are routed to the same channel as per-item alerts, formatted with the baseline window size, the current-run value, the delta, and the name of the check which fired. A minimum-history guard suppresses drift alerts until the oldest baseline record is at least three days old, preventing noise after a new judge or strategy is onboarded. The design is intended to complement the periodic re-scoring of a fixed calibration set; the fixed set catches judge changes that leave production distributions untouched, while the history-based check catches changes that manifest in production between occasional calibration runs. MOSS has run in production for over six months, monitoring three recommendation placements across three primary strategies and two additional strategies on a recurring cadence of every four hours. Each run samples seed items, scores the corresponding recommendation candidates using the calibrated o3 prompt, and produces structured alert logs for human review. The 1,773-pair calibrated production run summarized in Table~ is one representative run; the monitoring loop has executed continuously across this period. Two production incidents illustrate the diagnostic value of the monitoring loop. In a related-item placement, MOSS alerts were concentrated among long-tail items in a small set of categories rather than spread uniformly across the catalog. Investigation traced the pattern to an error in statistics calculations inside a collaborative-filtering step, degrading recommendations for a narrow but production-relevant slice of the catalog. Because the affected items represented a small share of total traffic, aggregate click or conversion metrics could easily dilute the signal. MOSS localized the issue to a specific serving context, category slice, and algorithmic component, enabling a targeted fix within the same monitoring cycle. MOSS flagged high alert rates in one of our production bundle placements. For a children's board-book seed, the LLM judge assigned high scores to same-franchise items and moderate scores to adjacent children's products, but sharply penalized backfilled items intended for older audiences, including an adult horror paperback , an adult romance novel , and a teen fantasy novel . The score pattern localized the failure to the backfill source rather than the primary candidate source. These alerts motivated efforts to improve the production bundling algorithms, leading to a newer enriched set of algorithms. To quantify the impact of these improvements, we ran a controlled offline experiment using MOSS on 99 matched seed items across the legacy and enriched placements. Micro-F1 pools all 466 query--result pairs together and computes a single F1 over the pooled decisions; it is the primary alerting metric because each candidate result contributes one review decision regardless of which query it came from. Macro-F1 instead computes F1 separately for each of the 21 queries and then averages the per-query F1 scores with equal weight; it is included as a stress test of per-query variability, where any query with poor performance pulls the average down regardless of how many pairs it contains. Macro-F1 is lower because several low-prevalence queries have few or no human alerts; under our conservative convention, zero-positive queries contribute per-query F1=0 even when the model correctly produces few alerts. Overall, these results suggest that the judge, score scale, and recommendation-selected alert threshold can transfer to search when the prompt is adapted from item-to-item recommendation relevance to query--result relevance. Several limitations remain. First, MOSS is a monitoring and triage layer, not an automated quality adjudicator: scores prioritize cases for review and diagnosis rather than replacing human judgment. LLM judges vary in calibration and can over-alert on borderline cases; generated rationales are therefore treated as review aids rather than authoritative explanations. Second, the evaluations differ in scope across surfaces because each surface uses a different evaluation design. Recommendations have full human labels and embedding baselines; offers use alert-enriched adjudication; search uses a 466-pair query--result benchmark. These differences support the intended monitoring use case but limit direct comparisons across surfaces. Calibrated-prompt results and operating thresholds are retrospective estimates; we mitigate threshold-selection concerns with leave-one-strategy-out analysis, but a frozen-prompt prospective test remains future work. Bootstrap confidence intervals are marginal rather than paired, so we report point-estimate comparisons without formal significance claims. The non-LLM baselines represent the strongest strategy-agnostic signals available in production; the benchmark is sized for monitoring evaluation rather than for training supervised classifiers. A further asymmetry is that the deployed scoring prompts use item titles only, while annotators had access to full product metadata; where titles are vague or ambiguous, this can produce false alerts that a metadata-aware prompt would avoid, and quantifying that gap is future work. The production incidents provide diagnostic evidence, and the bundle case includes a quantified offline comparison; measuring downstream business impact through controlled online experiments remains future work. Production embeddings cover 421 of 498 benchmark pairs, so production-embedding comparisons apply only to that subset. The warning and critical thresholds are initial operator-selected heuristics chosen to balance sensitivity and alert fatigue; prospective evaluation of detection delay and false-alert rates remains future work. MOSS operates on item, offer, query, and recommendation metadata rather than guest-level personal data; product examples are anonymized. All LLM scoring uses enterprise API endpoints governed by organizational data-use agreements. Search queries were sampled from high-volume head queries . Prompt-injection filtering, query-level PII detection, and systematic fairness audits are future work. No guest behavioral records or identifiable user data were used. Our results show that LLM-based relevance scoring, when embedded in a structured monitoring loop with sampling, thresholding, and diagnostic follow-up, can serve as a useful operational quality signal for production search and recommendation systems. The results support three operational observations for practitioners considering similar approaches. First, strategy-aware LLM judges add the most value where embedding similarity falls short. On our recommendation benchmark, the advantage over production embeddings and SBERT baselines is concentrated on Bundles and Substitutes, tasks that require intent-level relational reasoning rather than surface-level item similarity. For Related items, where category and attribute overlap are strong cues, production embeddings remain competitive. Second, a single judge, score scale, and threshold can transfer across surfaces. The o3 judge and alert threshold , selected on recommendations, achieve F1 0.831 and AUC 0.884 on a 466-pair search benchmark spanning 21 queries with diverse alert rates, within 0.005 of the search-optimal threshold, using only a surface-specific prompt adaptation. This substantially reduces the setup cost of extending monitoring to new surfaces. Third, the operational value of MOSS lies in localization, not just detection. In production, MOSS surfaced two confirmed serving defects that aggregate behavioral metrics would likely miss.
