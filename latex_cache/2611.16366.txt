While precise selection of these spans can improve context utilization, identifying them in isolation is challenging. A span may appear irrelevant by itself, and deciding whether to retain it may require examining other retrieved information. One can evaluate each span's candidacy with the full retrieved context, but this is computationally inefficient as the same text must be processed repeatedly. Pretrained decision model can be used to efficient evaluate multiple spans simultaneously, allowing each decision to consider the surrounding spans as well. However, our experiments show that their ability to identify relevant evidence deteriorates as the number of simultaneous decisions increases. To address this, we introduce , a fine-tuned decision model trained to make reliable evidence-selection decisions over large candidate sets. By training a decision model to select varying numbers of supporting spans from large candidate pools, CURATOR develops the capability to select evidence for RAG while retaining the computational efficiency of joint decision-making. We observe that CURATOR maintains a strong evidence discrimination capability as the number of candidates increases, generalizes across different tasks and document domains, and improves downstream RAG performance, particularly when the available context is limited. Retrieval-augmented generation enables large language models to answer questions using information retrieved from external knowledge sources . These retrieved external documents or passages helps LLM to rely less on parametric knowledge for knowledge-intensive tasks such as open-domain question answering . However, these retrieved passages often contain substantially more irrelevant information than is needed to answer the question at hand, e.g., background information, unrelated details on other topics, huge tables with only a few important cells, etc. Thus, providing the entire passage increases the number of tokens processed by the answering LLM and may make relevant evidence harder to use . This problem is amplified in agentic workflows, where redundant and unnecessary information consumes context that could otherwise accommodate future tool outputs and intermediate reasoning . This inefficiency motivates us to study how to select evidence at a finer granularity under a limited context budget. Rather than forwarding entire retrieved passages, we aim to retain only the sentences or spans that are useful for answering the question. . {figures/multihop_evidence_selection_infographic.png} Retrieved passages contain relevant evidence alongside unnecessary information. A collection of retrieved passages may contain hundreds of candidate spans. A simple approach is to evaluate each candidate independently using the question and the candidate span. However, a span may appear irrelevant when considered in isolation but become relevant when combined with information found elsewhere in the retrieved context. Figure~ illustrates this challenge through a two-hop question: one span establishes that London hosted the 2012 Summer Olympics, while another establishes that the River Thames flows through London. Collectively, these spans provide the right evidence needed to identify the river flowing through the city in question. Neither span alone provides the complete answer. Alternatively, if we provide the entire retrieved context while judging each span then a model would theoretically be able to better judge the candidacy of each span jointly with other evidences. However, this will be computationally very inefficient as we would need to process same contextual information repetitively while judging each candidate span. Joint decision models offer a promising alternative. These models score predefined choices rather than generating a separate textual judgment for each candidate . Recent multi-readout decision models can produce many decisions through shared inference . This makes it possible to evaluate multiple evidence units together while allowing the information to be shared between candidates. Figure~ illustrates three span-scoring strategies for identifying such evidence, motivating the use of joint decisions. However, the ability to produce many decisions efficiently does not necessarily guarantee their reliability. In our experiment, we show that pre-trained joint decision models become less effective at distinguishing supporting evidence from distractor spans as the number of candidate decisions increases. Figure~ illustrates this degradation on fact verification and multi-answer question answering tasks. This limitation is particularly important for fine-grained evidence selection for RAG, where a single retrieval step may yield many passages containing hundreds of candidate spans. That is why we investigate a core challenge in this paper: how can we preserve the computational advantages of joint inference while ensuring that the decisions for selecting evidence remain reliable as the number of candidate spans increases? To address this challenge, we introduce , a framework for scalable joint evidence selection. adapts a pretrained multi-readout decision model to identify supporting evidence spans across variable-sized candidate sets. We construct supervised evidence-selection examples from QAMPARI , HotpotQA , and 2WikiMultiHopQA , varying both the number of candidates and the proportion of supporting evidence present in those sets. This trains the model to make many simultaneous decisions across different evidence densities, including settings where only a few candidates among many are relevant. We use class-balanced joint evidence supervision to account for this imbalance. To mitigate the loss of general decision capabilities, we incorporate replay and distillation on general tasks spanning binary classification, multiple-choice selection, and scoring. At inference time, jointly scores every candidate evidence unit with the shared context. We then select the highest-scoring spans till the available token budget and leave out the rest. We introduce , a framework for adapting pre-trained joint decision models to reliable evidence selection over large candidate sets of spans. As shown in Section~, pretrained joint decision models become less reliable at distinguishing supporting evidence from the distractors as the number of candidate decisions grows. Our method addresses this limitation through fine-tuning a decision model with evidence selection supervision over variable-sized candidate sets. We also interleave general decision tasks in the fine-tuning process to reduce the risk of forgetting the pretrained model's original decision-making capabilities. Fine-tuning for our evidence-selection task requires a dataset with annotations identifying gold evidence spans within passages that support the corresponding answers. We use three datasets that provide such annotations: QAMPARI~, HotpotQA~, and 2WikiMultiHopQA~. These datasets contain questions, supporting passages, and the locations of supporting evidence within those passages. In particular, QAMPARI contains questions with multiple correct answers, each supported by evidence distributed across multiple passages. We divide the passages into candidate spans and use the dataset annotations to identify supporting evidence. HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA provide supporting-fact locations directly, while QAMPARI provides proof text that we match to spans in the corresponding passages. Supporting spans are labeled positive , and other spans are labeled negative . When the evidence label cannot be determined reliably, we assign $y_i= $. These uncertain spans remain visible in the shared context but are excluded from the training loss. To address the degradation in joint decision quality as the number of candidates increases, we construct training examples with varying candidate counts and evidence densities. We consider $K \{8,16,32,64,128\}$ candidate spans. For each candidate count, we use three sampling strategies: We uniformly sample the desired number of candidate spans. This preserves the naturally sparse distribution of evidence within the text. This sampling can also produce candidate sets with no supporting evidence. We select exactly three supporting spans and fill the remaining $K-3$ positions with distractors. As $K$ increases, the supporting evidence becomes increasingly sparse. We include up to $K-1$ annotated supporting spans and fill the remaining positions with distractors. This produces evidence-rich examples requiring multiple positive decisions within the same context. These strategies address two challenges of joint evidence selection. Varying $K$ trains the model to distinguish relevant spans as the number of simultaneous decisions increases. Varying evidence density exposes the model to both sparse and evidence-rich contexts, rather than restricting training to predominantly negative candidate sets. Together, they aim to improve evidence discrimination across different joint selection workloads. We develop our model on , a pretrained joint decision model based on Qwen3.5-4B . Retrieval-augmented generation improves language models by incorporating retrieved information . Conventional rerankers assess the relevance of retrieved passages or spans, typically scoring each candidate separately against the query . Listwise reranking instead considers multiple candidates together, but often relies on repeated generation to produce rankings . Related work on context compression reduces unnecessary retrieved information through extractive selection or token-level pruning . Unlike these approaches, we focus on making context-aware decisions for multiple evidence spans jointly, selecting original text under a fixed token budget. Decision models score predefined choices directly, avoiding the need to generate textual judgments . Recent multi-readout models extend this capability to multiple decisions within a shared inference . This makes them suitable for evidence selection, where the relevance of one span may depend on information in other retrieved passages. However, we find that pretrained decision models lose discrimination as the number of simultaneous decisions increases. We address this limitation by adapting a pretrained decision model through joint evidence supervision over variable-sized candidate sets. Concurrent work on DecisionRAG also uses parallel decisions from a pretrained decision model and observes limitations when evaluating large candidate sets. DecisionRAG retains a frozen model and uses hierarchical retrieval to divide large candidate pools into smaller groups. In contrast, improves the model's ability to make many decisions jointly through training, while operating on a fixed retrieved pool. These approaches are complementary: improving joint decision capacity could also benefit hierarchical retrieval. Unlike DecisionRAG, we evaluate fine-grained evidence selection under token budgets and its impact on downstream RAG performance. The two methods use different evaluation settings, so we do not make a direct performance comparison. We introduced , a span-level evidence selection framework that adapts a pretrained joint decision model to identify relevant information from retrieved documents under a limited context budget. By training the model to make multiple evidence decisions jointly, CURATOR improves selection quality while retaining the computational efficiency of shared inference. Our experiments demonstrate consistent improvements in evidence recall and complete-set coverage, particularly under tight token budgets. These improvements generalize to fact verification, financial documents, and legal contracts, and translate into better downstream RAG performance. Controlled scaling experiments further show that CURATOR maintains strong evidence discrimination as the number of candidates increases, addressing a key limitation of pretrained joint decision models. Our results highlight the potential of decision models for fine-grained context selection, moving beyond passage-level retrieval toward more selective and efficient use of retrieved information. CURATOR's joint scoring remains reliable at large candidate counts, suggesting it could extend naturally to agentic settings by re-ranking the accumulated evidence pool as new tool outputs arrive, dynamically sharing the token budget between retrieved evidence and the agent's working context. The experiment therefore does not establish a causal effect of decision count alone. We construct evidence-selection training examples from QAMPARI, HotpotQA, and 2WikiMultiHopQA. Each example contains a question, a set of candidate sentences, and evidence-membership annotations. The candidate sentences are extracted from the passages associated with each question. The datasets provide different forms of evidence annotations. HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA identify supporting facts at the sentence level. For QAMPARI, we align annotated proof text with sentences from the corresponding passages. These annotations provide the positive candidates used for training. We distinguish three types of candidate labels: positive, verified negative, and uncertain. Positive candidates are identified by supporting-evidence annotations. Negative labels are assigned only to candidates treated as verified non-evidence by the data-construction procedure. Candidates whose evidence membership cannot be established are marked uncertain. Uncertain candidates are retained in the model input because they are part of the retrieved context. However, their readouts are excluded from the training objective. This distinction is important when evidence annotations are incomplete: the absence of an annotation does not necessarily mean that a sentence is irrelevant. For each training question, we construct joint requests with $K \{8,16,32,64,128\}$ candidate sentences. The three sampling strategies described in Section~ expose the model to different evidence distributions. Fixed-positive sampling constructs requests containing three supporting sentences and $K-3$ distractors. For example, a request with $K=8$ contains 37.5\ supporting evidence, whereas a request with $K=128$ contains only 2.3\ selection problems without changing the number of supporting candidates. Natural sampling draws from the underlying candidate pool without requiring a fixed number of positives. It therefore includes requests with varying evidence density, including requests without annotated supporting evidence. Gold-included sampling prioritizes the annotated supporting sentences, subject to the capacity $K$, and fills the remaining positions with distractors. Unlike fixed-positive sampling, this strategy allows requests containing different numbers of supporting sentences. The combination of these strategies separates two important sources of variation: the number of decisions made jointly and the proportion of decisions that correspond to supporting evidence. We initialize from , a 4.54B-parameter pretrained decision model. We retain its existing decision interface, including the binary readouts used to score evidence membership. Each candidate is associated with a positive and negative decision option, and the positive-minus-negative logit margin defines its ranking score. We use low-rank adaptation on the query, key, value, and output projections of the model's eight noncausal full-attention layers. The LoRA rank is 16 and its scaling parameter is 32. The existing decision readout is also trainable, while the remaining backbone parameters are frozen. In total, approximately 3.8 million parameters are updated. Evidence requests constitute 60\ training requests, while replay requests account for the remaining 40\ defined at the request level rather than the individual readout level. Consequently, an evidence request containing 128 candidate decisions does not count as 128 separate training requests. For general-task replay, the frozen pretrained model supplies the teacher distribution over the valid options of each task. The teacher is not used to supervise evidence membership, since the objective is to improve upon its pretrained evidence decisions rather than reproduce them. The final checkpoint used in our evaluations is obtained after 2,000 training updates. At inference time, the model receives a question and a set of candidate evidence units. Each candidate has a binary decision readout, and its evidence score is computed using Eq.~. When the candidate set fits within the input context window, all decisions are evaluated jointly in one inference request. This avoids repeatedly processing the full candidate context for every individual decision. Some retrieved pools exceed the model's context window. In these cases, we divide the candidate pool into smaller groups and perform joint scoring within each group. In the full FEVEROUS evaluation, we use groups of at most 200 candidates. The resulting scores are combined into a single ranking across the original candidate pool. This distinction is important for interpreting computational efficiency. Joint inference eliminates repeated full-context evaluation within each group, but a sufficiently large pool may still require multiple inference requests. Therefore, large-pool evaluation should not be interpreted as processing every candidate in one model invocation. Grouping also introduces a limitation: scores produced in different groups do not share the same complete candidate context. Their relative calibration may differ from scores produced by a hypothetical single inference over the entire pool. In our matched FEVEROUS comparison, the pretrained joint model and use the same grouping procedure. Once all candidates have been scored, we sort them in descending score order. Starting with an empty selection, we admit a candidate if its token cost fits within the remaining budget $B$; otherwise, it is skipped. This continues until all candidates have been considered. The scoring and admission stages are separate. The same computed scores can therefore be reused across different token budgets without additional model inference.
