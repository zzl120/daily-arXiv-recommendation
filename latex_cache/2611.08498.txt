Andreopoulos}[ auid=1002,bioid=2] {Corresponding author} {Principal Investigator} {This work was carried out as part of the MS Computer Science graduate research project at San José State University.} The vast amount of information available in lengthy, distributed documents makes finding relevant answers difficult, especially when the answer requires connecting evidence from multiple sources. Standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems often struggle with this type of "multi-hop" reasoning and frequently face issues with context fragmentation. In this paper, we propose a hybrid RAG framework that integrates the structured reasoning of knowledge graphs with the broad semantic coverage of dense vector retrieval. Our approach employs a multi-stage pipeline using transformer-based summarization and semantic chunking to improve context retention. We construct a dynamic knowledge graph to capture explicit relationships between entities while simultaneously maintaining a dense vector index. During inference, the system uses a hierarchical mechanism that prioritizes graph traversal for precision and falls back to vector retrieval when necessary. We evaluated our framework on the MultiHop-RAG dataset. The results show that this hybrid approach outperforms standalone vector and graph-based baselines, achieving higher Exact Match scores and improved retrieval accuracy for complex queries. This confirms that merging structured reasoning with standard vector retrieval creates a more robust system for handling complex knowledge-intensive questions. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Contextual Retrieval Large Language Models Long Document Processing Semantic Chunking Hybrid Retrieval As the volume of textual information increases at an exponential rate, extracting relevant information from long texts requires innovation in Natural Language Processing retrieval mechanisms. Recent developments in Large Language Models have transformed NLP tasks by enabling models to generate fluent, coherent, and contextually dense text. However, despite their remarkable performance, LLMs suffer from notable limitations: a lack of grounding in external knowledge and an inability to effectively manage long contexts. Retrieval-Augmented Generation has emerged as a promising framework to address these shortcomings. By combining the reasoning and generation capabilities of LLMs with the contextual grounding of information retrieval systems, RAG models generate better-informed and reliable content. Document retrieval techniques along with generative models are an effective approach for optimizing long-document processing. In traditional RAG systems, embeddings are created for documents and indexed; subsequently, the top-$k$ most relevant passages are retrieved using vector similarity search and passed as context to the model. However, these approaches often fail when the information required to answer a complex question is spread across multiple documents or when long documents contain diverse topics. RAG encounters limitations when the understanding of intricate relationships between entities is not explicitly stated within a single segment. In scenarios where semantic similarity search is imprecise, or when the user's query is ambiguous, the system may retrieve irrelevant information, hindering response accuracy. Such situations necessitate advanced mechanisms capable of multi-hop reasoning across documents to selectively extract relevant content. To address these challenges, the LongRAG framework was designed to handle retrieval and generation in long-context settings. The authors concatenated semantically similar documents to form coherent units in a fixed-size window, leveraging advanced long-context LLMs. In our preliminary investigation, we utilized multi-hop Question Answering datasets, specifically Natural Questions and HotpotQA , to replicate the LongRAG pipeline. Our findings indicated that although long-context models combined with dense retrieval performed well on benchmark metrics, these systems lacked interpretability and struggled to infer implicit relationships between entities. Without a structured way to traverse the logical leaps, the system remains reliant on probabilistic proximity rather than verified evidence. This limitation is best shown by two common failure modes we saw in multi-hop scenarios: In cases where an answer requires linking a specific individual to a parent organization through a subsidiary, dense retrievers frequently fail to surface the intermediary document. This occurs because the intermediary chunk often lacks direct semantic overlap with the original query. Models often struggle to synthesize a 'hidden bridge' when the connection between two entities is implied by a shared context rather than an explicit cross-reference. While the model may retrieve the correct context, it lacks the symbolic framework to verify that these disparate facts represent a single logical chain. These challenges motivated our pursuit of a hybrid framework that augments unstructured retrieval with symbolic reasoning. In this paper, we propose a hybrid RAG framework that integrates a symbolic Knowledge Graph , built from document content, with a traditional dense retrieval pipeline powered by a vector database. The knowledge graph encodes entities and their relationships, offering a structured representation for logical inference. When generating answers, the system can reason across paths in the KG, while dense retrieval captures the semantic nuance of the text. This combination leverages the strengths of both structured knowledge and semantic understanding to create a more accurate and reliable QA system. We evaluate this approach using the MultiHop-RAG dataset , which represents real-world scenarios where evidence is distributed across multiple documents. Long texts introduce retrieval difficulties at several levels, including identifying topic shifts and maintaining contextual relevance within a single document. This is further compounded by information overload, particularly associated with multi-topic legal texts and scientific articles. Consequently, the ability to locate the most relevant document segments is crucial for the accurate satisfaction of user queries. Standard RAG pipelines face two primary structural limitations. First, fixed-window chunking arbitrarily severs semantic units, leading to "context fragmentation" where essential meaning is lost across segment boundaries. Second, simply increasing the context window is ineffective due to the "lost-in-the-middle" failure mode, where models struggle to attend to information buried between the beginning and end of a long input sequence. Furthermore, dense vector retrieval inherently flattens the hierarchical structure of complex documents. In scientific literature, semantic similarity is often insufficient; accurate retrieval requires exact terminological matches and the preservation of logical flow. A vector-only approach frequently retrieves conceptually similar but factually distinct segments. To address these failures, we propose a hybrid architecture that enforces the structural constraints of a knowledge graph alongside the semantic coverage of dense retrieval. The remainder of this paper details the implementation and feasibility of the proposed hybrid framework. We examine relevant prior work, demonstrate the efficacy of the hybrid approach for contextual retrieval in long-document QA tasks, and discuss the potential benefits and implementation challenges of this methodology for scientific literature analysis. Although LLMs excel at grasping human language and generating human-like text by predicting the next token, successful contextualization and retrieval from long, complex documents remain active areas of research. Work by highlights the capabilities of deep learning models to handle large volumes of information, such as clinical notes, to predict medical events with high precision. These developments suggest that similar techniques can be applied to improve contextual retrieval from longer texts—a challenge requiring both high-level semantic understanding and efficient retrieval mechanisms. LLMs are transformer-based architectures that excel at generating natural language responses based on extensive training data. However, their reliance on static training sets often leads to outdated, ungrounded, or hallucinated information, particularly when addressing dynamic or confidential topics. As noted by , LLMs face significant challenges: They tend to generate plausible but false information when the correct answer is unknown. They may present out-of-date or generic information when a specific, current response is required. Differing sources use same terminology to describe distinct concepts, leading to terminological confusion and inaccurate responses. LLMs are often augmented with external contextual information to mitigate these limitations. Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems supplement LLMs with external knowledge bases—such as articles, documentation, and structured data. This augmentation expands the accessible information scope and grounds the model to generate accurate, factually relevant responses. This approach is widely employable in sectors such as healthcare, finance, and legal analysis, where domain-specific accuracy is critical. RAG solves the problem of Large Language Models being static by combining parametric memory with non-parametric memory . Instead of generating responses based solely on training data, which may be outdated or incomplete, the system actively queries a reliable knowledge base during inference. This process helps by grounding the output in specific documents and mitigating the risk of hallucination in knowledge-intensive tasks. A functional RAG pipeline integrates three core subsystems: The document corpus is ingested and partitioned into discrete segments. An embedding model encodes these text chunks into dense vectors—high-dimensional numerical representations that capture semantic meaning . A dedicated database indexes the generated embeddings to support efficient Approximate Nearest Neighbor search. This component is responsible for retrieving context that is semantically similar to the input query. A pre-trained LLM takes the retrieved context and the original query as input to synthesize a coherent response. This is how a Retrieval Augmented Generation pipeline works at inference time: The user's natural language input is mapped to the same vector space as the document index. The system identifies top-$k$ most relevant document passages by performing a similarity search on the database. These passages are then concatenated with the query to form an augmented prompt. This helps LLM generate a response derived explicitly from this provided source material. RAG works well in areas where factual correctness is very important, like healthcare and technical help. However, its performance is heavily dependent on the quality of the retrieval stage. Heterogeneous data formats can introduce noise, while suboptimal text segmentation may cut off necessary semantic context. Furthermore, purely vector-based retrieval can struggle with temporal nuances. Incorporating metadata—such as document timestamps or authorship—helps to prevent the propagation of obsolete information . The key to effective retrieval is coherent organization of vast amounts of unstructured text available. Chunking is the process of breaking down large documents into manageable segments called chunks while preserving original semantic meaning. This step is critical to ensure that the model processes relevant context without exceeding token limits. Chunking strategies must balance segment size with contextual coherence. Overlapping chunks are often used to make sure that no vital information is lost at the boundaries. This is crucial for question answering and information retrieval. Knowledge Graphs systematically arrange data into a hierarchical schema comprising entities and relationships . Vector search encapsulates semantic closeness, whereas knowledge graphs represent clear logical relationships. This framework enables RAG systems to execute multi-hop reasoning by navigating connections to associate facts that may be textually remote yet logically interconnected . The main trade-off is the considerable overhead necessary for graph formation and the challenge of maintaining synchronization of the graph topology with swiftly changing underlying data. This section delineates the system architecture, organized into three processing phases: ingestion, dual-index creation, and hierarchical inference. The proposed framework operates on a dual-path logic to balance structural precision with semantic coverage: Raw documents are processed through a summarization model followed by a semantic chunking algorithm. This step ensures that text segments retain self-contained meaning before indexing. The processed segments fill two concurrent frameworks: a Knowledge Graph for entity-relationship mapping and a Vector Database for semantic similarity retrieval. The system implements a stringent priority sequence during retrieval. It initially attempts to address the query using graph traversal to identify explicit linkages. Should the graph path be incomplete or exhibit low confidence, the system initiates a fallback to a hybrid dense-sparse vector retrieval method. The experimental corpus contains significant unstructured noise, such as redundant headers and formatting artifacts. To address this, we implemented a preprocessing pipeline that prioritizes most salient content of the document over raw text preservation. {Arch.png} Initially, all the documents are passed through a summarization module based on transformer. This compresses the input text and helps get rid of non-essential formatting while retaining core informational content. By reducing the total token count before chunking, we decrease the computational load for downstream embedding and graph extraction tasks. The trade-off between efficiency and the retention of essential details needs careful consideration. Standard fixed-window chunking sometimes disrupts semantic relationships, isolating essential concepts within distinct parts. To prevent this "context fragmentation," we implemented a semantic chunking algorithm . Instead of splitting by arbitrary token counts, our method groups sentences based on embedding similarity. The algorithm computes the cosine similarity between successive sentences. Then a new segment is generated only when the similarity score falls below a dynamic threshold, signifying a change in topic. This guarantees that each segment embodies a self-sufficient concept, enhancing retrieval accuracy for cross-document inquiries. The symbolic layer transforms unstructured semantic segments into a structured graph schema. Rather than depending on established ontologies, we utilize a pipeline driven by a large language model to dynamically identify entities and relationships . {kGR.png} To optimize graph traversal, we implement a dynamic prioritization mechanism that classifies extracted entities into four categories: , , , or . The system assigns an importance score $I $ to each entity $e$ based on its degree centrality and relationship density within the chunk. This ensures that traversal initiates from the most information-rich nodes, reducing the search space for complex multi-hop queries. To ensure robustness, the system maintains a parallel retrieval pipeline. We utilize a hybrid retrieval strategy that combines dense vector embeddings with sparse keyword matching. Semantic chunks are embedded using a pre-trained Sentence Transformer model and stored in a FAISS vector database . We utilize an IVF64-Flat index to balance retrieval speed with accuracy. This component excels at capturing abstract conceptual similarities where exact keywords may not match. To complement the vector search, we maintain a sparse index based on token frequency. This is particularly effective for queries containing specific technical terminology or proper nouns. We combine the results from the Dense and Sparse indices using Reciprocal Rank Fusion : RRFscore = _{r R} {k + rank } where $R$ is the set of rankings from both retrievers and $k=60$ is a smoothing constant. This fusion prioritizes documents that are consistently relevant across both retrieval paradigms. Once candidate paths are identified, the system employs a weighted scoring algorithm to select the most relevant reasoning chain. The score $S $ for a given path $p$ is calculated as a composite of three metrics: The cosine alignment between the path's edge labels and the user's query terms. The frequency of the relation appearing across different document segments. A normalization factor that penalizes excessively long or circular reasoning chains to prevent hallucinations. This scoring mechanism ensures that the selected path balances semantic relevance with structural conciseness. This paper presented a novel hybrid Retrieval-Augmented Generation framework designed to address the challenges of knowledge-intensive question answering over long and complex documents. By synthesizing the structured reasoning capabilities of Knowledge Graphs with the semantic coverage of dense vector retrieval, we successfully mitigated the specific limitations inherent to each standalone approach. The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows: We introduced a dynamic data ingestion strategy that combines transformer-based summarization with semantic chunking. This mechanism ensures semantic coherence and reduces context fragmentation, which is critical for effective graph construction and multi-hop reasoning. We proposed a hierarchical retrieval system that prioritizes symbolic reasoning via knowledge graphs for high-precision entity traversal, while utilizing a dense vector fallback mechanism to ensure broad coverage when symbolic paths are insufficient. Through extensive evaluation on the MultiHop-RAG dataset, we demonstrated that the hybrid framework statistically outperforms baseline models across key metrics, including Exact Match, Context Precision, and KG Hit Rate. Our results indicate that while Vector RAG offers low-latency retrieval suitable for simple semantic queries, it lacks the interpretability required for complex logical deductions. Conversely, while Graph RAG provides precise reasoning paths, it suffers from sparsity issues in real-world unstructured data. The proposed hybrid RAG system effectively bridges this gap, delivering a system that is both interpretable and robust. Furthermore, our ablation studies highlight that the quality of retrieval is heavily dependent on the upstream preprocessing steps. The integration of summarization and semantic chunking proved significantly more effective than standard fixed-window techniques.
