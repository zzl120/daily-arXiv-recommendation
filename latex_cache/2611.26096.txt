Classical two-stage retrieval-and-ranking pipelines address scale by first retrieving a high-recall candidate set and then ranking it with a learned model~, but the quality of both stages is bottlenecked by how well the stage represents a user's evolving interests, not just the ranking stage. Recent work has shown that embeddings derived from large language models carry rich semantic signal that complements traditional ID-based collaborative representations in sequential and session-based recommendation. Boz et al.~ demonstrate several ways of injecting LLM-derived item embeddings into sequential recommenders, including aggregating them into a session-level embedding for direct similarity-based retrieval. Liu et al.~ use frozen LLM semantic embeddings together with retrieval-augmented self-distillation to address long-tail user and item coverage in sequential recommendation, and Cui et al.~ similarly use LLM-derived semantic embeddings to retrieve informative contrastive pairs for sequence encoder training. Kim et al.~ instead let an LLM directly consume the user/item embeddings of a pretrained collaborative filtering model, avoiding the extensive fine-tuning that LLM-based recommenders typically require. On the industrial side, LEARN~ freezes a pretrained LLM as an item encoder and aligns it to a twin-tower recommendation objective at industrial scale on a short-video platform, while TreeBridge~ bridges the semantic/behavioral gap between LLM embeddings and collaborative signals at Shopee with an online-offline hybrid serving paradigm, reporting a measurable lift in gross merchandise volume after deployment. Beyond embedding-level integration, a parallel line of industrial work has pushed retrieval and ranking architectures themselves toward greater scale and expressiveness. Zhang et al.~ establish a scaling law for large-scale recommendation models analogous to those observed for LLMs, and Zhai et al.~ reformulate recommendation as a generative sequential transduction task with their HSTU architecture, scaling to trillions of parameters and demonstrating a power-law relationship between model quality and training compute, while OneRec~ replaces the conventional cascaded retrieve-then-rank pipeline with a single end-to-end generative model. PinRec~ and GRank~ likewise adopt generative retrieval at industrial scale, the latter unifying target-aware candidate generation with lightweight ranking to avoid the maintenance cost of structured indices. Complementary to generative retrieval, Zhang et al.~ show that jointly optimizing recall and semantic relevance with a multi-head item-to-item retrieval architecture meaningfully improves both engagement and content diversity, Fan et al.~ propose a multi-embedding retrieval framework that represents a single user with multiple embeddings conditioned on implicit and explicit interests, and Lyu et al.~ show that incorporating an offline-computed, extremely long user history embedding is highly incremental on top of existing attentive sequence models deployed across multiple production surfaces. Together, these systems illustrate that the retrieval stage itself — not only ranking — is an increasingly active area where richer user/session representations, including those derived from LLMs, are being deployed at industrial scale. These results collectively confirm that LLM-derived session and user representations, and richer retrieval architectures more broadly, can meaningfully improve production recommender systems~. However, each of these systems was developed and tuned for a specific company's data, content catalog, and serving infrastructure, and the concrete design decisions (how the session encoder is adapted and fine-tuned, how its output is integrated into an existing retrieval stage, and how the resulting embedding is served with production latency and freshness constraints) are necessarily specific to each deployment context. We see real value in reporting a concrete, end-to-end account of how this integration was carried out in our own setting, to add to the body of practical, reproducible evidence available to the community. In this paper, we present LSER, a system built around our own multi-content-type recommendation setting on autodesk.com and the constraints of our existing serving infrastructure. We adapt a pretrained LLM to produce session- and history-level embedding vectors, concatenate them into our retrieval-stage user tower as features (while retaining learned in-tower sequence encoders for production), and build a nearline serving path that refreshes learned sequential and LLM-derived user vectors in time for our main content recommendation server. We validate their incremental value through an offline ablation across our full multi-content-type catalog, and further observe encouraging improvements in engagement and downstream conversion metrics in an online A/B test across several production surfaces. Our main contributions are: We design an LLM-based embedding path that produces auxiliary session and history vectors, adapt a pretrained LLM with custom modifications and a fine-tuning strategy tailored to our multi-content-type setting, and integrate these vectors into the retrieval user tower without replacing existing sequence encoders. We deploy nearline refresh of user-side embeddings for production serving and validate the system with offline and online experiments. LSER performs retrieval with a two-tower architecture: a that encodes a user's short- and long-term interests into a single dense representation, and an that encodes each candidate piece of content into a representation of the same dimensionality. Retrieval reduces to an approximate nearest-neighbor search between the user vector and the item vectors in the catalog, which scales to our full multi-content-type candidate pool (customer stories, product pages, industry pages, and learning content) at production latency. This retrieval stage produces a smaller candidate set of Top-$K$ items, which is then passed to a downstream ranking step that applies additional, more fine-grained scoring before final content is shown to the user. This section describes how representations in Eqs.~-----including $g_{}$ outputs and LLM-derived auxiliary vectors ---are kept fresh and supplied to the two-tower retrieval model without running heavy encoders on the user-facing critical path. A daily batch refresh of user embeddings would be too stale, since in-session intent can shift within minutes; running $g_{}$, the LLM encoder, and related sequence pipelines synchronously on each request is also infeasible given our latency budget ($ 100\,$ / $ 200\,$, with only $ 50\,$ allotted to model inference). First, the LLM path sees richer inputs: $g_{}$ consumes only events that resolve to a catalog item id (others are dropped or mapped to an unknown token), whereas the LLM encoder serializes the text of logged events across a broader range of content and page types, including pages with no catalog item id . Second, text embeddings carry semantic signal that can generalize to sparse or long-tail content whose id-based representations are weakly trained, which is consistent with the lift appearing on surfaces with a broad, sparse catalog. LSER is validated on autodesk.com, which serves on the order of millions of requests per day. It remains an open question whether the same retrieval architecture, session encoder design, and nearline serving approach would hold up at the scale of large social media or consumer platforms that operate several orders of magnitude more traffic and a substantially larger and faster-changing item catalog. In particular, the nearline serving path, whose freshness/cost trade-off is discussed above, may need to be redesigned at such scale, since the compute and infrastructure cost of recomputing session embeddings nearline grows with traffic volume. We view LSER as evidence that this approach is viable at our scale, rather than as a claim that it generalizes unmodified to substantially larger platforms. Our current LLM embedding path uses a single next-event-prediction objective to fine-tune the session encoder $g_{}^{}$ (initialized from a pretrained Qwen3-style checkpoint). We have not yet explored alternative training objectives or alternative base model architectures and sizes, any of which could further improve embedding quality or efficiency. Systematically exploring this design space is left to future work. We presented LSER, a production retrieval stack for autodesk.com that adds LLM-derived session and history vectors as auxiliary features in a two-tower model, with nearline refresh of the full user embedding stack . We described the retrieval architecture, feature construction, InfoNCE training objective, nearline serving on Amazon SageMaker, and an offline retrieval evaluation . Offline ablation toggles LLM features across the full multi-content-type catalog; an online A/B test shows statistically significant gains over a rule-based baseline on live engagement and product conversion metrics. Together, these results support augmenting learned retrieval with LLM-derived signals as a practical path for heterogeneous content recommendation at our scale, with online LLM-feature ablations and broader backbone exploration as natural next steps.
