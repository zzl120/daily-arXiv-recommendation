 Our ~datasets are collected from Kuaishou, a video-sharing platform serving hundreds of millions of users every day with a billion-scale candidate item set. We uniformly sample users from the full user population and collect their interaction logs over 30 days. We then apply user-side n-core filtering~ with n=100, retaining users with at least 100 interactions in the collection window to ensure sufficiently long histories for sequential-model evaluation. For each day, we retain interactions involving items created within the preceding seven days, forming a dynamic candidate item pool. As summarized in Figure , we provide two benchmark datasets: -FullMonth and -Small. The FullMonth dataset also includes a hold-out test set for the online leaderboard~. Table summarizes their key statistics, and Appendix provides additional analysis. Each user-item interaction record contains labels for multiple tasks, including viewing behaviors , positive interactions , and a negative interaction label . For each retained item, we provide a 64-dimensional multimodal embedding using the model in , an eight-level semantic ID generated by a fixed RQ-VAE-style tokenizer~, and MLLM-inferred tag IDs~ for semantic matching analysis. All these models were fixed during the generation of item meta information, and none was trained on the released 30-day interaction logs or held-out interactions, to avoid information leakage. The tokenizer card reports its training corpus, time window, and model version, and separately discloses any item-content overlap with external pretraining data. User IDs, item IDs, and tags are independently reindexed within the released datasets, and user profile features are omitted for privacy protection. Specifically, we summarize the following key features: On the first day, the pipeline trains a randomly initialized generative recommender or a pretrained model using the data from Day 1, i.e., $_{1}$. Denote the resulting model as $ _1$. Starting from $t=2$, for each subsequent day, the model parameters $ _{t-1}$ learned from day $t-1$ are used to initialize the training on the newly arrived data $_t$, generating a new model $ _t$. In this way, the model is continually updated as the data stream evolves over time. To preserve temporal consistency across days, for each user, when constructing the first few interaction instances of a new day, we concatenate the last several interactions from the previous day as historical context. This design avoids breaking user sequences at day boundaries, providing a more accurate representation of real-world streaming recommendation scenarios. For better regularization of the history lengths, we provide hyperparameter controls on their minimum $L_$ and maximum $L_$. For any Day $t$, the candidate pool will remove the candidate items created at Day $t-7$ and add items created at Day $t$. Besides ranking accuracy metrics, diversity metrics are equally critical in practical recommendation tasks. The mainstream applications of diversity indicators are dominated by list-level metrics, including Maximum Marginal Relevance, Intra-list Diversity, Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain, and Determinantal Point Process Score. Other works also reveal that system-level diversity metrics such as item coverage, entropy, GINI-index, and list variance are also important for maintaining a content-productive environment. In this work, we extend the aforementioned system-level diversity indicators to the token level. For generative recommendation models, token-level diversity metrics can reflect the diversity of generated results in a more granular manner. The pioneering attempts that studied the generative recommendation formulation date back to a decade ago~ when deep generative models~ were proposed in machine learning and later integrated in recommender systems. The main difference between generative and discriminative methodologies is whether the recommender is modeling the item generation probability $) or the expected feedback from the user $). While the generative modeling of $P $ is widely adopted in retrieval tasks~, the discriminative modeling of $P $ is more favorable in ranking tasks~. Despite this divergent viewpoint, both methodologies are functionally feasible for both retrieval and ranking tasks, and some recent works~ also notice that recommenders are fundamentally energy-based models that are simultaneously generative and discriminative . Technically, the generative modeling strategies in recommendation generally consist of two focuses: 1) The first focus is autoregressive generative solutions that aim to model user interaction sequences as next item/list prediction tasks. Following the insights from the development of large language models , new architectures~ are proposed for both retrieval and ranking tasks to reformulate the user interaction sequences with different tokenization techniques. For list-wise generation, additional designs are required to capture the candidate context information and item correlations in the output list~. 2) The second focus is non-autoregressive generative solutions~ that aim to model the distribution of user preferences across all items. Except for these LLM-style generative sequential recommenders, there are other directions that incorporate generative thinking. VAE-based models~ are capable of generating the entire item space~ and list space~, recent efforts have found that diffusion-based models~ and generative flow networks may have higher potential in generating item embeddings~, item scores~, discrete item selection space~, and list permutation space~. In this work, our main focus is to study the behavior of modern generative recommendation models on a large scale dataset, especially when the item space is too large to apply standard ID-based retrieval. The proposed ~benchmark is intentionally made agnostic to the recommendation models. For online systems that receive persistent user engagements and data updates, the notion of continual learning~ exists as a machine learning problem that addresses the challenges related to system dynamics, such as catastrophic forgetting~, robustness under distributional shifts~, and restricted computational resources~. Existing continual recommendation methods can be broadly grouped into replay-based, regularization-based, and architecture-based approaches. Our work provides the data and fundamental framework for continual recommendation. Recommender systems~ capture user characteristics and preferences from their interaction histories and filter new content of interest that fulfills users' demands for daily information consumption. Due to the similarity in the sequential modeling formulation, recommendation technologies have witnessed significant development in expressiveness and accuracy, as they benefit from the advancements of large language models and multi-modal models~. In addition to the LLM-enhanced recommendation paradigm~, recent discussions on the potential of LLM-style recommendation~ also reveal that scaling the sequential recommender on generalized semantic representations may achieve state-of-the-art performance, with generative retrievers~ and generative rankers~ as representatives. The great expectation under these new methodologies is a more guaranteed performance boost with the scaling of data and model size, i.e., achieving a better scaling law behavior compared with conventional recommenders~. {images/DataOrganization.pdf} Technically, these LLM-style generative models typically consist of: a) an , e.g., the item tokenizer, that transforms the item information into a token sequence in a unified representation space; b) a with scalable sequential recommender architecture that captures the sequential patterns in the unified token space; c) a , i.e., an item retriever that outputs items based on the generated tokens from the recommender; and d) a that either applies generative, discriminative, or hybrid learning strategies. Though promising, as this field has matured from its early stage, designers have frequently encountered critical challenges in practice: In content-intensive scenarios, e.g., video sharing platforms, composers frequently upload new materials and consumers continuously interact with the platform on a daily basis. On the one hand, this results in a highly dynamic item pool that forces the recommender system to design robust and long-term consistent solutions. Some studies~ show that generative recommenders with generalized item representation methods are more effective at handling incoming new items, but others notice that item IDs can become more expressive after these items receive sufficient interactions~. On the other hand, samples are chronologically fed into the training process in practice, following a continual learning environment~ that directly avoids the pitfall of ``learning the future, evaluating the past'' . This means that we need a more rigorous evaluation-while-training protocol for recommender comparisons, but it also brings new problems, including catastrophic forgetting~, robustness under distributional shifts~, and restricted computational resources~. : The item generation involves a token generation phase and a token-to-item retrieval phase, where both phases could be significantly biased even if the tokens are well-separated in the semantic space. In other words, the challenge of the accuracy-diversity trade-off~, which is widely studied with respect to items, also exists at the token level. In some extreme cases, this problem could be even worse since each item is typically represented by multiple tokens, where token-wise biases are inevitably aggregated. Consequently, solving this trade-off is expected to be much more challenging at the token level since the diversity-boosting strategy has to consider the combinatorial effect . On the other hand, diversity demands are often induced from explicit business requirements that have predefined categories. This terminology is not necessarily the same as the tokenization method in LLM-style generative recommenders; Thus, more effort is needed to align the diversity control protocols. Different from discriminative recommenders that can directly formulate the multi-task problem with multi-head/multi-expert predictive models~, generative sequential recommenders are not directly designed to optimize and control different metrics. During training, some studies show that multi-task SFT may help improve the generalizability of the model~, but the computational cost becomes a bottleneck due to the duplicated runs for each task during inference. Other solutions adopt an additional RL-based preference alignment phase~ that merges all objectives into a reward model for generator training guidance or inference-time reranking. Yet, there is no evidence that the current design of the reward is optimal for multi-task settings, neither is there a conclusion on whether training with multi-task learning or training with a single reward is a better strategy. recently witnessed several key technological advancements, benefiting from the semantic representation space and model-data scaling. Nevertheless, practitioners and researchers have frequently encountered critical inconsistencies between simple offline evaluations and complex online results, and this gap still exists in the era of large models. In this work, we address several key factors that contribute to this inconsistency: 1) the that assumes a practical demand to maintain long-term model effectiveness under dynamic item pools and distributional shifts; 2) the that requires the generation strategy to solve the accuracy-diversity trade-off in both the finer token level and the holistic item level; and 3) the , which is particularly critical for modern generative models that can accurately model the sequence distribution but are less controllable for the multi-task demands. To address these challenges and further pave the way for the development of more realistic recommendation solutions, we introduce , a deliberately constructed benchmark with industrial-level datasets from Kuaishou. Specifically, we provide a large-scale interaction dataset with million-scale multi-modal item information and long-term interaction records across 30 days, with a dynamic video candidate pool. Additionally, we provide a rigorous benchmark that evaluates retrieval accuracy, diversity, and multi-task performance in one rubric system, where an effective advancement occurs if and only if the new method improves all metrics.
