The structure attaches a stable identifier to the user, accumulates a history of visits against that identifier, and infers interest from the accumulated history. This structure rested on two premises. First, that browsers and operating systems would continue to supply identifiers. Second, that it was acceptable to send that history to a central server. Both premises are eroding, though not in the tidy way the industry once expected. Safari and Firefox block or partition third-party cookies by default. Chrome does not: in April 2025 Google decided to keep offering third-party cookies under user choice rather than deprecate them, and in October 2025 it wound down the Privacy Sandbox initiative that had been built to replace them~. So the accurate statement is not that the identifier disappeared. It is that a stable identifier is no longer something a system can : its availability now depends on the browser, on per-app consent that a minority of users grant, and on regulators who treat the linkage itself as the violation. A targeting substrate that only works when the identifier is present is a substrate with a shrinking and unpredictable denominator. In July 2026 the Korean Personal Information Protection Commission fined TikTok KRW~10.31~billion for collecting the third-party web and app activity of 9.45~million domestic users for customized advertising: the collection was not clearly disclosed at sign-up, and consent to it was bundled into the mandatory consent required for the service itself~. The same commission had previously fined Google and Meta a combined KRW~100~billion on the same axis. What is being sanctioned is not advertising but the --- behavioural histories accumulated against an identifier under consent the user could not meaningfully refuse. Without a persistent server-side identifier, cross-context history cannot be accumulated on the server in the usual way. Several alternatives exist --- contextual placement, first-party identity, browser-managed audiences. The one this paper takes is to the intent of the present moment on the device, keeping whatever history is permitted local. The search term the user just typed, the sentence they just read, the card they just tapped, already contains information about what that person is trying to buy. The question is where that information is processed, and the instant it is sent to a server we return to the very problem that just disappeared. A system answering the problem above has to survive inside someone else's application, on hardware we do not choose. Moving ad targeting off the server is not a new idea, and we do not claim it as one. Adnostic put the targeting decision inside a browser extension: the network ships a set of candidate ads and the client picks, so the profile never leaves the machine~. Privad reached the same goal through an interposed broker, keeping the network from linking a request to a user~. More recently, the Topics API had the browser itself derive a small set of coarse interest topics from local browsing and expose only those to callers~. A fourth line goes further than inference and moves the itself into the browser: Protected Audience keeps interest-group membership on the client and runs bidding there, so remarketing works without the server learning that membership~. That is a different problem from ours. It answers ``which advertiser's audience does this browser belong to''; we answer ``what is this person trying to buy right now'' --- semantic retrieval over thousands of commercial leaves inside an app-owned 3\,MiB payload. All four share our premise. Our matching problem is retrieval, not classification: a short colloquial query against $6{,}020$ leaf categories, most of which the model has never seen during training. The field's two standard answers are both unavailable to us. Lexical scoring~ needs no model but cannot bridge ``패딩 사고 싶은데'' to a category named 점퍼; dense bi-encoders~ and late interaction~ bridge it well but assume a transformer forward pass per query, which is exactly the inference runtime our size and latency constraints remove. The scale of the label space also has its own literature. Extreme classification builds partitioned label trees to keep prediction sub-linear in the number of labels, with dynamic search advertising as a motivating application~. We do not use that machinery: at $6{,}020$ leaves a flat scan is $0.8$\,ms on the device, so a tree would buy latency we do not need and cost us the property that matters more here --- that an unseen leaf is reachable the moment it appears in the table, with nothing retrained. Compressing the vectors themselves is likewise well-studied. Product quantization splits a vector into subspaces and codes each against a learned codebook~, which is what billion-scale indexes use~. We take the cruder route of scalar quantization with a per-row scale , because a codebook is state the device must carry and agree on, and our budget is spent on coverage instead. Shrinking a model by training a small one against a large one's outputs goes back to knowledge distillation~, and the standard products of that line for language --- DistilBERT , MobileBERT~ --- keep the transformer and shrink it. That is the wrong axis for us: a smaller transformer is still a transformer, and our constraint removes the inference runtime entirely, not just some of its parameters. Sentence transformers give context-aware representations but require an inference runtime and hundreds of MB of weights. The Model2Vec family distills a sentence encoder into a : a fixed vector is precomputed for every token, and inference performs only a row lookup and mean pooling after tokenization. There is no matrix multiplication, no attention, no runtime. The cost is the loss of context-dependence --- the same word receives the same vector regardless of its position in the sentence. In that respect the artifact we ship is closer to the static word-vector tradition than to a transformer, and inherits its weakness. What distillation changes is where the vectors come from: rather than being fit on co-occurrence, they are the teacher's sentence-level geometry collapsed into a table, which is why measures a $0.308$ gain over a same-size static model that was not distilled from a Korean teacher. This trade-off works to our project's advantage. What we need is not sentence reconstruction or fine-grained semantic similarity but routing short Korean queries to a commercial category, and for that task lexical discrimination contributes far more than contextual discrimination. Secure Aggregation is a protocol under which the server never sees individual deltas and can recover only the sum. What we run is a pairwise-masked aggregation prototype: masks cancel when every sampled client reports, but the share-based recovery that lets the sum survive a dropout is absent, so a dropped client leaves a mask that does not unwind. Throughout this paper we therefore write ``masked aggregation prototype'' and not ``Secure Aggregation'', and where an argument needs the real protocol's guarantee --- most importantly the privacy amplification in --- we mark it as an assumption rather than a property of what we built. There are two places to put the noise, and the choice decides who must be trusted. Local DP adds it on the device before anything is sent, which is why it can be deployed without trusting the collector at all~, at a well-known cost in utility per user. Central DP adds it to an aggregate and buys far better utility for the same $ $, but only if the aggregation step really is blind --- which for us is the masked aggregation prototype of , not a completed protocol. Our construction is neither textbook local nor textbook central: each device adds its own Gaussian noise before uploading, and the guarantee we claim is that of the , where those independent draws sum to $ $. That is a distributed implementation of a central-model Gaussian mechanism, and it is --- which is exactly the condition says we have not yet met. We mark the dependency rather than hide it. This asymmetry split our two-tier design into two branches . The project's presentation deck promised both an ``SDK under 5\,MB'' and ``running a 3B LLM on mobile with 4-bit quantization.'' The two cannot coexist. The weights of a 4-bit 3B model are about $1.5$\, \,$2$\,GB, and no compression fits that into 5\,MB. In other words, the two numbers cannot refer to the same artifact. Three interpretations were possible. Splitting the model off as a separate download voids the meaning of the 5\,MB figure, and publishers will not bundle the SDK. Treating the model as something the OS supplies works, but not every device has one. Making the always-on path a tiny static embedding rather than a 3B model removes the dependency on a resident model entirely. The conclusion is a two-tier structure combining the latter two: the tiny static embedding forms a floor that requires no inference runtime and no accelerator, and inference depth increases only on devices that have an OS model.
