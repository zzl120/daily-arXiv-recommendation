But a high score does not tell us whether the selected evidence is uncontested. Consider the query: A retrieval system may select the facts ``Alice was born in Paris'' and ``Paris is in France'' and return . A score-based confidence rule asks whether these selected facts received high scores. It does not ask whether the same local evidence graph also contains a competing birthplace, a conflicting path, or other candidates connected to the selected support. This distinction matters in structured retrieval systems. Agent-memory, knowledge-graph, and provenance-aware pipelines often already organize retrieved evidence as typed graphs. These graphs contain information that a scalar retrieval score discards: how the selected evidence is connected to plausible alternatives. We ask whether this structure can be used directly to decide when a retrieved answer should be trusted. Existing query performance prediction methods estimate retrieval quality from scores or relationships among ranked items . We instead study whether the boundary of the evidence actually selected to support an answer provides a query-level signal for abstention in evidence graphs. Our starting point is simple. Let $P$ be the evidence selected for the returned answer, and let the local evidence graph contain both $P$ and nearby unselected candidates. The boundary measures how strongly the selected evidence remains connected to unselected candidates in the evidence graph. A larger boundary indicates that the selected support is less structurally isolated; whether this separation predicts correctness depends on the semantics of the graph edges. The resulting abstention rule can be added to a structured retrieval pipeline without training a confidence model. It leaves the retrieval and answer-selection process unchanged, then uses a sparse edge sum over the available local evidence graph to decide whether to return the answer or abstain. This separates answer selection from answer acceptance, and offers lightweight post-retrieval abstention without additional model inference. The central question is: Our experiments show that the answer depends on what the graph represents. The signal is useful when edges encode : two connected candidates are expected to support the same answer hypothesis. Typed entity--relation structure can provide this property, whereas temporal adjacency, co-occurrence, or generic semantic similarity often do not. Our experiments identify alignment with answer-support semantics as a key condition for useful structural confidence. We study the weighted boundary between the evidence selected for an answer and the remaining candidates. We call this boundary statistic the ; it is the Dirichlet energy of the selected-support indicator , and it appears as `coherence' in table labels, figure legends, and released artifacts. An edge does not mean that two candidates are generally similar. It means that, for the current query, the two candidates should support the same answer. A boundary edge therefore connects selected support to an unselected candidate expected to support the same answer hypothesis; it does not by itself encode a contradiction or a competing answer. The method does not prescribe a specific encoder, scorer, or graph-construction implementation, subject to the support-compatibility requirement below; the abstention rule itself requires only the resolved selected support $P$ and the weighted graph. Retrieval scores are upstream inputs, used to resolve $P$ and for the score-based baselines. The abstention rule does not prescribe how the graph is constructed, but its usefulness depends on what the edge weights mean. In an existing knowledge graph, raw weights may encode similarity, proximity, or relation strength. For abstention, these weights can be preserved but gated by a query-dependent support function: $w^{}_{ij}=s_{ij}\,m $, where $s_{ij} 0$ is the original edge weight and $m $ measures whether candidates $o_i$ and $o_j$ should receive similar support scores for the current answer. More generally, the abstention rule can use any nonnegative application-specific edge function $w_{ij}=g 0$. Graph construction is separate from the abstention rule: a typed graph uses available entity--relation structure, while a semantic $k$NN graph has the cost of the nearest-neighbor routine used to build it. The method applies classical Dirichlet energy to the binary selected-support indicator, yielding the weighted boundary statistic in Eq.~. It measures how strongly selected evidence is connected to unselected support and supplies an abstention signal for the retrieved support neighborhood. For a binary selected-support indicator and nonnegative edge weights, zero cut means that no positive-weight edge crosses from $P$ to its complement. Equivalently, the indicator is constant on each positive-weight connected component, and $P$ is a union of such components. Increasing a crossing edge's weight increases the cut by the same amount; increasing an internal edge's weight leaves it unchanged. These are immediate consequences of the boundary sum in Eq.~ and do not establish answer correctness. Remark~ explains why graph construction matters. Increasing weight on a pair increases its contribution in proportion to the squared field disagreement. For the selected-support indicator $z_i= 1 $, $ ^2=1$ exactly when one endpoint lies in $P$ and the other does not, so increasing the weight of such an edge increases the selected-support boundary cut; this is the primary interpretation of the remark in our experiments. Adding weight is useful when the pair should agree under the current answer hypothesis, but harmful when the pair is only temporally adjacent, co-occurring, or generically similar. A support gate $m $ is therefore useful because it preserves raw graph weights only on pairs that should support the same answer. The preceding properties hold for whatever abstention weights the constructor produces, so their practical consequence is a requirement on graph construction: edges should connect candidates whose support scores should agree under the current answer hypothesis, and large weights on support-incompatible pairs (candidates that are merely temporally adjacent, co-occurring, or generically similar) add boundary cut unrelated to correctness. Appendix~ gives a continuous latent-class model that makes this requirement precise by decomposing Dirichlet energy into within-class and across-class terms; it is motivational intuition only and is instantiated in any reported experiment (every reported boundary cut result verifies the hard selected-support indicator of Eq.~). The experiments test whether the boundary cut is a label-free abstention signal when the evidence graph is support-compatible. MAB provides the positive setting. We compare the boundary cut to three kinds of alternatives. Score-based confidence baselines test whether the boundary cut adds information beyond retrieval confidence alone. Search-style baselines (leaf MCTS, chain-conditioned MCTS, and path-score reranking) test whether additional post-retrieval computation solves the same failures. Learned confidence baselines (logistic regression and a shallow gradient-boosted tree ensemble) test how a zero-fit graph signal compares with supervised confidence models trained on retrieval-time features. In addition, graph ablations and retrieval-stack shifts test the graph and the encoder/scorer dependence . Selective prediction is summarized by AUPCC: predictions are ordered most- to least-confident, and at prefix $k$ we take precision and coverage $k/n$; AUPCC is the trapezoidal area under this curve. AUPCC is the primary metric because the boundary cut is an abstention signal---it changes which predictions are trusted, not the base retrieval answer at full coverage---so we report both retrieval accuracy and precision--coverage quality, with bootstrap 95\ for key comparisons. The boundary cut is oriented so that a lower cut is more confident; raw, smoothed, and margin scores use higher-is-confident; predictions are sorted by $ $ and exact ties are resolved in the evaluator's native row order . Two implementations are used: the headline Table~ evaluator subsamples the $n$ prefixes to 50 points before integration, while the learned-baseline and bootstrap comparisons use all $n$ prefixes; on the headline signals the two agree to within $0.0024$ AUPCC. Continuous signals are effectively insensitive to tie ordering; discrete or constant structural-control signals (e.g.\ the closure indicator, complement size, or unweighted boundary count on the $P V$ subset) can be tie-order-sensitive, so their absolute AUPCC values are interpreted descriptively and compared as paired deltas under a shared ordering. Unresolved queries take a worst-confidence sentinel for the boundary cut that ranks them last; the score baselines carry their native small values and are not forced last, and the few missing score-derived features are zeroed by the historical implementation. Post-retrieval predictors have relied on the distribution of retrieval scores or on the coherence of the top-ranked documents, and score-based predictors remain competitive with supervised ones~. Diaz~ predicts performance from the autocorrelation of scores across similar documents, the nearest precedent for graph-smoothness signals over retrieved items. QPP has also been extended to conversational search and to retrieval-augmented generation~, and to dense retrieval under corpus growth~. Abstention has been studied for reranking in black-box settings~, and calibration for knowledge-graph RAG has been proposed because such systems are often overconfident when retrieved subgraphs are incomplete~. Several QPP methods are also unsupervised. The distinction studied here is the boundary between evidence selected to support a returned answer and unselected candidates in a typed evidence graph, used as an answer-abstention signal. The name is unrelated to coherence-based QPP, which measures similarity within the top-ranked set. Agent memory work~ organizes and retrieves long-term memory; we study when a retrieved answer should be trusted. Selective prediction~ uses pointwise signals ; the boundary cut is structural, using graph-neighbor agreement. The closest precedents are neighbor-agreement confidence methods: Trust Score~ and deep-kNN confidence~ trust a prediction when its metric neighborhood, estimated from a labeled reference set, agrees with it. The boundary cut shares this intuition but uses a typed evidence graph and no labels; the semantic-kNN ablation shows that generic metric neighborhoods do not reproduce the typed-graph signal. Conformal prediction~ provides distribution-free coverage via calibration; the boundary cut is training-free and could serve as a nonconformity score within a conformal pipeline. Laplacian regularization is classical in semi-supervised learning~. Test-time compute work~ shows abstention-guided selection outperforms extended search; we ask the analogous question for structured retrieval. Spectral-summary work~ uses weight-matrix spectra as data-free quality proxies; our signal is a Laplacian quadratic form over an evidence graph, with the support-compatibility view of Section~ clarifying when it should work. The model is qualitative intuition for support-compatible graph construction only, and it is not instantiated in the reported experiments. The abstention properties are deterministic and apply to the weights $w_{ij}$ of Eq.~, whether they are constructed directly or derived from raw graph weights $s_{ij}$ through a support gate $w_{ij}=s_{ij}m $; the energy only sees the final weights. To connect this to empirical behavior, consider a simple latent-class model. Suppose each candidate belongs to an answer-support class $y_i$, and its score is z_i = _{y_i} + _i, where $ _{y_i}$ is the class-level support and $ _i$ is local noise. Then E = _{ : y_i=y_j} w_{ij} ^2 + _{ : y_i y_j} w_{ij} ^2. The first term is score variation within the same support class. The second term is score variation across different support classes. A support-compatible graph constructor keeps little abstention weight in the second term: it assigns large weights mostly to candidates whose support scores should agree. A temporal graph, co-occurrence graph, or generic similarity graph may instead assign large weights across support classes, making the energy less informative for correctness. The latent-class model is stated for a continuous score field. On a connected component, zero score-field energy forces all observed scores to be equal; if that component contains candidates from two support classes separated by $ =| _a- _b|$, the noise must cancel the class-level gap. For independent $ $-sub-Gaussian noise, \[ 2 ). \] We use this only as intuition for the generic continuous-field model. Real retrieval scores are correlated through the shared encoder, scorer, and graph construction, so the independence assumption does not hold. Every reported boundary cut result verifies a binary selected-support indicator , so no reported energy is a continuous-field energy generated under Eq.~: we do not use the reported energies to estimate the model's parameters, and we do not use the model to explain the MAB zero-cut precision. For the selected-support indicator the relevant quantity is instead the boundary cut of Eq.~: what matters is which edges cross from the selected support to its complement. The latent-class partition is not, in general, the same partition as path membership (an unselected candidate can share the answer-support class of a selected one), so Eq.~ motivates graph construction rather than explains the experimental zero set. The observed MAB mechanism is instead deterministic and structural: in the regenerated population every zero-cut case satisfies $P=V$, so the indicator field is constant and its positive-weight boundary cut is empty , and Remark~ requires no concentration argument. We therefore retain the latent-class model only as motivation for support-compatible graph construction (a constructor should avoid placing weight on support-incompatible pairs), not as an account of the experimental zero-cut subset.
