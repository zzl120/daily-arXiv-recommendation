Generative and LLM-based recommendation has been studied through text-to-text task formulations~, efficient instruction tuning~, and semantic-identifier generation~. In e-commerce search, OneSearch generates semantic identifiers from the query together with short- and long-term user behavior sequences and refines personalized ranking with a preference-aware reward system~. Instruction-guided recommenders condition on explicit user requests~, and LLM recommenders can underuse sequential information~. These works establish instruction following and history use as objectives, whereas AIMS supplies intervention-derived supervision for the target--competitor comparison under a given request. Its asymmetry concerns auxiliary gradient routing, not the asymmetric input--output prediction tasks of LC-Rec~. Studies of personalized web search found that personalization helps queries with ambiguous intent but has little effect, or even hurts, when the intent is clear~. Personalized product search combines the query with the user's history~; the zero attention model attends over the user's history conditioned on the query and can attend to none of it~, and denoising attention has been proposed for query-aware user modeling~. Among LLM recommenders, train the model to follow the stated intention when it conflicts with preferences inferred from the history, using control instructions synthesized from item categories with labels from a sequential recommender, followed by reinforcement learning; our requests are instead real user queries with observed click targets. We therefore treat that pipeline as complementary to AIMS rather than as a directly comparable baseline. In LLMs more broadly, models may follow either the supplied context or their parametric knowledge when the two conflict~, context-aware decoding contrasts predictions with and without the context at inference~, and the instruction hierarchy trains models to favor higher-priority instructions~. These works analyze or control how much a model relies on the history or context, through its architecture, decoding, or training data. AIMS instead evaluates individual history events offline under the current request, using the observed target, and turns their effects into training supervision, leaving the architecture and inference unchanged. Retrieval-augmented generation also derives supervision from label-dependent estimates of context utility. Atlas trains its retriever to predict how much each retrieved document improves the likelihood of the output, and also evaluates a leave-one-out objective based on how much the prediction degrades when one retrieved document is removed~. FILCO labels useful context spans with label-dependent criteria, including string inclusion, lexical overlap, and the ratio of gold-output probabilities with and without a span as the supplied context; it then trains a filter that runs without gold outputs at inference and supplies the filtered context to the generator~. Because filtering can also discard relevant passages, instead train the reader on mixed relevant and irrelevant contexts. AIMS also measures signed utility with the label: in our audit , the tested label-free influence measure is not a reliable proxy for signed target support. AIMS differs in what it intervenes on and how it uses the result. It deletes individual events of a user's history under the current request, and it turns their effects into a margin target for the recommender itself rather than training a retriever or filter or editing the input, so the recommender keeps the complete history during training and inference. Training on the same deletion views instead is less effective . Every model in that comparison uses the complete history at inference, so it concerns how deletions enter training. A filter trained on our deletion labels and applied at inference would instead add a selection step to deployment, and it would have to predict target-dependent effects without the target . CauseRec scores concepts relative to the next-item target and replaces indispensable or dispensable concepts to construct contrastive user-sequence representations~. It therefore provides a precedent for target-aware history transformations used during training. STEAM learns keep, delete, and insert operations and supplies corrected sequences to its recommender~. In candidate-conditioned click-through-rate prediction, ReLLa retrieves semantically relevant historical interactions and mixes retrieved and original views during tuning~. In full-catalog generative retrieval there is no candidate item at inference, so ReLLa would have to retrieve history by the request, an inference-time selection step like the filters discussed above. These approaches improve recommendation through sequence synthesis, correction, or selection. AIMS evaluates the contribution of historical evidence to the current target--competitor comparison. An accepted deletion need not identify an erroneous interaction: the recorded behavior may be valid while contributing adversely under the present instruction. CFT combines standard likelihood training with a target-label objective on the difference between full-history and history-absent logits~. Its counterfactual signal is thus label-directed, rather than an unsigned sensitivity objective. GDRT groups target-token occurrences according to their predictability without user history and applies group-distributionally robust tuning~. This changes training emphasis across groups, whereas AIMS constructs a margin target for an individual request. SPRec addresses output bias through self-play: observed interactions provide positive responses, previous model predictions provide negatives, and SFT alternates with DPO~. AIMS instead keeps its initially decoded competitor fixed throughout continued training. At decoding time, D$^3$ addresses amplification and homogeneity by removing length normalization associated with nearly deterministic tokens and incorporating scores from an auxiliary recommender~. AIMS retains the original SID-constrained deployment decoder. Relative item preference is central to recommendation objectives such as BPR~. In generative retrieval, GR$^2$ incorporates graded relevance through identifier design and constrained contrastive learning~. Among our baselines, LETTER applies LETTER's temperature-scaled ranking-guided generation loss~, and LTRGR adds a learning-to-rank phase with a rank loss over retrieved candidates~. OneRec applies iterative preference alignment to generated recommendation sessions, using a reward model to select preferred and rejected outputs~. For language-model alignment, DPO optimizes response pairs using reference-relative sequence probabilities, while SimPO uses length-normalized likelihoods and a target reward margin~. S-DPO, another baseline, extends DPO to multiple negatives for LM-based recommenders~. Unlikelihood training complements likelihood maximization with penalties on undesirable outputs~. These objectives shape the ranked list, preferences between outputs, or the likelihood of undesired outputs in general. AIMS instead derives its comparison threshold from reference margins, strengthened by eligible history interventions, and gates its auxiliary penalty by the student's margin shortfall. Recent ACPO likewise uses asymmetric preference optimization, retaining the chosen-response gradient while adaptively scaling the rejected-response gradient~. AIMS instead stops the target branch of its auxiliary hinge and updates the competitor branch when the constraint is active. Routing the auxiliary gradient through both scores, as symmetric pairwise losses do, lowers Recall@10 relative to competitor-only routing . Analyses of finetuning dynamics and likelihood displacement show why direct gradient choices must be distinguished from their effects on the full predictive distribution~. In particular, shared parameters can still change target scores indirectly despite the stopped auxiliary branch. Learning with different teacher and student inputs connects AIMS to generalized distillation~ and context distillation, which transfers benefits obtained from a teacher's context into a model evaluated with a different context~. More directly, Margin-MSE trains neural rankers to match cached teacher score differences between document pairs~. AIMS also transfers a scalar comparison, but strengthens it with eligible history deletions and treats it as a minimum desired margin with a tolerance, rather than an equality target. The focus on initially correct requests connects to the broader goal of retaining existing capabilities during adaptation, as in Learning without Forgetting~. AIMS operates within one recommendation task, with the original training requests available. Its reference supplies a selected target--competitor comparison, which an eligible intervention can strengthen. This selective constraint complements CE without requiring the student to reproduce the reference's entire output distribution. A KL penalty toward the reference model, as in the KL-regularized objective underlying DPO~, is the distribution-level form of such preservation, and our S-DPO baseline optimizes a preference loss derived from this objective with $ _0$ as the frozen reference . Quantifying a feature's contribution through removal is a well-established explanation strategy~. Additive attribution methods such as SHAP also assign signed contributions to individual predictions~. Influence functions instead trace predictions to training examples through approximate changes in model parameters~. ACCENT extends this approach to the score difference between a recommended item and a replacement, searching for a small set of historical training interactions whose removal would reverse the recommendation~. Its intervention concerns estimated retraining effects, whereas AIMS directly evaluates input deletions with a frozen model and uses their label-directed improvements as supervision. Personalized search asks when to rely on the user's history~, and train LLM recommenders with category-based instructions and reinforcement learning to follow stated intentions that conflict with past preferences. CFT and GDRT improve history use via counterfactual logit comparisons and group-robust training~; CauseRec, STEAM, and ReLLa synthesize, correct, or select the input history~; and retrieval-augmented generation trains retrievers and inference-time filters on label-dependent utility~. LETTER, LTRGR, S-DPO, and OneRec use ranking or preference losses~, and Margin-MSE distills teacher score margins~. AIMS instead turns single-event deletions into request-specific margin targets while keeping complete histories in training and inference, penalizing shortfalls via the competitor only . Generative recommenders retrieve and rank items by decoding item identifiers autoregressively. In the semantic-identifier design, each identifier is a short sequence of discrete codes derived from item content~. Recent systems build on a pre-trained large language model ~, whose ability to interpret natural language enables ~, the setting of this work. Given a natural-language request that states the user's current intent and an interaction history that records past behavior, the model should retrieve items that satisfy the request and match the user's preferences. Ideally, the two inputs play different roles: the request constrains what is acceptable now, and the history personalizes the choice among acceptable items. =-1 In practice, however, history events that conflict with the current request can override it. Suppose a user who watched a cooking video of braised pork ribs asks for a quick vegetarian weeknight dinner, under 30 minutes . The video suggests a taste for slow-cooked meat, so the model ranks a three-hour beef stew higher than it would without the video, although the stew is neither vegetarian nor quick. Meanwhile, the same video pushes down the 20-minute chickpea pasta the user wants . Existing approaches improve the use of interaction histories through counterfactual training and context-bias mitigation~, edit or select history events before supplying them to the model~, or reduce output bias through preference optimization~. Editing a history can reduce the effect of an unhelpful event, but may also remove information useful for personalizing the recommendation. We instead use the request-dependent effects of single history events to define a score gap between the target and a competitor near the recommendation cutoff, which the model learns from the complete history. Using single-event interventions as supervision requires knowing which ones help, and an audit of frozen recommenders reveals two obstacles. First, the magnitude of an event's influence on the prediction, a label-free quantity, does not determine whether the event supports or suppresses the target: the most influential event in a history is not reliably more likely than a random one to support the target. We call this separation between prediction influence and signed target support . Second, a higher target score does not necessarily improve the recommendation: a deletion can raise the target score while raising that of a competing item near the recommendation cutoff even more, reducing their score gap . These findings motivate two design choices. We evaluate interventions offline, where the observed training target, unavailable at inference, is known and signed support can be measured directly. We accept an intervention only if it improves both the target score and its margin over the boundary competitor, treating this margin as a surrogate for, not a guarantee of, the decoded decision. Selecting deletions by label-free influence, or by target uplift instead of margin gain, yields fewer eligible deletions and lower Recall@10 . We therefore propose , in which a history deletion defines the supervision, not the training input. A frozen reference model evaluates single-event deletions, accepts a deletion only if it improves both the target score and the target--competitor margin, and caches the largest accepted margin as a request-specific . A student initialized from the same checkpoint learns this margin from the complete history. Cross-entropy supervises the observed target on all training requests, and an auxiliary loss, , penalizes the student's margin when it falls below the counterfactual margin by more than a tolerance. This loss is asymmetric in its gradient: since the target is already supervised directly, its score enters the forward margin but is detached, so the auxiliary gradient passes only through the competitor score. It is also selective in scope: ACS applies only to initially correct training requests, to help protect them from displacement during continued training, yet the gains also extend to initially missed requests . At inference, AIMS uses the original decoder on the complete input, with no reference-model evaluation or deletion search. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: We document Influence--Utility Misalignment: a history event's influence on the prediction does not determine its signed support for the target. We also show that target-score gains alone do not determine the decoded ranking. AIMS converts instruction-conditioned history deletions into counterfactual margin targets and penalizes margin shortfalls on complete histories through an asymmetric auxiliary loss. On an industrial dataset and two public benchmarks, AIMS improves Recall and NDCG over strong baselines. Matched ablations support request-specific margins, margin-based deletion selection, complete-history training, and ACS. On constraint-bearing training requests, the selected deletions remove violating history events more often than expected by chance. A history-dropout control does not reproduce the gains.
