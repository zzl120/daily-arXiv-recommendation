Generative retrieval reframes candidate retrieval as autoregressive sequence generation~, assigning each item fixed-length Semantic IDs and training a single model to generate SIDs directly~. This formulation mitigates item cold-start challenges and avoids unbounded vocabulary growth, supporting its increasing adoption in industrial search, advertising, and recommendation systems~. Despite these advances, the dominant GR paradigm combines training with at inference~: each target is supervised independently, and beam search produces the Top-$K$ list. This combination has two limitations: Candidates are generated independently under a shared request context, without explicitly conditioning on previously retrieved items. By making candidates compete for the top-1 position, item-level reinforcement learning struggles to optimize list-level objectives such as diversity~ and may compromise deeper coverage~. {Figures/intro.png} A natural alternative is ~, which generates the entire list in a single autoregressive trajectory, with each item conditioned on the preceding items. However, when both decoding strategies use the same directly trained list-wise model, greedy decoding underperforms beam search by $18.4\ Beam search explores more SID sequences than greedy decoding, enabling it to identify items with high joint SID probabilities. The advantage is evident at HR@1. In interaction-ordered targets, converted items precede clicked and impressed items. Because conversions are sparse, list position can become associated with interaction type, weakening supervision for retrieving relevant items across all list positions. This supervision bias contributes to the widening HR gap at larger cutoffs. The strong performance of beam search suggests that the trained model has learned relevance patterns that single greedy decoding does not fully exploit. From a distributional perspective, existing relevance supervision provides only a partial view of the retrieval space: behavioral labels reflect interactions with exposed items, while fine-ranking targets cover candidates selected by upstream retrieval. Direct training therefore learns from a distribution shaped by the existing system's candidate-selection and exposure mechanisms, although retrieval must identify relevant items across the full item catalog. This raises a natural question: can we reconstruct supervision beyond the observed candidate subset and make it accessible to single greedy decoding? Our key insight is to replace supervision restricted to observed candidates with list-wise targets derived from a teacher-induced retrieval distribution over the full item catalog. The teacher uses relevance patterns learned from existing supervision to identify promising candidates beyond those observed for each request. Crucially, broader candidate coverage alone does not teach a greedy decoder how to recover these candidates within one trajectory. The supervision must also organize them into an ordered list, enabling the student to learn successive retrieval decisions conditioned on preceding items. We instantiate this idea as . Its turns offline item-level beam-search outputs into list-wise distillation targets, transferring both candidate coverage and ordering into a single autoregressive trajectory. Rather than requiring online search to recover candidates from multiple branches, SLIDE trains the student to generate the full Top-$K$ list in a single greedy pass. This decouples multi-paths search from online serving~, allowing teacher capacity to scale without changing the deployed model or its online serving requirements. This work presents an approach to improving list-wise GR under single greedy decoding. Generative retrieval and recommendation formulate item retrieval as sequence generation~, a paradigm increasingly adopted in industrial search, advertising, and recommendation systems~. Many existing methods combine point-wise training with item-level beam search to retrieve candidate lists, sometimes followed by reranking~. Item-level beam search retrieves candidates along separate SID paths under a shared context rather than making sequential item-generation decisions conditioned on preceding items. Consequently, it does not explicitly model cross-item dependencies. RL methods such as OneSearch-V2~ and UniVA~ also optimize at the item level: each rollout generates one item and receives item-level rewards, limiting direct optimization of list-level objectives. UniSearch~ identifies point-wise beam generation as a limitation for result diversity and proposes list-wise generation as a direction for future work. Another line of work uses sessions or pages as the unit of generation to capture cross-item dependencies~. OneRec-V1~ models cross-video dependencies through session-wise learning and a session-level reward model. EGA-V2~ learns high-value ad sequences from logged impressions and uses a reward model to select among beam-search candidates. GenRec~ uses page-wise supervision during training but returns to item-level beam search at inference, separating list-wise training from item-wise retrieval. GenPage~ serializes structured multi-row pages and explores page-level RL. These works demonstrate the value of list-wise generation for cross-item modeling and list-level optimization. However, most still rely on the exploratory capacity of beam search at inference. SLIDE instead enable the model to generate the full list in a single greedy pass. Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from a teacher to a student, often reducing serving costs while retaining prediction quality~. Beyond model capabilities, distillation can also transfer the benefits of computationally expensive decoding. DeGRe~ applies offline exploration and distillation to generative reranking over a given candidate set. It trains an online generator with sequence-level hard targets and step-wise soft supervision, enabling list generation through single greedy decoding. Whereas DeGRe preserves the list-wise output structure of the reranking task, SLIDE converts item-level beam-search outputs into list-wise generation targets for retrieval. The teacher retrieves candidates through item-level beam search, and their SIDs are concatenated in beam order into a single distillation target. This transforms candidates retrieved along separate search paths into consecutive item-generation decisions within one autoregressive trajectory. We describe efficiency-oriented RL for two generative retrieval paradigms: with item-level beam search and with single-pass greedy decoding. Each item is represented by a triplet of Semantic IDs . In item-level retrieval, each response represents one item, whereas in list-level generation, each response represents an ordered list produced within a single autoregressive trajectory. Both settings use the efficiency reward \[ r = , \] provided by an efficiency scoring service. List-level generation additionally applies a repetition penalty. For each input, we sample $n$ responses during rollout, each containing the SID triplet of one item. These triplets are mapped to their corresponding items and scored by the efficiency service. We normalize the rewards within the sampled group to obtain relative advantages, assign each advantage to its corresponding response, and update the policy using standard GRPO. Because each response contains a single item, response-level and item-level rewards have the same granularity. Candidates compete within the sampled group for higher relative advantages rather than being jointly optimized as a complete list. The training candidate group is formed through sampling, whereas the deployed Top-$K$ list is assembled through item-level beam search. For each input, we sample $n$ responses during rollout, each containing an ordered list whose length may vary. We extract the SID triplets from all sampled responses and deduplicate them to form a candidate set for that input. Each unique triplet is then mapped to its corresponding item and assigned an efficiency score. Within this candidate set, items with valid efficiency scores receive Z-score-normalized advantages: \[ A = { + }, \] where $s$ denotes an item represented by a SID triplet, $ $ and $ $ are the mean and standard deviation of valid rewards in the candidate set, and $ $ ensures numerical stability. We assign $A $ to the tokens encoding item $s$, rather than sharing one advantage across the entire response. These token-level advantages are used in a GRPO-based policy update, without further response-level normalization of the efficiency signal. Items above and below the candidate-set mean can therefore receive positive and negative advantages within the same response. This enables fine-grained credit assignment across list positions while retaining the full list as a single rollout. We additionally apply a repetition penalty to discourage duplicate items and prevent repeated generation of high-reward items. For each repeated occurrence, the tokens encoding the item receive no efficiency advantage, and the final token receives a negative advantage.
