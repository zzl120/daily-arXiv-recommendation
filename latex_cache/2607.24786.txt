Answer: Justification: Abstract and Introduction. Guidelines: The answer means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims made in the paper. The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A or answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers. The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings. It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals are not attained by the paper. { Limitations} Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors? Answer: Justification: Conclusion and limitations. Guidelines: The answer means that the paper has no limitation while the answer means that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper. The authors are encouraged to create a separate ``Limitations'' section in their paper. The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions . The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be. The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated. The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon. The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size. If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness. While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren't acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations. { Theory assumptions and proofs} Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and a complete proof? Answer: Justification: No theory. Guidelines: The answer means that the paper does not include theoretical results. The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if they appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short proof sketch to provide intuition. Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented by formal proofs provided in appendix or supplemental material. If the contribution is a dataset and or model, the authors should describe the steps taken to make their results reproducible or verifiable. Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model , releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed. We formulate this task as the of a per-frame spatial probability map over the visual tokens of $x_t$ to isolate the sounding sources. We build on top of a large-scale audio-visual retrieval model composed of three transformer-based encoders: an audio encoder, an image frame encoder, and a video encoder. PE-AV is a particularly strong testbed for our question because it combines rich pretrained audio-visual semantics with a clear architectural bottleneck: dense frame-level visual tokens are ultimately compressed into a single token before temporal modeling. Our goal is to show that this retrieval architecture can be adapted into an audio source localizer through a principled modification of that interface. Let $c_t^{L} ^{F}$ denote the classification token from the final layer $L$ of the frame encoder. The PE-AV architecture constructs a video-level representation by processing the sequence of frame-level CLS tokens $\{c_t^{L}\}_{t=1}^{T}$ through the video encoder. Consequently, downstream temporal modeling operates exclusively on a single aggregated token per frame. While this design is effective for global retrieval tasks, it introduces an inherent information bottleneck for AV-SSL, as the video encoder is disconnected from the localized visual features necessary for precise spatial grounding. Consistent with the observations in , we posit that spatial detail is progressively lost across the upper layers of the frame encoder, so that the final representation no longer retains the local information required for precise grounding. This is also reflected by a simple gradient-based attribution probe applied to the original PE-AV retrieval pathway: the norm of the gradient of the audio--visual similarity with respect to the image tokens yields diffuse, poorly interpretable heatmaps that do not isolate the sounding region. Thus, we hypothesize that intermediate layers preserve richer spatial features necessary for localization. Consequently, we propose to perform sound source localization using representations from an intermediate layer $ $ of the frame encoder. Let $V^{ }_t ^{HW F}$ denote the spatial tokens at layer $ $ for a given frame at time $t$. Specifically, we utilize the $ $-th layer . Within this framework, the localization task relies on the audio representation at the corresponding time step, $a_t ^{F'}$, in conjunction with $V_{ }^t$ to estimate a segmentation mask $S_t ^{HW}$ that identifies the active sound sources at time $t$. After temporal alignment and projection to the visual space, we denote the corresponding frame-aligned audio token by $a_t ^{F}$. Operating at intermediate layers introduces a non-trivial challenge, as the spatial tokens $V^{ }_t$ and audio tokens $a_t$ reside in distinct latent spaces, resulting in representational misalignment. This raises a fundamental architectural question: To address this, we use the audio token to query the intermediate visual tokens and progressively pool them into a single audio-informed token that replaces the original frame token, while focusing on regions corresponding to sounding objects. To this aim, we introduce AiSP, a cross-modal pooling module that outputs this replacement token while exposing attention maps for localization. This approach ensures that the localized spatial representations are projected into a space compatible with the pretrained audio-visual embedding space, thereby facilitating effective contrastive alignment with the audio tokens. The cross-modal localization model is mainly trained using a constrative loss. However, using audio tokens to construct this replacement frame token induces a possible leakage issue, as the audio information to be contrasted within the contrastive loss could be transmitted directly. This induces the need of a specific modification of the constrastive loss . We call the proposed method . We now describe LAIP and its AiSP module. The proposed method, , is illustrated in Figure~. Rather than attaching a separate localization head on top of frozen PE-AV features, LAIP modifies the interface between the frame encoder and the video encoder. Its core building block is the AiSP module. The design is governed by two primary functional requirements: progressively pooling the intermediate visual tokens into an audio-informed token $_t^L$ that can replace $c_t^L$ as input to the video encoder, and enabling unsupervised segmentation based on the audio token at the corresponding time step. Addressing these objectives jointly brings a key advantage as a synergy emerges between the construction of the audio-informed token $_t^L$ and sound source localization. On the one hand, connecting the pooled token to the video encoder provides a rich supervision signal, allowing the LAIP pipeline to learn a hierarchical representation of each frame, which enables more accurate localization. On the other hand, audio-based segmentation allows us to down-weight image regions corresponding to non-sounding regions and therefore gradually improve our hierarchical representation towards relevant objects in the scene. In this way, it can benefit from the rich temporal modeling capacity of the video encoder and thus achieve rich spatio-temporal understanding. Formally, given spatial visual tokens from frame $t$, LAIP must pool them into a token $_t^L$ compatible with the original PE-AV video encoder. This compatibility constraint acts as a form of : even without an explicit distillation loss, it allows us to insert audio-guided spatial pooling before the pretrained temporal stack while still exploiting the semantics and temporal modeling already encoded in that stack. The module is therefore encouraged to extract localized spatial information while producing per-frame embeddings that the frozen retrieval pipeline can consume directly. While this compatibility objective could in principle be satisfied by a standard Transformer or ConvNet, the requirement for precise spatial localization imposes specific structural constraints on the architecture. A straightforward approach to spatial grounding could involve a single cross-attention layer between the audio representation and the full set of spatial visual tokens. However, large-scale vision transformers are known to produce , outlier tokens with disproportionately high norms and overly globalized semantics , which tend to dominate cross-attention weights and degrade spatial precision. To circumvent this, the LAIP architecture adopts a hierarchical design inspired by the classic convolution-pooling paradigm . We employ a series of blocks that progressively reduce the spatial resolution of the visual tokens until they collapse into a single vector, serving as the replacement classification token $_t^L$. We opt for a convolutional backbone rather than a transformer-based architecture because convolutions inherently preserve local spatial structure, a property shown to be beneficial for cross-modal grounding tasks . Departing from traditional ConvNets, however, LAIP introduces a novel mechanism through AiSP. Drawing inspiration from the hierarchical aggregation found in Swin Transformers , our module performs progressive spatial reduction guided by the audio token via an attention-based bottleneck. This design ensures that the pooling process remains spatially grounded: the audio signal selectively attends to tokens corresponding to active sounding regions rather than to registers or tokens that have already aggregated global context. Consequently, the resulting attention maps are directly interpretable as high-fidelity localization masks. A byproduct of this hierarchical design is the generation of multi-scale attention maps across successive pooling layers, providing a progressive refinement of the spatial grounding. The initial layers preserve the high-resolution granularity necessary for pinpointing fine-grained spatial details, whereas the deeper stages yield smoother, more semantically coherent representations. This multi-layer progression inherently enhances robustness: as features are aggregated through the stack, the influence of potential register tokens is effectively marginalized. To produce the final result, these multi-resolution localization maps are fused via upsampling to the resolution of $V^{ }_t$ and averaging. This results in a prediction that benefits from both local precision and global contextual stability. Crucially, the audio and image-frame encoders operate at different temporal resolutions. To facilitate per-frame spatial grounding within LAIP, we align the audio stream with the video frame rate using a lightweight local aggregator . This procedure yields a synchronized audio embedding $a_t$ for each visual frame $x_t$. Each frame-aligned audio token subsequently serves as a query to the spatial visual tokens of its respective frame, enabling the cross-modal interaction required for localization. Audio-visual sound source localization aims to identify, in each video frame, the spatial regions of active sound~. As a fundamental problem in audio-visual understanding, it underpins applications in video editing, robotics, and multimodal retrieval. Yet, it is inherently challenging: in real-world scenes, visual content is often dense and cluttered, while sound sources are typically sparse. Consequently, models must pinpoint the regions responsible for the audio, such as , or . This sparsity makes the task technically difficult, as it requires precise spatial disambiguation under weak supervision , and dense spatial annotations are rarely available. Obtaining such annotations for videos is burdensome: annotators must delineate sounding regions frame by frame across long clips. In parallel, large-scale contrastive pretraining has produced strong multimodal retrieval models across image--text~, image--any-modality~, and audio--visual settings~ by aligning global embeddings of those modalities over large dataset. Retrieval models are optimized for global alignment---matching audio to an entire frame or video---rather than for local matching of the specific region that emits the sound. It results in frame and video features with poor spatiality. In principle, dense spatial annotations could recover locality through fine-tuning, but collecting them is costly at scale. To train under this constraint, we adopt a SigLIP-style contrastive objective inspired by FLAIR~, which aligns the pooled video representation with its corresponding audio embedding while treating mismatched audio-conditioned views as negatives. Audio-visual sound source localization aims to identify the visual region responsible for a sound. Weakly supervised methods show that synchronized video--audio pairs already provide a useful grounding signal, while also exposing recurring failures such as attending to correlated but silent objects or mishandling off-screen sources . The task has since expanded to segmentation-oriented and open-vocabulary settings . EZ-VSL remains a key reference point, formulating localization as multiple-instance learning. Later works revisited protocols , reduced false negatives , or strengthened semantic alignment . However, most methods remain frame-centric, aligning one audio representation with one image. This is restrictive for realistic videos, where sources move, appear off-screen, or switch across objects. AVATAR addresses this gap with a video-centric benchmark and TAVLO, which combines spatial and temporal attention under a temporal extension of the EZ-VSL objective. Our work is complementary to these localization-specific architectures. Instead of preserving spatial outputs throughout the model, we start from a retrieval encoder that compresses each frame or clip into pooled tokens before alignment, and ask whether localization can be recovered from this interface. PE-AV~ provides a concrete instance of this setting. Recent multimodal representation learning follows a scaling paradigm in which large encoders are trained on massive paired datasets to align modalities in a shared embedding space. CLIP established this recipe for image--text learning, SigLIP~ replaced the softmax contrastive loss with a pairwise sigmoid loss, ImageBind~ extended the shared-embedding perspective to additional modalities, and PE-AV~ brought the same philosophy to joint audio, video, and text learning using roughly 100 million curated audio--video pairs. However, these models are optimized for global retrieval: they align modalities through pooled representations rather than dense spatial correspondences, and therefore do not directly expose the spatial outputs required for sound source localization. FLAIR~ addresses an analogous problem in image--text learning by using the text embedding to pool local visual tokens and recover text-conditioned image features from a globally trained model. Our setting is the audio-visual analogue: can audio pool local visual tokens so that a retrieval model becomes spatially informative? We evaluate LAIP on three benchmarks. In contrast, our AiSP maps are explicitly produced by using the audio token to query dense visual tokens, which makes them much more spatially specific.
