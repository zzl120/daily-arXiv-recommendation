News recommender systems~ help users navigate this landscape by providing personalized article suggestions. Modern approaches are dominated by deep learning methods that learn dense representations for users and news, achieving state-of-the-art performance on large-scale benchmarks~. Learned sparse retrieval has emerged as an alternative to dense retrieval. By using pre-trained language models to predict term importance and expand documents with latent terms, LSR produces sparse, lexical representations. Much like classical lexical models such as BM25, these representations can be indexed and retrieved with inverted-index machinery~, with every dimension mapping directly to a vocabulary term. In ad hoc retrieval, LSR methods have demonstrated strong in-domain and out-of-domain effectiveness~. Despite this success, the application of LSR to the news domain remains unexplored. The primary existing sparse recommender, STAR~, represents a user by concatenating their interaction history into a single query string. For news recommendation, this formulation is insufficient due to two critical bottlenecks. First, concatenation collapses a user's distinct reading interests into one undifferentiated query, leaving the model unable to weigh some articles as more relevant than others. Second, news articles are long-form text, so concatenating several of them quickly exceeds the context window of the underlying language model, forcing truncation that severely degrades recommendation quality. Both bottlenecks stem from the same root cause: STAR has no dedicated mechanism for representing a user from a collection of articles, and instead flattens the history into one document. We propose , the first learned sparse recommender designed for the news domain. Our central contribution is a user encoder that operates over independently encoded articles. Rather than flattening a user's history into a single string, LSNR encodes each article on its own and aggregates the resulting per-article sparse vectors with a learned, attention-based user encoder, fused with a dense-projected expansion at the user level. This design directly resolves both bottlenecks. It represents the user at the granularity of individual articles, learning which interactions matter rather than blending them together, and by encoding articles independently, it eliminates the context-window bottleneck. Because articles are encoded independently of the user, their sparse representations can be precomputed offline, leaving only user-side aggregation at inference time. On the MIND and EBNeRD benchmarks, LSNR significantly outperforms the leading sparse baseline STAR, improving NDCG@10 by 11\ This short-history advantage is especially consequential for news, where a large fraction of served users are new, infrequent, or sparsely tracked, so the regime in which LSNR overtakes dense models is one that news platforms encounter routinely. Beyond effectiveness, the sparse formulation gives LSNR a compact footprint: it serves from an index that is an order of magnitude smaller than an uncompressed dense baseline. Because every dimension of the user vector maps to a vocabulary term, the vector can also be inspected directly. We introduce LSNR, the first learned sparse news recommender. We diagnose why STAR, the existing learned sparse recommender, fails to transfer to the news domain, and resolve it by replacing history concatenation with independent article encoding and a user encoder that aggregates the per-article representations with learned attention. We show that LSNR significantly outperforms the STAR baseline on MIND and EBNeRD, maintaining this advantage even in short-history settings where truncation is absent. We show that LSNR is competitive with dense models in the full-history setting, on par with the strongest dense recommenders on MIND and trailing them modestly on EBNeRD, while surpassing every dense baseline in short-history scenarios, the regime most characteristic of news. An ablation of the user encoder shows that learned attention-based aggregation outperforms simpler pooling strategies, and that both pathways contribute to performance. We release our code to facilitate reproducibility.} Two areas are particularly relevant to the work in this paper: news recommendation and learned sparse representations. News recommendation poses unique challenges. Article content is short-lived, rapidly updated, and highly time-sensitive. This high turnover makes standard ID-based collaborative filtering approaches infeasible~. Sequential recommenders such as SASRec~ and its successors~ are effective in domains with stable catalogs, but are generally considered suboptimal for news due to the rapid obsolescence of articles~. Even hybrid methods that combine sequential and content-based features are rarely applied to the news domain~. Consequently, content-based neural news recommendation prevails. The standard architecture is a dual encoder that uses dense representations to model users and news~. Early approaches relied on sparse lexical features~, while pretrained embeddings shifted the focus to dense text encoders~, sometimes enhanced with auxiliary signals~. Large language models have also been explored~, but their inference latency currently makes them infeasible for the retrieval stage focused on in this work~. On the user side, dense dual encoders typically aggregate the history with attention, weighting interactions by their estimated importance. NRMS~, for instance, builds the user representation with multi-head self-attention over clicked articles. LSNR adopts a similar attention-based aggregation, but instead, the user representation is constructed in a sparse lexical space rather than a dense one. This yields a compact user representation whose dimensions correspond to vocabulary terms, whose properties we discuss next. Learned sparse representations are commonly used in ad hoc retrieval to bridge the gap between exact lexical matching and semantic dense retrieval~. State-of-the-art LSR methods, e.g., SPLADE~, use masked language modeling heads to predict term importance and to expand documents with relevant latent terms~. These methods allow for flexible efficiency and effectiveness trade-offs via techniques such as block-max pruning and quantized inverted indices~. Because every dimension corresponds to a vocabulary term, these representations are often described as interpretable, though recent analyses show that SPLADE can route ranking signals through terms only loosely related to the input, or even through irrelevant tokens~. News recommendation differs fundamentally from ad hoc retrieval. The input is not a text string but a history of previously read articles, from which a user representation must be constructed. The pioneering learned sparse recommender, STAR~, adapts LSR by concatenating the interaction history into a single text string. As discussed in Section~, this is ill-suited to news: it collapses a user's distinct interests into one undifferentiated representation and quickly exceeds the language model's context window, forcing quality-degrading truncation. We instead encode each article independently and aggregate the resulting sparse representations with a learned user encoder, representing the user at the granularity of individual articles rather than as a single concatenated string. We have introduced learned sparse news recommendations , a framework that adapts learned sparse retrieval to the long-form text and user histories of the news domain. By encoding each article independently and aggregating them via a learned, attention-based user encoder, LSNR eliminates the context-window bottleneck and the interest collapse that concatenation induces in prior learned sparse methods such as STAR. Our evaluation on the MIND and EBNeRD benchmarks yields three findings. First, LSNR significantly outperforms both BM25 and STAR by representing users at the granularity of individual articles. Second, this advantage persists in short-history settings where STAR processes the same input without truncation, showing that LSNR's gains stem from improved per-article learning rather than from avoiding truncation. Third, LSNR performs competitively with state-of-the-art dense recommenders in standard settings and significantly outperforms them when user history is short, while serving from an index an order of magnitude smaller than the uncompressed dense baseline on both datasets. We identify several limitations of our work. First, LSNR's advantage over dense models is concentrated in the short-history setting: in the full-history setting, it matches the strongest dense recommenders on MIND but trails them by 2 to 3\ Beyond textual signals, auxiliary features such as article categories or user demographics could further sharpen the sparse user vector. Because each dimension of the user vector maps to a vocabulary term, the representation could in principle support term-level inspection or editing of user interests. To validate the effectiveness of LSNR, we compare it against both sparse and dense content-based recommender systems. We first test our hypothesis that learned, attention-based aggregation is superior to concatenation by comparing against and ~, the state-of-the-art learned sparse recommender. We adapt STAR for the news domain by concatenating the most recent interactions, as we found recency-based selection more effective than the original IDF-based selection. We concatenate historical interactions into STAR's input until the 512-token context window is filled. For the user-encoder ablation, we additionally implement several aggregation variants and pathway configurations in place of our default hybrid user encoder, described in Section~. We benchmark LSNR against three leading dense architectures, ~, ~, and ~, each implemented with a pre-trained language model backbone to align with modern standards~. Recent work has shown that BERT-based variants of these models perform on par with much larger LLM-based encoders~, justifying our use of BERT-scale backbones. LSNR, STAR, and NRMS use only article texts. NAML and LSTUR additionally include article categories and user IDs. We exclude generative LLMs due to their prohibitive inference costs at the retrieval stage~. We initialize English models with the checkpoint and Danish models with the checkpoint. For STAR on MIND, we use the checkpoint to match the original implementation. As no Danish SPLADE checkpoint is available, we train STAR on EBNeRD from the same backbone as LSNR, so both sparse models start from identical initialization. On MIND, this initialization asymmetry favors STAR, which begins from a retrieval-pretrained checkpoint while LSNR begins from vanilla BERT. The additional transformer layer in the news encoder and the contextualization layer in the user encoder each use a single layer with 16 attention heads. Following~ in freezing the lower backbone layers to balance performance and efficiency, we freeze the first 9 of the 12 backbone layers across all methods. We optimize using AdamW on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, grid-searching the learning rate over $\{10^{-6}, 10^{-5}, 10^{-4}\}$ with early stopping on validation NDCG@10. We tune the negative sampling strategy per model to give each its strongest configuration. For dense models, we sample 4 negatives from the candidate pool. Increasing this yielded no improvement, consistent with prior work~. For sparse models , we sample 16 negatives, as we found learned sparse models benefit from a larger negative pool. We structure our evaluation in five stages, each targeting a distinct question. We report NDCG@10, NDCG@5, and MRR, and assess statistical significance using a two-tailed paired t-test over the per-user metric distributions, where $^ $ denotes a significant improvement over the second-best method . Following the standard MIND and EBNeRD protocol, all NDCG and MRR metrics throughout the paper are computed by ranking the candidate articles recorded in each impression log, treating clicked articles as positives and co-displayed non-clicked articles as negatives, and averaging over impressions. The full-catalog retrieval used for the efficiency measurements in Section~ applies only to index footprint and latency, not to accuracy. We first assess recommendation performance on the full test set against the sparse baselines BM25 and STAR. This stage tests our core hypothesis that learned aggregation over independently encoded articles outperforms STAR's concatenation. A natural concern is that LSNR may outperform STAR only because STAR is forced to truncate long histories. To isolate the effect of our aggregation mechanism from the avoidance of truncation, we simulate short-history users by training on the complete training set but truncating test-set histories to the $N$ most recent interactions, $N \{1, , 5\}$. At small $N$, and especially at $N=1$, STAR processes the exact same input as LSNR with no truncation, so any remaining gap is attributable to how each model represents the user rather than to lost input. We benchmark LSNR against the state-of-the-art dense recommenders NRMS, NAML, and LSTUR, on both the full dataset and the short-history setting, to assess whether learned sparse representations can match dense embeddings. We report the index footprint of LSNR against the dense NRMS baseline. Because LSNR user vectors are sparse, we also examine the effect of retrieval-time pruning: we sweep a threshold $K \{5, , 100\}$ that retains only the top-$K$ highest-scoring terms of each user vector, and trace recommendation accuracy against user-vector sparsity . We report user-vector and document sparsity and on-disk index size, comparing LSNR against the uncompressed dense NRMS baseline on identical hardware, and report end-to-end retrieval latency. Finally, to justify the design choices in the user encoder, we run two complementary ablations. The first isolates the contribution of each pathway in the hybrid construction , training variants that disable $_{}$, disable $_{}$, or include both. The second varies the aggregation function within $_{}$, comparing our additive attention against four alternatives: attention with positional encodings, mean pooling, max pooling, and a recency-weighted temporal decay. A comparison against the parameter-free pooling variants isolates the contribution of learned weighting, while the positional-encoding and temporal-decay variants test whether interaction order carries signal beyond article content.
