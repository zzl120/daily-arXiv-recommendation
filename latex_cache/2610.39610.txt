Large language models have rapidly become a powerful tool for relevance ranking, where a single model is prompted with a query alongside an ordered list of candidate passages and asked to directly emit the permutation that best reflects their relevance~. This listwise formulation lets the model reason jointly over all candidates and exploit cross-passage comparisons that pointwise and pairwise scorers cannot capture, and it has proven so effective that the capability of proprietary models has been distilled into compact open-source rankers that now serve as strong and reproducible baselines~. Driven by these successes, listwise ranking with large language models has quickly become a central paradigm in modern retrieval pipelines. However, in web search and retrieval-augmented applications, the response must also be produced within an inference budget. A conventional listwise reranker serializes the candidate passages into the ranking prompt. Its input grows with both the number and length of passages, even when the entire list fits inside the model's context window~. A long candidate list, or passages that are individually long, quickly inflates inference latency, degrading both ranking quality and responsiveness. As shown in , the prevailing remedy is to process the list through a sliding window that reranks overlapping blocks and propagates the ordering across passes~, but this comes at the cost of repeatedly re-encoding overlapping content. These costs motivate reducing the information consumed by the ranking stage, while explicitly accounting for any work needed to prepare that information. {figures/fig1.pdf} A promising solution is to compress each passage before ranking, for example, extracting a summary or embedding the passage~. Besides, a family of soft-compression methods encodes long inputs into a handful of trainable gist tokens that retain the conditioning effect of the original text while drastically shortening the sequence~. We argue that such compression is a natural fit for improving inference efficiency in ranking tasks, as it could replace each verbatim passage with a compact set of gist tokens. Prior work has in fact measured gist and KV-level compression on reranking and reported a large gap to full attention~, but has treated that gap as an intrinsic cost of compression. We argue it is instead a feature of the compression objective: relevance judgment hinges on fine-grained, query-conditioned distinctions among closely competing candidates, whereas objectives optimised for reconstruction or generative conditioning are never asked to preserve those distinctions. In ranking, the compressed states must expose relative preference across candidates, not merely preserve a useful account of each passage. Bridging this gap therefore requires adapting the gist representation so that it preserves the discriminative signals on which ranking depends. We propose , which equips a large language model with compressed gist tokes passage representations for efficiency listwise ranking through a three-stage training procedure. In the first stage, we learn faithful gist representations: fixed-length documents are followed by a set of gist tokens that are in turn concatenated with the query, with the query trailing the gist tokens. The model is trained to predict the subsequent tokens conditioned solely on the gist, while granting the gist tokens richer bidirectional access to their source context. In the second stage, with the gist tokens visible, but the original documents hidden, we train these representations on real-world search engine interaction data with pairwise objectives, instilling the discriminative capability required to distinguish competing candidates for the purpose of ranking. In the third stage, we continue training the model directly on labeled ranking data, so that it learns to leverage the gist tokens to produce the listwise permutation both efficiently and accurately. We evaluate both relevance and inference cost rather than treating a shorter prompt as proof of a faster complete pipeline. In this section, we position ~at the intersection of listwise ranking and soft context compression, focusing on how discriminatively trained gist tokens can improve ranking efficiency while preserving effectiveness. Ranking a candidate set as a whole rather than scoring documents in isolation has long been recognized as a principled formulation, and the earliest learning-to-rank approaches directly optimized list-level objectives that account for the relative ordering among all candidates~. With the advent of pretrained language models, this paradigm was reframed as estimating relevance from deep contextualized interactions between a query and each document, first through cross-encoders that score pairs pointwise and then through sequence-to-sequence formulations~. The emergence of large language models pushed this further by feeding an entire ordered list of passages into a single prompt and instructing the model to emit a permutation, which proved remarkably effective even in zero-shot settings and was subsequently distilled into smaller open models for practical deployment~. Subsequent methods have taken both accuracy and efficiency into account—for instance, sliding-window-based approaches, as well as those utilizing reinforcement learning or collaborative ranking~. In real-world retrieval, deploying listwise rankers requires both strong ranking performance and manageable inference costs. Yet existing token-level listwise methods have largely overlooked gist tokens trained with a discriminative ranking objective as a way to compress passage representations while retaining ranking-critical information. Using soft context compression for model inputs has become an effective way to reduce the computational overhead of model inference. A line of work has sought to shorten the context consumed by a language model by encoding long inputs into a handful of trainable soft tokens, where the seminal approach learns dedicated gist tokens that compress an entire instruction into a compact prefix while preserving its conditioning effect on subsequent generation~. Building on this idea, later efforts scaled the compression to arbitrary contexts by training autoencoding objectives that reconstruct the original text from a few memory slots, or by recursively summarizing long documents into accumulated soft prompts that extend the effective context length~. More recent studies have pushed the compression ratio to extreme regimes, and integrated such condensed representations into retrieval-augmented pipelines to reduce the cost of conditioning on retrieved evidence~. Furthermore, research has already extended pre-sorting compression to the token level in an effort to improve efficiency. However, these works are primarily optimized for tasks such as representation compression or generative guidance, while capabilities regarding discriminative comparison tasks have been overlooked. There has no research repurposed gist-style soft tokens as compact passage representations for ranking.
