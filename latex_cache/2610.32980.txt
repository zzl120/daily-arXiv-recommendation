Generative retrieval represents each document or item by a short sequence of discrete tokens and retrieves it by decoding this sequence~. TIGER~ brought this idea to sequential recommendation with SIDs from residual quantization~. Later work improved item tokenization~, combined generative and dense retrieval~, adapted pretrained LLMs to SID-based recommendation~, and scaled generative recommenders to industrial traffic~. These works develop item representations and recommendation models; we focus on the computation repeated during candidate decoding. Full-backbone SID recommenders, whether they read the history as a flat sequence of SID code tokens or as one fused token per item~, generate the codes either in the backbone or, as in encoder--decoder models such as TIGER and OneRec, in a decoder of comparable or larger size that cross-attends to the encoded history at every step. OneRec-V2 drops the encoder and lets a large decoder read the context through shared keys and values~, cutting the cost of reading; writing, which we target, still runs through the large model. As generative recommenders grow, adopt large decoder-only LLMs, and reach industrial scale, reducing repeated decoding work becomes important for meeting latency budgets. The cost of beam search is a recognized obstacle to deployment~. Serving optimizations include early sorting termination, valid-item filtering and overlap between computation and data transfers~. Speculative decoding verifies a small model's drafts with the backbone~; NEZHA uses an autoregressive head over per-level backbone states and filters invalid SIDs afterwards~. RPG generates unordered codes in parallel and searches a graph~, generates order-agnostic identifiers~, and LLaDA-Rec uses discrete diffusion~. instead separates reading from writing: it runs the entire autoregressive beam search in a light decoder conditioned on a fixed-size, cacheable next-item concept produced by the heavy backbone. Wider beams and deeper SIDs increase decoder computation without further backbone calls. Unlike the parallel and diffusion-based methods above, retains left-to-right autoregressive decoding with trie constraints, ensuring that every retained prefix extends to a catalog item. Recommendation shapes how people find products, films and music on the Web, and every request begins with candidate retrieval: selecting a few hundred to a few thousand items from a large catalog within tens of milliseconds. Generative retrieval casts this stage as sequence generation~. Each item receives a semantic ID , a short tuple of discrete codes derived from its content; SIDs let similar items share code prefixes and keep the output vocabulary far smaller than the catalog. A sequence model reads the user's history and generates SIDs code by code using beam search constrained by a trie of valid SIDs. The SIDs retained at the final step identify the candidate items. Generative recommenders inherit the architectures and scaling practices of language models~, already serve industrial traffic~ and increasingly build on pretrained LLMs with billions of parameters~. SID-based retrieval has two sources of inference cost: encoding the history and decoding candidates. In a flat representation , a history of $T$ items with $K$ codes each occupies $TK$ tokens. Fusing each item's code embeddings into one token reduces the input to $T$ tokens. Both models encode the history once during , but use the full backbone for autoregressive decoding. Beam search of width $B$ over $K$-level SIDs requires $O $ backbone decode tokens per user, each attending to the cached history. In our measurements at beam width 100, beam search takes up to 91\ This cost grows with the SID depth, the candidate budget and the backbone size, so input compression alone does not remove this decoding bottleneck. Our starting point is to separate modeling the user's preferences from spelling out candidate SIDs. Our experiments show that, given a compact next-item representation from the backbone, a much smaller decoder can generate these identifiers without sacrificing recommendation quality . Direct decoder access to the history gives no consistent gain on the low-repeat datasets, while a separate copy mechanism helps with repeat consumption . and use the backbone for both tasks, so SID generation costs $O $ full-backbone decode tokens per user. We propose , which makes this division explicit . The backbone reads the fused history once and emits a compact shared by every candidate in the beam. It delegates the entire trie-constrained beam search to a light KV-cached decoder conditioned only on this concept and the generated prefix. The SID codes and trie stay unchanged. The decoder can be trained with cross-entropy alone, and distillation from a full-backbone model improves its accuracy. For repeat-heavy domains such as music streaming, a light pointer over the history retrieves the exact items a user returns to. The light decoder returns final candidates without the backbone verification used in speculative decoding~, and unlike parallel or single-pass decoders~, it keeps every step autoregressive and trie-constrained. A concept summarizes what comes next, not which past items the user has seen. In domains such as music streaming, however, users often return to items they have consumed before~. Such a target is easy to point to but hard to generate: the model must know past item to repeat, and a fixed-size concept is not meant to carry the identities of a hundred earlier items.
