A major challenge in making these collections publicly accessible is enabling effective search and discovery. Many historical images contain incomplete, sparse, or no descriptive metadata. Consequently, machine learning~ methods are increasingly being used to generate or enrich metadata to improve the searchability of such collections. However, artificial intelligence~ -based systems can reproduce or amplify existing biases, particularly when applied to historical imagery. Factors such as differences between the data used to train the models and the historical images on which they are applied, degraded image quality, monochrome photographs, and historically biased or incomplete descriptions can reduce model performance and introduce bias into search and retrieval results. In this paper, we examine different forms of bias in historical image collections and review current methods to mitigate them. We investigate how these biases arise and how they influence AI-supported search and retrieval systems. Historical image collections are not neutral: the categories used to describe them, and the omissions of what was documented, reflect the priorities and power relations of the period and institutions that produced them~. These issues are particularly evident in colonial collections, which were shaped by unequal power relations and colonial perspectives. When such collections are made searchable, particularly where retrieval is supported with modern machine learning methods, these embedded assumptions are not simply preserved but risk being actively reproduced or reinforced, shaping how present-day users access and understand the past~. Bias and its mitigation have so far been predominantly studied for modern imagery, and existing mitigation techniques tend to work best for such images. Comparatively little research addresses bias and bias mitigation in historical imagery, particularly where descriptive metadata is sparse or absent~. In this paper, we address this gap, examining how bias arises in historical image collections and which mitigation techniques remain applicable. We ground this discussion in the case of colonial photograph archives, drawing throughout on the Visual Analytics for Images from Colonial Contexts~ project as a concrete instance of the challenges discussed here; though bias dimensions and mitigation gaps are not specific to this collection alone. The VABiKo collection comprises more than 50,000 photographs collected between 1887 and 1943 by the German Colonial Society and its successor organizations, and held today by the Universitätsbibliothek J.\,C.\ Senckenberg in Frankfurt am Main~. As illustration of the particularities that this material raises, these images are predominantly monochrome and, having been digitized from glass-plate negatives and prints by 1990s microfilming, may be physically degraded. They can also depict sensitive content, including violence, nudity, or displays of colonial power. We return to these particularities and their consequences for bias in detail in Section~. Bias itself is framed differently across disciplines. In the humanities, it is typically understood as prejudice or unfair treatment directed at a group based on attributes such as race or gender~. In machine learning, it instead denotes a property introduced at specific stages of the data-algorithm-user pipeline, assessed by evaluating system outputs. Effective mitigation, however, has been shown to depend on interdisciplinary collaboration between heritage professionals, subject-matter experts and data scientists~. We therefore adopt a socio-technical framing that treats these two perspectives as complementary. This paper makes two main contributions: a systematic mapping of bias categories from an established ML fairness taxonomy onto retrieval tasks relevant to colonial-context photography archives, grounded in the VaBiKo collection, and an analysis of standard mitigation techniques and their limitations for these biases. Section~ examines mitigation techniques for these dimensions and where their assumptions hold or break for colonial-context material. Section~ concludes with open problems for future work. Understanding bias in AI-supported retrieval systems requires drawing on two distinct bodies of work: research that defines and categorises bias as a concept, and research that proposes concrete techniques to detect and mitigate it. Section~ reviews how bias has been conceptualised both in the humanities and in machine learning. We outline which of these perspectives, and which pipelines stages are the most relevant to historical image collections. Section~ then surveys existing mitigation techniques developed primarily for contemporary imagery and datasets, providing the technical baseline against which we later assess applicability to the colonial-context material described in Section~. Bias in historical collections cannot be fully understood from a single disciplinary perspective; therefore, we draw on both the humanities and machine learning literature before deriving the dimensions most relevant to our own context as presented in Section~. Most historical collections contain some form of metadata, such as location, date, title, and description, often structured through ontologies or classification systems inherited from colonial perspectives. These metadata may reflect displays of power, propaganda, and other colonial ways of representing people, places, and events. From a humanities perspective, bias, therefore, begins with how historical material was originally produced, collected, selected, described, and curated over time~. In the case of historical imagery, this includes decisions about who had the authority to produce photographs, which people and activities were photographed, how subjects were represented, which images entered institutional collections, which materials survived, and which objects were later selected for digitisation~. These processes may create archival silences and uneven visibility, in which some communities, geographic regions, and historical perspectives are extensively documented, while others remain absent or difficult to access~. In the present VaBiKo collection , these issues are visible in metadata fields such as historical ethnic ascriptions, content keywords, depicted persons, and locations, which may preserve externally imposed colonial categories, contain incomplete identifications, or omit the perspectives of the people represented. When machine-learning methods are used to generate or enrich metadata, there is also a risk of digital violence if the same historical categories, terminologies, metadata structures, and ontologies are transferred into modern digital infrastructures. In this way, colonial perspectives embedded in historical collections may not only be preserved but also reproduced and reinforced through AI-based systems~. This humanities perspective therefore explains how bias becomes embedded in the source material and metadata before it is operationalised as data and processed by machine-learning systems~. The technical half of this discussion is grounded in the taxonomy proposed by Mehrabi et al.~, who organise sources of bias in AI systems into a data-algorithm-user feedback loop. biases arise from how training data is collected, sampled and aggregated. biases arise from model design and how outputs are presented to users. arises from how user behaviour and historically embedded assumptions feed back into future data. We adopt this structure, shown in Fig.~, as the organising frame for the remainder of this section and for the bias dimensions derived in Section~. The taxonomy was developed primarily with contemporary, well-documented datasets and demographic fairness problems in mind . It says comparatively little about the specific conditions in historical image archives: sparse or absent original metadata, image degradation, monochrome photography, and description authored under the assumptions of a colonial-era archivist rather than modern annotator. The following overview summarises bias mitigation techniques developed and evaluated in the general ML fairness literature. Section~ revisits each of these in light of the specific bias dimensions and data particularities identified in Section~, discussing how they are applicable to this context. One important step in developing a domain-specific model for metadata generation or classification is training it on historical images. However, historical image collections often contain substantial imbalances across attributes such as gender and race. Creating a more balanced training dataset can help ensure that these attributes are represented more equitably and reduce the risk that certain groups become less visible in model predictions or retrieval results~. Data augmentation can support this process by increasing the diversity of under-represented classes. Common techniques include image flipping, rotation, reflection, and transformations of colour, brightness, or contrast~. Such techniques are widely used in domains including medical imaging and speech recognition. Although data augmentation has also been applied to cultural heritage collections, its use to address demographic imbalance in historical black-and-white imagery remains relatively limited~. One such study, conducted on the Architectural Heritage Elements dataset and the Swedish World Culture Museum dataset, applied noise injection, image flipping, and colour jittering as augmentation techniques and showed an increase in the performance of the classifier models. Noise injection yielded the largest improvement, closely followed by colour jittering~. Adversarial debiasing is an in-processing technique in which a primary model learns the target task while an adversarial model attempts to predict a sensitive attribute from the primary model's learnt representation. The primary model is trained to jointly minimise its task loss and maximise the adversary's error, discouraging it from encoding or relying on that attribute~. For example, in a historical image collection, a model trained to retrieve or classify images depicting people could be paired with an adversary that attempts to predict gender from the model's learnt representations, discouraging the model from associating the general concept of "person" more strongly with men than with women~. The VABiKo project deals with the photographs held by the Universitätsbibliothek J.\,C.\ Senckenberg in Frankfurt am Main, collected between 1887 and 1943 by the Deutsche Kolonialgesellschaft and its successor organisations. The library also holds at least 20 further successor and other smaller collections from colonial contexts that arrived after 1995. Its main goal is to improve access to the DKG holdings for scholars and a broader public. Since usability is limited by incomplete metadata, the project pursues semi-automatic content indexing, using computer-vision methods to generate metadata that experts subsequently review and correct. These components are to be adapted to the particularities of this material rather than adopted off the shelf, and are developed and evaluated for images of Persons of Colour from sub-Saharan Africa and Oceania . Finally, the project aims to balance openness with sensitive handling of material that may depict violence or carry terms offensive to the communities and countries of origin. The photographs in question are predominantly glass-plate negatives and positive slides for presentations, alongside prints and photographic film, digitised through DFG-funded microfilming in the 1990s and held since 2021 in UB JCS's Visual Library system. Cataloguing was performed by librarians in a largely content-descriptive fashion, since resources to research photographer, subject, place, or date against their historical context were not available. The images may depict colonial violence, nudity, and displays of power, and reflect the hegemonic structures and racist stereotypes of their production context~; the metadata may also do so. Despite these limitations, the collection has been openly accessible since 1999 and is actively used in publications, exhibitions, and teaching, in Germany and in the countries and communities of origin. This paper examines bias in AI-supported retrieval for colonial-context photographic archives from humanities and machine-learning perspectives. Grounded in the VaBiKo collection, we map how historical, representational, metadata, algorithmic, ranking, and evaluation biases arise at different stages of the retrieval pipeline. The analysis shows that incomplete descriptions, uneven representation, rare entities, and black-and-white imagery can cause bias. It reviews the standard mitigation techniques that have been deployed in similar contexts to address these biases. What helps with dealing with bias is a series of small corrections at different points, and setting tasks, bias types, and mitigations out together shows which of those points a given project can reach. This paper's methodology works from use cases to the tasks they require, from those tasks to the documented bias types, and from those to mitigations reported in the literature. This progression is reusable -- other GLAM institutions can apply the same steps to their own collections. It turns a general concern about bias into a set of concrete decisions to be taken before a system is built, and it makes visible where no mitigation, or no suitable evaluation data, is available yet.
