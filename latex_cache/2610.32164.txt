However, the differences between users are not only about the underlying information need. Even when the retrieval intent and core semantic content are kept aligned, different users can still express the same need in different linguistic forms~. These expression differences can further affect lexical matching and query representation~, and downstream ranking~, so retrieval robustness depends not only on whether the system understands what users want, but also on whether it can stably handle how users express that need~. We call these linguistic differences between users, which do not depend on changes in underlying intent, as personalized expression differences. In other words, personalization involves not only what users want, but also how users express the same need. In this paper, we aim to explore the impact of personalized expression difference on information retrieval methods. Existing benchmarks, however, do not directly support this question. The personalization signals in existing personalized retrieval datasets are dominated by preference, interaction behavior, or intent differences~. The other real query datasets without personalization contain natural user expressions but typically lack stable user identities and sufficient historical expression evidence~. Motivated by this gap, this paper focuses on the following central question: To answer this question, we first need to answer which definitions can represent personalized expression. In real retrieval settings, user expression differences have already been observed as a stable phenomenon: the same user repeats similar writing habits and syntactic organizations across long-term historical texts, whereas different users differ identifiably in these respects~. Such differences appear both at the level of word form and in how the same semantic content is organized into a sentence. We therefore focus on two conceptually distinct and complementary dimensions of personalized expression: personalized writing errors and personalized syntactic expression. The former captures a user's long-term writing-error habits~, such as letter swaps, letter repetitions, or keyboard-adjacent substitutions, and different users can show stable differences in error occurrence frequency and in which error mechanisms they prefer. The latter captures the different syntactic organizations a user can adopt when expressing the same semantic content~: even when two queries share largely the same entities, attributes, and product requirements, they can still differ in phrase organization, modifier structure, and clause construction. On this basis, PQB covers three Amazon product domains---Musical Instruments, Video Games, and Baby Products---and evaluates personalized expression robustness on five retrieval models spanning lexical, learned sparse, dense bi-encoder, late-interaction, and hybrid architectures, together with two LLM-based ranking models. The main contributions of this paper are fivefold: We are the first to systematically investigate retrieval robustness to user-specific query expression differences derived from users' historical writing under a fixed information need, and we propose personalized syntactic expression and personalized writing errors as the two defining dimensions of personalized expression. We construct a personalized expression retrieval benchmark grounded in real users' historical expression evidence. Benchmark quality is validated along four dimensions---Personalized Syntactic Attribution, Personalized Writing-Error Consistency, Query Quality, and Evaluation Agreement---through LLM assessment and human evaluation. The benchmark covers three Amazon product domains and 6{,}516 query instances. The benchmark construction is grounded in users' real historical review texts. For personalized syntactic expression, PQB extracts structural rules such as dependency tuples and POS trigrams from historical expressions, learns representations that distinguish users' syntactic patterns through same-product supervised contrastive learning under a fixed product context, and fits a per-user low-rank-plus-diagonal Gaussian to capture the user's characteristic syntactic position and within-user variation. For personalized writing errors, PQB automatically extracts and aligns error annotations from historical reviews with GECToR and ERRANT, retains only errors that primarily alter surface form, and derives each user's error frequency and mechanism preference. We systematically evaluate personalized expression robustness on five representative retrievers spanning lexical, learned sparse, dense bi-encoder, late-interaction, and hybrid architectures, together with two LLM-based ranking models, analyzing how syntactic variation and writing-error variation each affect ranking, and show that overall retrieval effectiveness does not correspond in any simple way to robustness to expression variation. We further validate the effectiveness of personalized expression modeling through two experiments. For personalized syntactic expression, we compare the rank-one-plus-diagonal Gaussian with single-embedding representations, alternative Gaussian covariance structures, probabilistic distributions, and neighborhood-based methods. The results demonstrate our proposed rank-one-plus-diagonal Gaussian's stronger user attribution and discrimination, indicating its effectiveness in capturing user-specific syntactic patterns. For personalized writing errors, we compare user-specific error-mechanism sampling with global, uniform, character-random, and wrong-user profile baselines. Personalized sampling achieves better user attribution, demonstrating that historical writing-error profiles capture individual error-mechanism preferences and support personalized query construction in PQB. The work most closely related to PQB falls into three strands: query variation and controlled generation, the effects of writing errors on retrieval, and personalized retrieval. Prior work has shown that, even when the underlying information need stays essentially unchanged, query formulation and surface-form variation can still influence retrieval behavior; at the same time, personalized retrieval and synthetic query generation have provided the foundations for exploiting user histories and constructing controlled query variations. These strands, however, are typically studied separately---one focuses on query reformulation, another on retrieval noise, another on user preference, and another on synthetic variation---and relatively little work constructs controlled expression differences from individual users' historical language behavior while fixing the target product and the retrieval intent. PQB lies at the intersection of these strands and studies personalized expression differences as an independent evaluation variable for retrieval ranking. When the underlying information need stays essentially unchanged, query formulation can still affect retrieval effectiveness. Prior work has examined verbose queries, query variability, syntactic complexity, query clarity, and query-performance prediction~, and has found that structural differences between human-written and LLM-generated queries can also change retrieval behavior~. IR research also uses POS patterns, dependency structures, and syntactic organization to describe or model query structure~; meanwhile, authorship and writing-style research shows that structural linguistic patterns can reflect individual writing characteristics~. Together, these studies indicate that syntactic form is not irrelevant to retrieval behavior, yet existing retrieval studies usually treat it as query-level reformulation and rarely connect it to the long-term expression patterns of individual users. Synthetic query generation provides a complementary route to controlled query variation. Related methods have been widely used for training-data augmentation, semantic annotation, and retrieval benchmark construction~, and other studies have increased expression diversity through demographic attributes, personality traits, query rewriting, or term-level controls~. These methods can generate large numbers of query variants efficiently under semantic constraints, but their variation controls usually come from prompts, generic rewriting mechanisms, or predefined attributes, and therefore need not correspond to the expression behavior that individual users develop over time. PQB likewise uses controlled generation to fix product semantics, but its personalization signals and query-selection constraints are derived from historical user texts: historical expressions are used to estimate personalized syntactic distributions and further guide candidate-query selection. Writing errors and orthographic variation can also change retrieval matching behavior. Prior work has analyzed natural spelling variation, spelling correction, and regional conventions~, and has tested the robustness of neural retrieval models to misspellings through character-level, token-level, or adversarial perturbations~. These studies show that, even when the underlying information need is preserved, local surface-form changes can still cause retrieval degradation. Existing robustness evaluations, however, usually model writing errors as population-level or task-level perturbations, such as uniform corruption probability, fixed error mechanisms, or adversarial modifications, and rarely model long-term error frequency, mechanism preference, and context-conditioned patterns from the historical writing behavior of individual users. PQB therefore automatically identifies writing-error evidence from historical user texts, builds user-specific writing-error profiles, and applies constrained character-level editing with these profiles to the already determined personalized syntactic expression queries, thereby forming same-user personalized syntactic expression and personalized writing-error query pairs. This setting allows the effects of writing errors on retrieval outcomes to be evaluated while keeping the target product, the retrieval intent, and the original syntactic expression essentially unchanged. Personalized retrieval typically relies on user embeddings, interaction histories, session context, long-term behavior, and learning-to-rank signals to improve personalized relevance matching~; datasets such as JDsearch further provide real queries alongside user-interaction histories~. These methods use personalization signals to model the preferences, behaviors, or intents of different users, that is, they focus on what different users want and change relevance estimation or ranking accordingly. PQB addresses a different personalization dimension. Instead of using user expression history to change the relevance target, it learns personalized expression signals from user historical texts and asks whether ranking still changes when users express the same need differently. Specifically, PQB constructs same-product cross-user personalized syntactic expression query cohorts to compare the personalized syntactic expressions of different users on the same product, and constructs same-user personalized syntactic expression and personalized writing-error query pairs to evaluate personalized writing-error perturbations. Personalization in PQB therefore serves mainly as an expression variable under study rather than as an additional user signal for improving relevance matching. Overall, prior work has separately shown that query formulation, writing errors, user histories, and controlled query generation all affect retrieval modeling, but these directions are usually independent of one another. PQB further organizes them into a unified setting: it learns personalization signals from the historical language behavior of individual users and constructs cross-user syntactic variation and within-user writing-error variation separately, so as to systematically analyze how personalized expression differences affect retrieval outcomes. PQB's construction goal is to introduce personalized variation built from real users' historical expressions while holding retrieval intent and core product semantics fixed. A single embedding vector can represent a user's typical syntactic style but cannot describe the variation among the different syntactic expressions that the same user may employ. Prior studies have adopted Gaussian embeddings to represent variability beyond fixed vector representations~, while distribution-based personalized recommendation has used Gaussian means and covariances to characterize user preferences and relationships between latent dimensions~. Motivated by these approaches, PQB adopts a Gaussian distribution as a tractable approximation of each user's historical syntactic representations, rather than assuming that these representations strictly follow a Gaussian distribution. The mean characterizes the user's typical syntactic style, while the covariance captures within-user variation. This representation also provides a unified basis for Gaussian likelihood scoring and distance-based query selection in Section~3.3. The structure of the covariance determines how much of the within-user variation this distribution can retain: a full covariance matrix is the most general choice, but each user provides only a limited number of historical expressions, so estimating it directly introduces substantial estimation noise, whereas keeping only the dimension-wise variances is more stable yet discards the correlated variation across latent dimensions. The rank-one-plus-diagonal structure splits the difference: a low-rank component along a principal direction captures the correlated variation among dimensions, and diagonal residuals model the remaining dimension-wise variation, so the within-user variation structure is retained at a tractable estimation complexity. The process first extracts writing errors and their syntactic contexts from the users' historical reviews to build personalized error profiles, then applies local character modifications to these queries according to the users' historical transformation records and error-mechanism preferences, forming personalized writing-error queries for evaluating changes in retrieval performance. PQB extracts writing-error characteristics from the users' historical reviews to construct personalized error profiles. Specifically, GECToR~ first produces the corrected forms of the texts, and ERRANT~ then aligns the original and corrected texts to identify and annotate the edit operations. For the extracted historical edits, PQB records the error type, the transformation form and the associated syntactic context, and counts the historical transformations that are available for character-level perturbation. Unlike continuous syntactic representations, writing errors consist of discrete character-level editing operations. Their personalized characteristics involve not only how frequently users make errors, but also which error mechanisms they prefer and how these mechanisms relate to syntactic contexts. A shared global error distribution overlooks individual differences in mechanism preferences, whereas a context-independent user distribution cannot capture the relationship between writing errors and local syntactic structures. Meanwhile, fine-grained contexts may contain only limited historical error evidence for individual users. PQB therefore adopts a user-specific, context-conditioned error-mechanism distribution estimated from historical frequencies, while retaining each user's overall mechanism preferences and observed character transformations. To characterize a user's writing-error preferences under different syntactic structures, PQB further counts the relation between error mechanisms and syntactic contexts. This distribution describes the relative frequency of different error mechanisms for the user in a given syntactic context. In parallel, PQB aggregates the user's historical edits to compute the overall character-level error-mechanism preferences and historical transformation frequencies. A user's writing-error profile therefore consists of the historical edits, the global error-mechanism preferences and the context-conditioned error distribution, and is used for the personalized writing-error query generation in Section~. After the personalized writing-error profiles are obtained, PQB perturbs the personalized syntactic expression queries selected in Section~3.3 at the character level. For user $u$ and the corresponding product $a$, let the personalized syntactic expression query be $q_{u,a}$ and the generated personalized writing-error query be $_{u,a}$. The process uses the user's historical error mechanisms and their context information to modify local characters of the query, constructing paired queries for evaluating the impact of writing errors on retrieval outcomes. For each personalized syntactic expression query, PQB ranks the alphabetic tokens of at least three characters by the user's historical character-level error rate and determines the candidate edit positions. At each selected position, PQB first attempts to reuse a character transformation observed in the user's historical reviews. Transformation matching proceeds from the most specific context to the least specific, backing off from the full syntactic context through dependency and POS information, coarse-grained context, and token-level context. When no applicable historical transformation exists, PQB samples an error type from the frequency distribution of the user's overall character-level error mechanisms and generates the corresponding character modification. To control the perturbation scope, PQB attempts to modify at most three distinct token positions per query and constrains the edit distance and the query length change. To monitor the semantic effect of these edits, PQB constrains the semantic similarity between each personalized syntactic expression query and its writing-error counterpart: a generated writing-error variant is rejected when its semantic similarity to the original query falls below $0.9$, where $ $ is computed with the same all-mpnet-base-v2 encoder~.
