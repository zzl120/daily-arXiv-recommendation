Saberi} {Tal Wagner} {Stanford University} {Amazon AWS} {Tel Aviv University} Efficient vector retrieval requires both a corpus geometry that supports retrieving the right documents through vector similarity, and a query encoder that can embed queries near their desired documents in the embedding space. Recent work has studied geometric capacity through the lens of the minimum embedding dimension needed to realize all top-$k$ answer sets of $n$ documents. We study a different notion of geometric capacity—the maximum recall achievable for a frozen document index—and explore whether learned query encoders can reach this ceiling. On several real-world retrieval benchmarks, we show that retrieval quality of single-vector query encoders often lies far below what the document indices can support. Motivated by this observation, we give theoretical evidence that learning query encoders can be computationally hard. In particular, we construct a retrieval task that admits a query encoder with perfect recall which is representable by a small one-hidden-layer ReLU network, but any statistical-query learner provably requires exponentially many statistical queries to achieve non-trivial recall advantage over the random baseline $k/n$. Taken together, our results suggest substantial unrealized geometric capacity in retrieval benchmarks and establish query encoder learnability as a possible barrier in embedding-based retrieval. Efficient, large-scale modern vector retrieval systems are based on the bi-encoder architecture in which documents are embedded offline as vectors and stored in a that can be reused across a stream of queries. At retrieval time, a maps the incoming query into the shared vector space, and the system returns the $k$ best documents by similarity score . The success of this architecture depends on two distinct requirements: the must support accurate retrieval through vector similarity---that is, there must exist a query embedding whose $k$-nearest documents match the semantically intended documents for that query; and it must be to learn a query encoder that produces these query embeddings. The first has been the primary focus of recent work. Theoretically, it has been shown that even if documents are embedded optimally, geometry can be the limiting factor when embedding dimension is low and the retrieval task requires realizing many distinct top-$k$ subsets of the document corpus; at the same time, empirical studies have observed that these geometric limits do not necessarily take effect in practice . These findings motivate the questions: to what extent does a document index support high recall, and subject to this ceiling, how much recall can a learned query encoder achieve? We investigate both questions empirically and theoretically. Our first contribution is an empirical study of the gap between the of a document index and the recall achieved by learned query encoders. We develop a ranking support vector machine -based method for efficiently gauging the geometric capacity of a given embedded corpus, then use it to study the recall attained on 20 datasets from the BRIGHT, BEIR and LIMIT retrieval benchmarks by various pre-trained and fine-tuned query encoders. Our results show that geometric capacity is rarely the limit---on most datasets, even a lightweight document encoder can support near-perfect retrieval. On the other hand, the tested query encoders---even when fine-tuned on queries and their gold sets---mostly fail to exceed 50\ Our second contribution is a theoretical result which shows the existence of a retrieval task for which learning a good query encoder is computationally hard. Namely, we construct a task for which a query encoder with perfect recall exists and can be represented as a small ReLU network, but learning a query encoder with better-than-random recall requires exponentially many steps in the statistical query learning model ---a computational model for supervised learning in which a learner may only access the training set via aggregate statistics. This establishes a rigorous setting where retrieval quality is constrained not by the document geometry, but by the inability to efficiently learn a good query encoder. This result is formalized and proved in . Our results complement the recent line of work on geometric limits of embedding-based retrieval, shining a spotlight on query encoding as a barrier and possible source of hardness. A recent line of research has explored whether embedding dimension constrains the performance of single-vector retrieval methods by considering the question: showed dimension lower bounds for realizability with and without margin, and introduced the LIMIT data to showcase these limitations empirically. Subsequent work has extended the no-margin lower bounds beyond inner product scoring , characterized the minimum embedding dimension for max-margin separation , and examined further limits imposed by quantization . Each of these results assumes the ability to embed documents optimally. Empirically, independent observations have shown that geometry is not, in fact, the primary source of difficulty for the LIMIT dataset , instead attributing difficulty to mechanisms like drowning in documents or general learning failures. We show that this phenomenon is not unique to LIMIT---on realistic retrieval benchmarks, dense retrievers often achieve performance far below what is supported by the document geometry. \\ The statistical query model restricts a learner's sample access to aggregate statistics of the training distribution; a setting that captures gradient-based learning under suitable precision and batch-size conditions . Following the foundational result that parity functions are not efficiently SQ-learnable , subsequent work has characterized weak SQ-learnability for function classes and developed SQ lower bounds for search problems in which an algorithm must produce an output satisfying a success criterion . Complementing recent work on the geometric limitations of embedding-based retrieval, we explore whether learned query encoders can exploit the geometric capacity of a frozen document index. Our main takeaway is that producing a high-quality query encoder can pose a substantial barrier even when the geometry of the underlying document index is not restrictive. We exhibit this empirically on real-world encoder models and benchmarks through a ranking-SVM method to gauge geometric capacity, and theoretically through a computationally hard-to-learn family of retrieval tasks in the statistical query model.
