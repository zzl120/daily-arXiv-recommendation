These exploratory, unadjusted analyses support relevance decisions as a complement to dense retrieval within this protocol, without establishing general superiority, probability calibration or equivalence between depths. Dense semantic retrieval compares separately encoded queries and documents, making a large corpus reusable across queries . Sentence embeddings also support cosine-based semantic similarity . Similarity is a representation-based ranking signal. It need not directly express whether a document contains evidence relevant to a particular scientific claim. Retrieve-then-rerank methods allocate additional query-dependent computation to a shortlist , while retaining the first stage's candidate-recall constraint. Sequence-to-sequence ranking already uses relevance-label token scores to order documents . Decision APIs provide another externally hosted scoring interface. In our experiments, Jev's Noul interface and OpenAI Decisions' Predicate interface each return a raw probability for an independent query--document decision. We ask each whether a document contains information relevant to assessing a claim, including supporting or contradicting evidence. The comparison concerns interfaces and observed outputs, rather than identical architectures or a verified common calibration scale. There is no answer-generation stage. We evaluate the official BEIR SciFact test benchmark with a dense-only BGE-M3 baseline . Two complementary protocols distinguish ranking from discovery. We evaluate identical positive-enriched -document pools for queries and rerank unchanged Top-50 and Top-100 prefixes retrieved from the full -document corpus. Additional provider contrasts and candidate-depth analyses are exploratory and use unadjusted intervals. Candidate depth controls known-evidence availability, the number of potentially expensive decisions, and opportunities for ranking errors or score ties. An exploratory, non-preregistered depth comparison extends the saved dense prefix from 50 to 100 without positive injection. Our questions are: , how do dense similarity, Jev Noul and OpenAI Predicate rank identical bounded candidate pools? , how effectively do the two APIs rerank identical candidates retrieved by full-corpus BGE-M3? , how do score saturation, exact ties, usage-derived cost and operational latency affect interpretation and practical applicability? , how does increasing first-stage dense candidate depth from 50 to 100 affect reranking effectiveness, candidate recall, score behavior and operational cost? Separately encoded representations permit similarity computation without joint inference for every query--document pair. Sentence-BERT trains sentence embeddings that can be compared by cosine similarity . Dense Passage Retrieval learns separate question and passage encoders for retrieval . These related designs illustrate reusable representations, but semantic-similarity training and retrieval supervision are distinct objectives. A single-vector bottleneck does not imply uniformly poor generalization. Large dual encoders improve out-of-domain retrieval through greater model capacity and multi-stage training while retaining fixed-size dot-product scoring . This evidence motivates evaluating a concrete retriever and training configuration rather than attributing all observed ranking errors to vector similarity. BEIR exposes variation across retrieval tasks and domains . One dataset cannot establish universal model superiority. SciFact identifies scientific evidence supporting or refuting claims . We use its BEIR retrieval judgments rather than generating veracity decisions or rationales. M3-Embedding supports dense, sparse and multi-vector representations . Our BGE-M3 baseline selects only its normalized dense representation. The study therefore evaluates a specific dense retrieval configuration rather than every capability of the embedding model. Sentence-BERT and the large dual encoders provide context, not additional experimental baselines. Query-dependent ranking encompasses several computational arrangements. BERT passage reranking jointly represents a query and candidate passage to estimate relevance . ColBERT instead performs late interaction between independently encoded token representations, preserving offline document encoding while retaining token-level matching . ColBERTv2 combines residual compression and denoised supervision to address the quality and storage costs of this multi-vector approach . Joint encoding, late interaction and single-vector similarity allocate storage and query-time computation differently. They are not interchangeable algorithms. Sequence-to-sequence document ranking trains a model to produce relevance-label tokens and normalizes the corresponding true/false logits to obtain a score for each candidate . Candidates are then ordered by those scores. This is an established connection between language-model outputs and independent relevance scoring, but it does not establish that target-token probabilities equal Jev Noul or OpenAI Predicate scores, share their calibration, or reveal their proprietary implementations. RankT5 directly outputs numerical query--document ranking scores and explores pairwise and listwise ranking losses . The distinction concerns the training objective as well as the inference output. An independently computed score can be learned using relations among candidates. Our API experiment observes independent decisions and their ranking effects. It does not identify either provider's training loss. Language-model reranking also includes generating a permutation of a group of candidate passages. Sun et al. investigate this instructional ranking approach with ChatGPT and GPT-4 . A generated group ordering differs from sorting independently returned scalar relevance decisions. Their models, inputs and protocols are not experimentally interchangeable with the interfaces evaluated here. Language-model-based reranking thus predates this study. No priority claim is made. A bounded first stage permits richer scoring without evaluating every corpus document, but constrains the evidence available to the reranker. Our BGE-M3 followed by Jev or OpenAI Decisions uses this established retrieve-then-rerank organization. Cross-encoders, late interaction, sequence-to-sequence ranking, RankT5 and generative LLM reranking provide context. None is an experimental comparator here, and we make no performance claim about either decision API relative to these methods. A scholarly preprint evaluates Jev on classification, inference and structured-decision benchmarks . Another studies decision-oriented recommendation reranking using controlled candidates derived from SASRec . The latter uses a distribution over candidate choices, whereas our Jev and OpenAI conditions independently score a binary relevance decision for each document. Both works are preprints, without verified peer review. They provide evaluation context rather than proof of calibration or retrieval performance for the scores studied here. We evaluate dense vector similarity and two probabilistic decision-scoring systems under shared scientific retrieval conditions. Jev Noul and OpenAI Predicate receive semantically equivalent relevance questions and identical candidate sets, with fixed provider-specific inputs. This makes their observed ranking behavior comparable within the protocol without establishing identical proprietary architectures, training procedures or probability scales. Controlled pools isolate ordering when every known positive is available. Dense Top-50 and Top-100 prefixes test reranking under a first-stage coverage constraint. Returned score saturation, exact ties, paired effectiveness, usage-derived cost and separately defined operational timers are evaluated for both providers. The contribution is this empirical comparison, not a new reranking architecture or a claim of priority for decision-based relevance scoring. Calibration, cross-domain robustness and conventional cross-encoder comparisons remain outside the evaluated scope. We investigate how probabilistic relevance decisions compare with dense vector similarity for semantic retrieval, and whether they improve dense retrieval when used as a reranking stage. The evaluation concerns retrieval and ranking of documents for scientific claims. It contains no answer-generation stage. We evaluate BGE-M3 and two independent relevance scorers, Jev and OpenAI Decisions, under shared SciFact protocols. We distinguish ranking within a controlled candidate pool from discovery of candidates in the full corpus. We use the official BEIR SciFact test benchmark , with documents, test queries and positive query--document relevance judgments . Query text is the scientific claim. Document input combines its title, a newline and its text. Raw corpus, query and qrel files were preserved unchanged, without alteration. Only the official test queries enter the evaluation. We refer to pairs without a positive qrel as . This term describes the benchmark annotation status and does not establish that a document is irrelevant because many pairs are unjudged. Recall and nDCG are evaluated against the official qrels, with absent judgments contributing no positive gain under that reference. The dense baseline uses . We use only the dense representation. CLS pooling followed by L2 normalization produces -dimensional embeddings. Sparse representations and ColBERT/multi-vector functionality are excluded. For normalized embeddings $e_q$ and $e_d$, the score is = e_q,e_d , which is cosine similarity for unit-length vectors. Claims receive no added query instruction. Documents receive no chunking or query-dependent expansion. The configured maximum input length is tokens. Inference uses float16 on Apple MPS, document batches of , and CPU threads. Encoded vectors are stored as float32. Corpus embeddings are cached, and CPU NumPy computes dot products and rankings using the stored matrix. Queries are encoded individually. All methods break exact score ties by lexicographic document ID after descending raw score. This is a deterministic rule on the string identifiers, not numeric identifier order. Bounded inference workers release persistent MPS allocations without changing model precision, text construction, maximum length or scoring. The Jev condition uses the pinned model and the frozen specification . Each claim--document pair is evaluated independently, with the claim, document title and text supplied as state. The question is whether the candidate contains information relevant to assessing the scientific claim. Its positive criterion includes information supporting, contradicting, qualifying or otherwise directly bearing on the claim. It does not ask whether the claim is true or supported specifically. The ranking signal is the raw returned Noul relevance probability, =P , where $R$ denotes document relevance under the decision specification. This notation represents the API's returned score. Probabilistic calibration was not evaluated. Candidates are sorted by this score in descending order. No relevance threshold was selected, and no decision wording was tuned after observing benchmark results. The relevance criterion remains fixed across both candidate protocols and depths. No claim about model internals is required by this evaluation. The OpenAI condition uses at , with one named question per claim--document request. A deterministic text input labels the claim, document title and document text, separated by newlines. The positive and negative instructions retain the Jev relevance criterion. This creates semantic task equivalence, not evidence of identical architectures, capabilities, output resolution or probability calibration. We rank by the raw in the named predicate answer, $ $, independently of other candidates. We neither ask for explanations nor generate answers to claims. Requested and returned model identifiers, raw responses, request identities, serializer version and usage are preserved. A refusal or missing/invalid probability is a recorded failure, not an assigned zero. The final two conditions are complete. The wording was frozen after connectivity verification and before pilot/full evaluation. No quality-driven test-set prompt tuning was performed. Saved scores are summarized separately for each provider and candidate condition, including counts, means, medians, dispersion, exact-zero/one fractions and distinct values. Qrel-positive and nonpositive groups are descriptive benchmark strata. We distinguish ties anywhere in a pool, ties crossing the top-five or top-ten boundary, mixed-label tied groups intersecting the top ten, and changes in evaluated metrics. These definitions prevent a large nonpositive tied tail from being mistaken for an effectiveness loss. The OpenAI secondary sensitivity analysis uses predeclared seeds, 0--99. For each seed, a Python stream is reset for each query. Lexicographically sorted IDs are shuffled only within exactly equal raw-probability groups. Unequal scores retain their ordering. Official-qrel metrics are then averaged over the same test queries. Reported minima and maxima cover these seeded realizations, not every possible permutation. Primary rankings remain the original document-ID policy. No favorable seed is selected. For each of the queries, we construct one -document pool containing every official positive plus sampled nonpositive corpus documents. Sampling uses seed and deterministic per-query streams. The candidate pools are fixed across all three scorers. Dense ranking did not select the sampled nonpositive candidates. Pilot candidate pools are retained in the controlled benchmark. BGE-M3, Jev and OpenAI receive exactly the same frozen candidates. This condition isolates ranking quality given a bounded candidate set, with positives guaranteed to be present. It does not evaluate full-corpus Jev or OpenAI candidate discovery. The guarantee of positive inclusion distinguishes it from the full-corpus retrieval condition below. The full dense condition ranks all SciFact documents for each of the official test queries. RQ1 isolates a different question. All official positives are guaranteed in the controlled pools. Sampled nonpositive documents are often unlike the harder dense-selected shortlist. Higher controlled metrics therefore cannot be interpreted as a gain from shrinking a production corpus, and no measured candidate-size scaling curve is established by placing the two conditions side by side. OpenAI's positive Recall point estimate relative to dense has an interval including zero, which narrows the effectiveness claim for that outcome. Jev has higher observed Recall@5 and nDCG@10 than OpenAI in both tested conditions. The negative OpenAI-minus-Jev paired intervals support this descriptive ordering under the evaluated query sample, but the exploratory nature of the additional contrasts and unadjusted multiple comparisons limit confirmatory interpretation. The hybrid Recall interval's near-zero upper endpoint merits particular caution. Neither point estimates nor these intervals establish a universal best provider or superiority on other models, languages or application domains. RQ3 shows that the numerical behavior of returned scores matters. OpenAI's many exact zeros produce large tied groups. A group entirely in a nonpositive tail may have no metric effect. Mixed-label groups crossing a cutoff can affect recall, and positional changes within a retrieved list can affect discounted gain. The saved tie realizations show measurable sensitivity, with primary results still defined by document-ID order. The realized ranges quantify this analysis only. They are not exhaustive bounds or alternative scores to select for reporting. Jev also returns tied scores despite avoiding the exact endpoints in these runs. Endpoint fractions alone do not explain all ranking errors. Neither output distribution was calibrated against fully adjudicated relevance probabilities, and no architectural or training explanation follows from the histograms. The APIs implement semantically comparable relevance decisions, but replacing one with the other requires attention to model identity, input serialization, numerical behavior and request/response handling. A shared binary question is insufficient to make their probabilities interchangeable. Provider updates and nondeterministic serving may change outcomes even when model identifiers remain fixed. Both APIs add hosted-request latency and monetary expenditure to local dense retrieval. Jev's observed condition costs and operational query medians are lower in these records, but dates, cache reuse, retries and timer boundaries differ. These observations do not estimate hardware-controlled inference efficiency. Overlapping cached pairs prevent adding usage-derived condition totals as fresh expenditure. Latency-tolerant ranking applications might accept this trade-off, whereas interactive applications would need deployment-specific validation. Neither application class was evaluated here. Established cross-encoder and late-interaction work demonstrates query-dependent reranking . We compare Jev and OpenAI Decisions as independent relevance-scoring interfaces under the same candidate constraints, without inferring their internal architectures. No cross-encoder experimental baseline was included. Our contribution lies in a common SciFact protocol with retained query pairing, raw-score diagnostics and traceable costs/timers. The evidence answers the four questions within that protocol while leaving cross-domain robustness, calibration and alternative reranker comparisons unresolved. RQ4 distinguishes the opportunity to retrieve known evidence from the scorer's ability to place it near the top. Extending the dense prefix recovers only additional known-positive pairs. Jev's final metrics rise slightly. OpenAI's Recall rises but nDCG falls slightly. All four depth intervals include zero, leaving small effects uncertain rather than demonstrating equivalence. Additional high-scored candidates can displace earlier documents, and incomplete judgments complicate interpretation of those intrusions. Mixed cached/new timers cannot quantify a cold latency penalty relative to Top-50. The observed quality--cost trade-off warrants caution about assuming deeper reranking is automatically beneficial. It does not establish Top-50 as universally optimal or Top-100 as useless because only two depths, one benchmark and one frozen dense retriever were evaluated. Controlled-100 is positive-enriched ranking. Its metrics do not measure the effect of expanding a dense shortlist. Direct probabilistic relevance decisions can complement dense retrieval, but scorer selection should account for ranking effectiveness, score behavior, operational latency and cost.
