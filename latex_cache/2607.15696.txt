In multi-turn mobile interaction scenarios, user requests typically rely on dialogue history, coreference relationships, and implicit slots, where a single input is often insufficient to fully describe the objective. This necessitates that agents transform ambiguous instructions into semantically clear, well-bounded, and executable atomic sub-tasks to guide high-precision tool retrieval. Early prompt engineering based methods, such as ReAct~, ToolReAGt~, Q2E~, and PLUTO~, achieved progress in few-shot scenarios through explicit chains of thought or structured plans. However, due to the lack of end-to-end optimization mechanisms and heavy reliance on intrinsic knowledge, these methods are prone to decomposition deviations caused by cumulative errors when facing unseen tools or long-range dependencies. To overcome this limitation, reinforcement learning based end-to-end methods like ToolQP~ incorporated downstream tool retrieval metrics into mixed reward signals. Although these approaches enhance final retrieval performance, they fundamentally rely on static outcome-oriented supervision and suffer from two core limitations. First, directly employing retrieval metrics as rewards is highly susceptible to . Models tend to maximize matching scores through superficial strategies such as repetitive decomposition, leading to a spurious correlation between the shallow lexical features of decomposition results and retrieval outcomes. This severely impairs generalization in Out-of-Domain scenarios involving unseen tools. Second, there is a lack of preference modeling for decomposition structure. plan-level similarity-based rewards fail to constrain structural quality dimensions such as logical coherence, context faithfulness, and atomicity, further exacerbating the issue of repetitive decomposition. Empirical results in Figure~ validate this phenomenon: existing methods perform reasonably well on in-distribution data but suffer significant performance degradation on OOD datasets involving unseen tools . This framework introduces a preference reward $R_{}$, which leverages a trained reward model to score candidate decompositions against human references conditioned on context, providing fine-grained structural quality supervision via relative score differences; simultaneously, it incorporates a counterfactual reward $R_{}$ that uses the retrieval results of the raw query as a baseline to quantify the marginal gain of the decomposition strategy on tool ranking, thereby offering causal feedback for decomposition behavior. The dataset highlights the dynamic evolution of intents across multi-turn dialogues and cross-application collaboration, providing a standardized basis for quantifying the impact of decomposition quality on retrieval effectiveness. At the task structure level, the system supports proportional sampling of three modes: single-tool, multi-tool collaboration, and multi-intent parallelism, while allowing dynamic injection of new tasks during context inheritance. The tool sampling phase combines global random weighting with scene-based knowledge base guidance, utilizing pre-defined or LLM-dynamically-generated scene templates to ensure logical self-consistency of tool combinations within specific domains. Corresponding to the content synthesis module in the pipeline, the framework employs a dual-channel mechanism to generate user queries $q_t$ and assistant responses $o_t$ separately. The user-side generator integrates available tool lists, historical state trajectories, and linguistic style features, employing natural language strategies to avoid mechanical concatenation and explicitly completing missing information dimensions in clarification states. To guarantee corpus quality, we embed a structured hard-constraint layer into the generation process, including: the one-tool-one-request atomicity principle; colloquial conversion of terminology; normalization of temporal expressions; and zero-response specifications for silence states. These constraints act as quality filters throughout the generation process, ensuring that the output data possesses both linguistic naturalness and logical rigor. To characterize the true contribution of task decomposition to tool retrieval, we construct a Structural Causal Model , as illustrated in Figure~. Specifically, $X$ denotes the multi-turn dialogue input, $D$ denotes the task decomposition strategy, $Y$ denotes the retrieval ranking quality, and $Z$ represents confounding factors such as high-frequency keywords, tool priors, and model parametric memory. Traditional methods directly optimize outcome-oriented metrics such as Recall or NDCG, which are susceptible to the spurious path $X Z Y$. As a result, retrieval gains caused by raw-query keywords or tool priors may be incorrectly attributed to task decomposition, inducing shallow strategies such as repetitive decomposition and keyword stacking. To mitigate this attribution bias, we introduce a paired counterfactual comparison. Given the same input, tool library, retriever, and ground-truth tool set, we jointly evaluate the decomposition branch $d$ and the non-decomposition baseline $d_0$. Therefore, the residual performance difference can be used as a controlled estimate of the marginal contribution of task decomposition. Research on agent planning and tool retrieval has evolved along two primary paradigms~: Prompt-based and In-Context Learning approaches, grounded in the Reasoning-Acting-Reflecting cycle established by ReAct~ and ToolReAct~. Subsequent works have achieved effective tool selection within fixed inventories by explicitly generating chains of thought, sub-goals, or API invocation sequences; and Joint optimization via Retrieval-Augmented Generation ~ and Reinforcement Learning~, designed to address challenges in large-scale open tool libraries. These methods typically decompose complex instructions into structured sub-queries and refine policies using synthetic trajectories and RLVR. Recent studies further incorporate GRPO~, fine-grained tool-use rewards, or hierarchical advantage estimation to enhance end-to-end performance in long-horizon planning and multi-step invocation. However, existing paradigms directly use retrieval metrics as rewards, which easily induces reward hacking by relying on spurious correlations between shallow lexical features of decompositions and retrieval outcomes, leading to OOD generalization failure and repetitive decomposition. To address this, we propose the PCTD framework, which cuts off spurious correlations via counterfactual rewards and synergizes preference rewards to enforce fine-grained structural constraints, achieving joint optimization of task planning and tool retrieval in both causal gain and structural quality. Existing benchmarks for agent task decomposition and tool retrieval fall into two primary categories: tool-interaction benchmarks , which focus on tool selection, parameter alignment, complex long-tail invocation, and retrieval robustness; and task-oriented benchmarks , which emphasize end-to-end planning, task decomposition, and completion rates in open environments. Although recent efforts like HammerBench~ have extended evaluation to function calls in real-world mobile scenarios, mainstream benchmarks remain constrained by Outcome Bias, relying predominantly on final success rates or trajectory-level metrics. They critically lack fine-grained process annotations for multi-turn interactions, such as cross-turn context inheritance, coreference resolution, dynamic intent evolution, task atomization, and semantic tool mapping. To address this, we introduce MTDTool, a fine-grained benchmark for multi-turn mobile interactions. By providing multi-level annotations covering dialogue intent evolution chains and ranked tool candidates, it establishes a new paradigm for fine-grained process evaluation in complex mobile contexts, offering a standardized basis for quantifying the impact of task decomposition quality on tool retrieval efficacy. In this work, we reveal that existing reinforcement learning-based task decomposition methods are prone to reward hacking due to ill-designed reward signals, leading to repetitive sub-task decomposition and severe OOD generalization failure. To address these issues, we propose PCTD, a framework that synergizes counterfactual rewards to cut off spurious correlations and preference rewards to enforce structural quality, fundamentally resolving the aforementioned challenges. Furthermore, we release MTDTool, a fine-grained benchmark for evaluating task decomposition in mobile multi-turn interactions. This category includes three types: Single-turn Single Tool, Single-turn Multi-tool , and Single-turn Cross-category Multi-tool. We further define several common multi-turn interaction types, including Domain Switching, Exception Scenario, Unexecuted Task Inheritance, Follow-up Selection, User-added Task, Mixed State Type, and Multi-turn Inheritance. Domain Switching refers to situations where the current turn abruptly shifts to a completely new topic or domain that is unrelated to the previous turn. Exception Scenario describes cases where abnormal execution states occur during task execution, such as failures, cancellations, re-executions, or rollbacks. Unexecuted Task Inheritance indicates that a silent task from the previous turn is still running in the background and its state must be tracked or inherited in the current turn. Follow-up Selection refers to interactions where the assistant asks the user for missing information or clarification, or where the user selects among multiple options. User-added Task describes situations in which the user introduces a new task during the dialogue that is unrelated to the previous task.
