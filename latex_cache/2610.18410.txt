It also supports LLM agents and long-term memory systems by identifying relevant documents, passages, or stored facts. Dense retrieval embeds queries and passages in a shared vector space and ranks candidates by vector similarity . This approach is effective when supporting evidence is semantically close to the query, but may miss relevant passages connected through intermediate entities or multi-step relations. Knowledge graphs represent these connections explicitly, but using them for retrieval introduces two challenges. Traversing or reranking the graph for every query increases query-time complexity, while modifying embeddings according to graph structure may distort the semantic relationships captured by the original embeddings. This leads to the central question of this work: Prior work has either incorporated relational knowledge into embeddings~, used graphs directly at query time~, or refined retrieval through textual feedback alone~. Our work connects these directions by examining both how graph-modified embeddings should be constructed and how they should be used in multi-stage retrieval. We construct graph-modified embeddings offline using several transformations, including local neighbourhood aggregation, random-walk endpoints, community centroids~, and convex neighbour pull. We then compare retrieval strategies ranging from replacing the original embeddings with graph-modified embeddings to multi-stage strategies that combine both. At query time, every strategy uses only cosine-similarity searches over stored embeddings; the graph is neither traversed nor queried. Our central hypothesis is that graph-modified embeddings are more useful for guiding the expansion of retrieval than for measuring relevance to the original query. The best-performing strategies therefore assign distinct roles to the two sets of embeddings. An initial search over the original embeddings retrieves semantically relevant results . Their graph-modified embeddings are then aggregated to guide the selection of candidates that are both relevant to the query and related to the seeds through the graph. Finally, the original embeddings of the selected candidates are used to reformulate the query for another search over the original embeddings~. We refer to this use of graph-modified embeddings to guide retrieval as . To evaluate these strategies, we introduce a synthetic professional-network benchmark in which the corpus, graph, questions, answers, and supporting evidence are generated from the same underlying facts. The benchmark includes single-fact, relational and collection, and extremisation questions, together with exact gold answers and complete gold-evidence sets containing the intermediate and competing records required to establish each answer~. We select configurations on a development corpus and report results on separately generated validation data . All methods use the same original embeddings, cosine similarity, and the same 40-node retrieval limit. Multi-stage controls without graph conditioning allow us to distinguish improvements produced by graph-modified embeddings from those produced by multi-stage retrieval alone. None of the graph-modified representations improves direct retrieval over the original embeddings in our experiments. The original embeddings recover $73.1\,\ We provide a controlled comparison of multiple graph-based embedding transformations and retrieval strategies under a common cosine-similarity framework, without requiring graph access at query time. We introduce a synthetic benchmark with questions of different relational complexity, exact gold answers, and complete question-specific supporting evidence. We show that retrieval performance depends more on how graph-modified embeddings are used than on the particular transformation that produces them, and that they are most effective as intermediate signals within a multi-stage retriever. We introduce a retrieval strategy in which graph-modified embeddings guide exploration while the original embeddings preserve semantic alignment with the query, and we quantify its performance. Our work connects research on graph-modified embeddings, feedback-based and multi-hop retrieval, and graph-based retrieval. We also draw on evidence-level evaluation to assess whether these approaches recover the passages needed to answer a question. Retrofitting adapts pretrained embeddings using external relational knowledge. Faruqui et al.~ introduced an objective that balances fidelity to the original embeddings with attraction between connected concepts. Counter-fitting adds attraction and repulsion constraints~, while Functional Retrofitting introduces relation-specific transformations for heterogeneous knowledge graphs~. Retrofitting has also been applied beyond word embeddings, including multilingual sentence representations aligned through Abstract Meaning Representation~ and sense representations specialised using WordNet relations~. Related approaches use entity descriptions and language models to construct representations for knowledge-graph completion, incorporating structural information through different training objectives or architectures . Another line of work derives node representations from graph connectivity. DeepWalk learns embeddings from contexts generated by truncated random walks~, while node2vec uses biased walks to capture different network neighbourhoods . These methods are conceptually related to our random-walk transformation, which instead averages existing textual embeddings at walk endpoints without learning new representations from walk sequences. Simple Graph Convolution separates graph-based feature propagation from a learned classifier~, providing a related perspective on our neighbourhood-based aggregation of existing embeddings. We draw on these ideas to study graph-modified embeddings for evidence retrieval, rather than intrinsic similarity, node classification, or knowledge-graph completion. In particular, we compare their use as direct search representations with their use as intermediate signals in a multi-stage retriever. Pseudo-relevance feedback refines a query using the results of an initial retrieval stage and then performs a second search. NPRF brought the idea into learned ranking, feeding the top-ranked documents of a first pass into a neural ranking model~. More recent approaches apply the same principle to dense retrieval, where the query is a vector rather than a set of terms, and differ in what that vector is allowed to be. ColBERT-PRF keeps a multi-vector query and extends it, extracting representative embeddings from the pseudo-relevant documents and adding them to the query representation before re-retrieval~. ANCE-PRF keeps a single query vector but learns an encoder that consumes the initially retrieved passages, leaving the document index unchanged~. Multi-hop retrieval extends this sequential approach to questions whose supporting evidence cannot be identified from the original query alone. Multi-Hop Dense Retrieval conditions successive searches on previously retrieved passages to recover chains of supporting evidence . Other approaches use generated text to guide subsequent retrieval. IRCoT interleaves retrieval with intermediate chain-of-thought reasoning steps , while Iter-RetGen alternates retrieval and generation, using each generated output to inform the next retrieval iteration . Our method uses offline graph-modified embeddings to guide intermediate retrieval, followed by a Rocchio-style update that blends the query with the mean embedding of retrieved nodes in the original space , without query-time graph access or LLM generation. Controls without graph conditioning isolate the contribution of graph conditioning. Graph-based systems use explicit relations to find evidence that semantic similarity may miss, such as education records linked to a company through its employees. GRAFT-Net reasons over question-specific subgraphs of facts and text~; PullNet expands them through iterative retrieval~; and HippoRAG propagates relevance through entity connections using Personalized PageRank~. These systems use the graph during query processing, whereas we encode its relations into embeddings offline and retrieve evidence through stored vectors alone. GraphRAG uses community summaries to answer global questions about a corpus~. Although related to our use of graph communities, it produces text for corpus-level synthesis, whereas we produce node-level vectors for retrieving question-specific evidence under a fixed budget. Multi-hop question-answering benchmarks support evaluation beyond final-answer accuracy by identifying the evidence needed for reasoning. HotpotQA provides sentence-level supporting facts alongside questions and answers, including both bridge and comparison questions . MuSiQue constructs multi-hop questions by composing single-hop questions whose reasoning steps depend on one another, explicitly addressing shortcuts that allow apparently multi-hop questions to be answered without combining the required evidence . Ground-truth-subgraph benchmarks such as SynthKGQA and GTSQA further support the evaluation of graph retrieval independently of answer generation . We adopt this evidence-centred perspective: our synthetic corpus and questions are generated from the same structured facts, enabling us to specify question-specific gold-evidence sets and measure their recovery directly. The benchmark includes relational and collection questions and extremisation questions, allowing us to examine retrieval of both indirectly connected evidence and the competing records required to establish an extremum. We evaluate how graph-based embedding transformations and retrieval strategies affect evidence recovery on a synthetic professional-network benchmark. We use separate development and validation corpora, generated by the same pipeline and containing synthetic resumes and organisational personnel rosters. Each corpus is represented as a tripartite graph with three node types: documents, chunks (textual evidence from those documents), and entities . Three relation types connect them: links chunks to documents, links documents to their primary entities, and links chunks to entities referenced in their text. The schema admits only these three edge patterns. Each corpus contains approximately 1,200 chunks, 370 entities, 230 documents, and 3,700 edges. The development and main validation corpora are node-disjoint: none of the 1,759 node identifiers in the validation corpus occurs in the development corpus. We use Cognee.} to ingest and store the generated graph and manage its associated node embeddings. We select configurations on the development corpus and report results on the validation set of $977$ questions, including $90$ generated on a supplementary corpus from the same pipeline. Appendix~ provides generation details, exact validation statistics, and the schema with an example . We generate questions and ground-truth answers from the structured facts underlying the dataset. Gold evidence includes every chunk required to establish an answer. The benchmark contains 11 question families, detailed in Appendix~. We group them into three categories: These ask for attributes directly associated with entities mentioned in the query and generally require a single evidence chunk or an immediate relation. For example, ``What skills did a person use at a given employer?'' can be answered using a job chunk that mentions the person, employer, and skills. These ask for attributes or entities connected to a named entity through intermediate entities. For example, ``Which universities were attended by the employees of an organisation?'' requires evidence linking the organisation to its employees and those employees to their universities. The target chunks may lie two or three relations from the named entity and share little surface content with the query. These compare candidates to identify a maximum and may then request another attribute of the selected candidate. Establishing the maximum requires the relevant records of all competing candidates. For example, ``What certifications does the most experienced worker at a given company hold?'' requires experience records for the comparison and the winner's certification evidence, which may be semantically unrelated to the comparison criterion. We modify the original embeddings offline to incorporate information from related nodes in the graph. The five transformations differ in which nodes influence each modified vector and how their contributions are combined . Graph-modified embeddings guide the search for additional evidence, while the original embeddings maintain semantic relevance to the query. This supports developing graph-modified embeddings specifically for multi-stage retrieval and evaluating their contribution to the complete retrieval process. Retrieval should adapt as evidence accumulates. The question and earlier results should guide when to use graph information, how to allocate the remaining search budget, and when enough evidence has been found to support an answer. Future evaluation should test the approach on natural corpora, additional embedding models, and graphs with missing or incorrect relations. It should also establish whether smaller retrieved contexts offset the cost of additional searches. The corpus is a fully synthetic professional network of fictional candidates, organisations, resumes, employer rosters, and textual evidence. Generation is deterministic under a fixed seed and does not involve a language model: every fact is sampled from predefined source pools and then rendered into text by fixed templates. Because the graph is built before any question exists, no answer information can enter its construction. A candidate's name is a first--last pair drawn from two flat pools of 512 and 458 entries. A set rejects any repeat and the draw is retried, so every person in the dataset has a distinct full name before the identity-collision checks performed at ingest. With roughly $2.3 10^{5}$ available combinations and a few hundred candidates per graph, rejection is rare. Uniqueness is what allows the human-readable identifier to serve as the canonical key: two candidates sharing a name would otherwise collapse into a single node and silently merge their evidence. Each candidate is assigned a primary sector sampled uniformly from eleven, which governs certifications and supplies the default source for employer and role attributes. A candidate receives one or two education records, between one and four jobs, zero to two projects per job, zero to three certifications, and zero to two endorsements. Education degrees are sampled with weights $50\,\ With probability $0.2$ a job's role attributes---title, skills, responsibilities, tenure, salary band, team size, and project name---are drawn from a different sector, selected through a fixed affinity map, while the employer remains tied to the candidate's primary sector. This prevents the graph from collapsing into isolated sector-specific clusters and produces known bridges between otherwise sector-oriented regions, such as technology roles inside healthcare organisations. Employment information is represented from two document perspectives. Resume-side chunks describe one person's education, jobs, projects, endorsements, and certifications. After all candidate histories are generated, a second pass groups jobs by employer: any employer with at least two employees receives a roster document and a roster chunk listing every employee and title, which mentions the organisation and each employee individually. The same employment fact therefore appears both in a person's job chunk and in an employer's roster chunk, so information about one entity is distributed across documents connected only through shared entities. This is what makes the corpus suitable for graph-retrieval experiments rather than reducible to independent flat documents. This anchored neighbour-attraction formulation is related to retrofitting~, applied here to corpus-node embeddings. jointly adjusts node embeddings by balancing fidelity to their original textual representations against agreement between graph neighbours. A useful physical interpretation is a system of springs: each node is attached to its original position and to every adjacent node. The modified embeddings are the equilibrium of this coupled system. Let $_i$ be the original embedding and $_i$ the unknown modified embedding. Let $E$ contain unordered edge pairs, each counted once, and let $N $ be the corresponding neighbour set. The objective is J =a _i\| z_i- x_i\|_2^2 +b _{ E}\| z_i- z_j\|_2^2. Here $a=>0$ and $b= 0$. We use $a$ rather than $ $ to distinguish this penalty weight from the blending coefficient used by other transformations. Squared Euclidean distances yield a differentiable quadratic objective with linear stationarity equations. The positive anchor term makes the objective strictly convex, so its minimiser is unique. The implementation rejects nonpositive anchor weights. With $a=0$ and $b>0$, any embedding configuration constant within each connected component has zero energy; the minimiser is no longer unique. The original vector has weight $a$, while each neighbour has weight $b$. Thus, total graph influence grows with degree: the denominator is $a+3b$ for three neighbours and $a+60b$ for sixty. An isolated node remains at its original vector. Although each coordinate update has a closed form, the global solution requires solving the coupled system. The solver initialises $ Z$ with the original embeddings and visits nodes in sorted identifier order. Each update is written immediately, so later nodes in the same sweep use the newest available neighbour vectors. This is Gauss--Seidel iteration, rather than a synchronous Jacobi update based entirely on the previous sweep. It requires no learning rate or gradient clipping, although the objective weights and stopping controls remain configuration choices. For this anchored quadratic, convergence is to the same unique minimiser; finite-sweep results and convergence speed can depend on update order. Sorted identifiers ensure a reproducible order. During sweep $t$, the solver records the largest Euclidean displacement over all node updates, _t= _i\| z_i^{}- z_i^{}\|_2. The displacement is measured before overwriting each vector. Convergence is declared when $ _t<10^{-6}$; the criterion uses the maximum rather than the mean, so every node must satisfy the tolerance. On the reported development graph of 1,811 nodes, convergence took approximately forty sweeps, with a final maximum displacement of $9 10^{-7}$.
