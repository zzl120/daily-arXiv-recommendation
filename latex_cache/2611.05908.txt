A system that can detect when additional computational effort is unlikely to improve effectiveness may then engage alternative actions, including asking users to clarify their information need~ or suggesting alternative query formulations~. This capability has particular relevance in modern agentic retrieval workflows, where autonomous agents coordinate multiple reasoning, retrieval, and synthesis steps to satisfy complex information objectives. In Agentic Information Retrieval , an agent connects observation, reasoning, and action across multiple steps to achieve the desired information state rather than simply returning a static ranked list~. Similarly, multi-agent RAG frameworks orchestrate planners, reformulators, extractors, and synthesizers to address distinct subproblems within a complex task, invoking specialized agents on demand~. These paradigms underscore the importance of diagnostic signals that can inform decisions on whether to continue ranking, move on to refinement, or solicit human interaction. In particular, determining whether a query is limited by ranking quality or by query formulation depends on observing how relevant documents are distributed across retrieval depths . Importantly, this distribution is not only a property of the ranking model itself, but a latent characteristic of both the query and the documents within a collection that are relevant to it. Using dense LLM-based judgments, we directly observe depth--effectiveness trajectories for each query. As shown in Figure~, queries exhibit markedly different behaviors: some benefit monotonically from deeper re-ranking, others plateau early, and some experience clear degradation as depth increases. For each query, we define an optimal re-ranking depth as the shallowest depth that achieves maximal effectiveness under the ideal DCG envelope. We also analyze an LLM-assessed estimate of first-stage ranking quality as an indicator of whether deeper re-ranking is likely to be beneficial. Importantly, although both quantities derive from LLM assessments, they are computed over different objects---first-stage versus re-ranked lists. Our analysis reveals that queries exhibit distinct optimal re-ranking depths, and that re-ranking beyond this depth can degrade effectiveness below that of the initial ranking. We evaluate a suite of standard query performance prediction methods as proxies for selecting re-ranking depth and find that they are generally ineffective under this setting as compared to ideal-DCG computed with the automated judgments. We show that an LLM-assessed estimate of first-stage ranking quality is more informative than standard QPP for calibrating depth, reducing average re-ranking depth by up to a factor of three while preserving overall effectiveness across multiple first- and second-stage configurations. RLT methods~ aim to determine a cut-off point $k$ for a ranked list based on retrieval signals, either globally or on a per-query basis. RLT methods are typically designed as reactive stopping mechanisms, optimizing a trade-off between effectiveness and cost under incomplete relevance judgments. Our study is conceptually distinct. Rather than deciding when to stop re-ranking based on observed signals, we analyze re-ranking depth as a query-intrinsic property under near-complete relevance information. This allows us to ask a different question: whether the optimal re-ranking depth of a query can be predicted from properties of its first-stage retrieval under dense relevance judgments, and how much effectiveness and efficiency are theoretically recoverable under such predictions.
