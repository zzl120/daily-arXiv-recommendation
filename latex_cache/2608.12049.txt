This process naturally forms a sequential decision problem, where each decoding step selects a DocID token and the complete token sequence determines the retrieved document. However, retrieval effectiveness is typically evaluated only after the full DocID is generated, creating a mismatch between token-level generation and document-level relevance supervision. As a result, existing reinforcement learning methods for generative retrieval mostly rely on sequence-level rewards, where the same document-level feedback is propagated to all decoding steps. Such coarse-grained feedback makes it difficult to identify which token decisions are responsible for successful or failed retrieval. In this work, we propose a fine-grained reinforcement learning framework for generative retrieval with token-level relevance rewards. Instead of assigning a single reward to the entire generated DocID, we estimate step-wise rewards by measuring how each token decision changes the expected retrieval quality of the corresponding generation trajectory. Recent neural retrievers typically encode queries and documents as dense vectors, retrieve candidates through maximum inner-product search~, and often apply a separate reranking stage~. Although effective, this paradigm depends on an external index, fixed-dimensional representations, and multi-stage objectives that do not directly optimize the whole retrieval process toward final relevance~. Generative retrieval provides an alternative by directly producing the identifier of a relevant document conditioned on the query~. The generated document identifiers are decoded into retrieved documents, converting retrieval into a sequence generation problem that can represent corpus indexing and query-time retrieval within a unified model. With suitable identifier design, constrained decoding, and sequence-to-sequence training, this framework has shown promising effectiveness and has become an active direction in neural information retrieval. Despite this progress, the training objective of generative retrieval remains imperfectly aligned with the goal of document retrieval~. Most existing methods rely on autoregressive supervised fine-tuning, which maximizes the likelihood of each target DocID token~. This objective is natural for sequence generation, but retrieval quality is evaluated only after the complete DocID sequence is generated and mapped to a document. The model is therefore optimized at the token level while being judged at the document level. A generated DocID may contain several locally plausible tokens but still refer to an irrelevant document; conversely, some intermediate token decisions may be important because they route the generation process toward a semantically appropriate region of the identifier space. The standard next-token likelihood does not explicitly distinguish these cases, since it treats DocID generation as imitation of observed identifiers rather than direct optimization of document-level relevance. Reinforcement learning provides a principled way to bridge this gap by treating DocID generation as a decision-making process and optimizing the model with relevance feedback~. Recent work has explored reinforcement learning from relevance feedback for generative retrieval , as well as preference-based optimization methods inspired by direct preference optimization . These approaches move beyond pure teacher forcing and seek to align generated DocIDs with document-level relevance. However, they typically use sequence-level rewards: once a complete DocID is generated, the resulting document receives a relevance score, and the same feedback is assigned to all tokens in the sequence. This coarse supervision leaves a critical credit assignment problem unresolved~. In an autoregressive DocID, different tokens play different roles. Early tokens may constrain the candidate subspace, middle tokens may refine semantic or structural choices, and later tokens may determine the exact document. Assigning an identical reward to every decoding step obscures which token decisions actually improve or harm retrieval quality. In this paper, we argue that effective reinforcement learning for generative retrieval requires token-level credit assignment. We formulate DocID generation as a sequential decision problem, where the state is the query together with the partially generated DocID, the action is the next DocID token, and the final outcome is the retrieved document. From this perspective, the key challenge is not only whether the generated document is relevant, but also how each token decision changes the expected quality of the generation trajectory. A fine-grained reward should encourage tokens that move the policy toward relevant documents and penalize tokens that divert decoding toward irrelevant regions, even when the final sequence-level outcome alone is ambiguous. To address this challenge, we propose , a fine-grained reinforcement learning framework that separates token-level reward design from the choice of policy optimization algorithm. Our training pipeline follows two stages. First, we perform supervised fine-tuning to endow the model with basic query-to-DocID generation ability. Second, we refine the model with reinforcement learning under token-level credit assignment. Instead of assigning one reward to the entire generated sequence, provides step-wise feedback for intermediate DocID tokens by comparing the generated hidden-state trajectory with the gold DocID trajectory from a frozen reference model. This trajectory-based reward gives the policy a dense signal for distinguishing helpful and harmful token decisions without requiring an additional reward model. As an optimizer-agnostic credit assignment framework, assigns fine-grained credit to individual DocID tokens while remaining compatible with different reinforcement learning algorithms. In this work, we consider two representative implementations: , which uses group-relative advantage estimation to compare multiple candidate DocIDs for the same query, and , which uses value-function-based advantage estimation under the same token-level reward design. Both implementations are compatible with different forms of DocIDs and can be combined with constrained decoding over the legal DocID space. We conducted experiments on widely used retrieval benchmarks to evaluate the effectiveness and generality of token-level credit assignment. Document retrieval is a fundamental task in information retrieval, where the goal is to find relevant documents from a large corpus given a user query. Traditional approaches to document retrieval include term-based methods such as TF-IDF and BM25~, which rely on exact term matching and heuristic scoring functions. More recently, neural retrieval methods have gained popularity, leveraging deep learning techniques to learn dense vector representations of queries and documents . These methods typically involve encoding queries and documents into a shared vector space and using similarity measures to retrieve relevant documents. However, they often require separate stages for indexing and retrieval, which can be computationally expensive and may not fully capture the complex relationships between queries and documents. Generative retrieval formulates document retrieval as an autoregressive generation problem, where a model directly generates document identifiers for a given query. Existing studies have mainly improved this paradigm from four aspects: : Existing DocIDs can be broadly divided into lexical and numerical identifiers. Lexical identifiers, such as titles, URLs~, representative n-grams, or summaries~, are human-readable and easy to interpret. Numerical identifiers instead encode documents as discrete semantic codes, including clustering-based semantic codes~ and RQ-VAE-based quantized identifiers~. : Existing methods enrich indexing through document chunking~, pseudo-query generation~, and rehearsal-based augmentation~. Other studies further investigate multi-granular indexing~ and continual indexing for dynamic corpora~. : Existing methods can be broadly divided into autoregressive and non-autoregressive paradigms. Autoregressive decoding generates DocIDs token by token, typically using constrained beam search to restrict the output space to valid DocIDs~. Recent studies further explore non-autoregressive DocID generation to improve efficiency and global control~. These methods generate multiple DocID tokens in parallel or refine identifiers through iterative procedures, such as diffusion-based language models and parallel generation frameworks. : Early generative retrieval models are mainly optimized by supervised likelihood training~. Later work incorporates ranking-oriented supervision, including marginal ranking loss~, knowledge distillation~, and contrastive learning~. Recent RL-based methods further optimize retrieval feedback: GenRRL~ adopts RLHF with an external reward model, while DDRO~ leverages pairwise relevance feedback without explicit reward modeling. In contrast, our work studies generative retrieval from a sequential decision-making perspective and views DocID generation as a trajectory of token-level decisions, where each generated token affects the final document-level retrieval quality. Credit assignment concerns how a training signal should be distributed to the individual decisions that produced it. In LLM reinforcement learning, the central challenge is often token-level: although generation proceeds autoregressively token by token, supervision in RLHF is typically provided only as a sequence-level reward on the final response~. Some methods introduce denser supervision directly, such as sentence-level human feedback~, while a larger line of work infers token-level rewards from response-level preference signals through reward densification~, reward redistribution~, continuous token-level reward modeling~, and token-wise preference optimization objectives~. Although these studies demonstrate the importance of fine-grained credit assignment, they are not directly tailored to generative retrieval. Unlike open-ended text generation, generative retrieval produces a document identifier as a constrained token sequence, where each token can be viewed as a routing decision over the document space. An incorrect token may lead the decoding trajectory to a wrong branch and make the target document unreachable, even if the remaining tokens are locally plausible. Therefore, credit assignment in generative retrieval is more structurally sensitive: the model needs to understand how each token decision contributes to identifying the correct document, rather than simply improving the overall quality of a generated response. We propose , a reinforcement learning framework that assigns credit to individual DocID tokens in generative document retrieval. Given a query, a generative retriever autoregressively produces a document identifier, and the generated identifier is mapped back to a document in the corpus. The key idea of our method is to optimize this process at the same granularity at which decisions are made: instead of assigning one sequence-level reward to the complete DocID, we estimate token-level rewards for intermediate decoding steps and use them to update the generation policy. The training pipeline contains two stages. First, we perform supervised fine-tuning to initialize the model with basic query-to-DocID generation ability. Second, we run reinforcement learning post-training with token-level credit assignment. In this stage, an SFT model is frozen as a reference model, gold DocID trajectories are cached under teacher forcing, and the current policy generates candidate DocIDs under constrained decoding. Token-level rewards are then computed by comparing the generated hidden-state trajectory with the gold trajectory, and group relative policy optimization is used to update the policy. This design combines token-level credit assignment with the stability of group-relative advantage estimation. We first train a base generative retriever with standard supervised fine-tuning. In this work, we consider two representative DocID designs. The second uses product quantization codes, termed PQ, which discretize document representations into semantic code tokens through vector quantization. These two identifier types represent different retrieval regimes: TU DocIDs are more interpretable and lexical, while PQ DocIDs provide compact semantic identifiers. This objective teaches the model the syntax of the DocID space and the basic alignment between queries and document identifiers. A token that receives high likelihood under teacher forcing may still lead to an irrelevant document when generated under the model's own prefix. This limitation motivates the second training stage, where we use relevance-aware token-level rewards to refine the policy. Generative retrieval generates DocIDs token by token, but existing RL feedback is usually document-level. This paper addresses the mismatch with , which compares generated hidden states with gold DocID trajectories from a frozen reference model to assign token-level trajectory rewards. improves generative retrieval by turning document-level supervision into dense feedback over the actual decoding trajectory. Across MS MARCO and NQ, consistently improves top-ranking metrics over supervised fine-tuning and sequence-level reinforcement learning baselines, while shows that the reward design is not tied to a single optimizer. The reward ablations further indicate that the gains come from token-level credit assignment rather than reinforcement learning alone, and the training analyses show that constrained rollout, moderate group size, and DocID-dependent KL regularization are important for stable and effective optimization. These results also highlight two practical limitations. First, introduces a more complex training pipeline because it relies on a supervised reference model before reinforcement learning optimization. Second, the reinforcement learning stage adds nontrivial training overhead due to rollout generation and token-level reward computation. Simplifying this two-stage procedure and improving the efficiency of RL-based optimization remain important directions for future work. More broadly, our findings suggest that assigning credit at the same granularity as autoregressive retrieval decisions is a promising route toward better aligning generative retrievers with retrieval objectives.
