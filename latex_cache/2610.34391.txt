The continuous growth of scientific literature makes manual review and cross-paper comparison increasingly inefficient. The problem is especially acute in materials science and condensed-matter physics, where important evidence appears as chemical formulas, abbreviated compound names, numeric properties, and multiple notations for the same material. Traditional search systems rely mainly on titles, abstracts, and word overlap. They therefore miss relevant publications when authors use different wording, chemical symbols, material aliases, or abbreviations. Scientific portable document format files add further difficulty. They contain multi-column layout, tables, mathematical expressions, superscripts and subscripts, line breaks, and chemical formulas. Converting such documents to text may disrupt reading order and distort individual symbols. Technical text extraction alone is insufficient: domain context and links between extracted entities are also required. This challenge is particularly important for superconductivity literature when analyzing temperature values. A single paper may mention synthesis, annealing, magnetic and structural transition temperatures, Debye temperatures, and results for several materials. The numerically largest temperature in a document is not necessarily the superconducting critical temperature $T_c$. Correct $T_c$ assignment requires analysis of surrounding text and an explicit link between each temperature value and the corresponding material. Existing search methods address only part of this problem. Keyword search handles paraphrases, abbreviations, and alternative chemical notations poorly. Dense vector retrieval can find semantically similar passages, but textual similarity does not guarantee physical correctness. A retrieved fragment may refer to a different compound, experimental configuration, or type of phase transition. The same limitation appears in retrieval-augmented generation systems. Answer quality depends on which fragments are retrieved and passed to the language model. If the material is misidentified, a chemical formula is corrupted, or a temperature is linked to the wrong physical process, the system may produce a fluent but factually incorrect answer. Generic retrieval-augmented generation approaches usually do not model domain-specific constraints or verify links between materials, properties, and experimental values. A key research gap is that scientific data extraction, publication search, and answer generation are often treated as separate tasks. Extraction systems produce structured records, whereas search and retrieval-augmented systems work mainly with raw text fragments. Whether normalized domain features can improve document ranking, evidence collection, and answer groundedness remains insufficiently studied. In this work, we treat these stages as one integrated process. Thematically selected arXiv superconductivity publications are transformed into a corpus in which full text is augmented with structured material and physical-property information. These fields are used together with semantic representations of text fragments. The approach combines dense retrieval with precise domain constraints, such as material name and $T_c$ value. Our focus is on the information-processing side of the problem: we build the pipeline and then measure, stage by stage, whether domain-oriented structured data helps search and answer generation compared with keyword-only or dense text-only methods. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: An end-to-end pipeline covering thematic arXiv selection, scientific document processing, structured extraction, evidence retrieval, and question answering. A domain-aware extraction module for superconducting materials and critical transition temperatures with context filtering to reduce false material--temperature links. A hybrid retrieval approach that combines dense semantic search over scientific text fragments with structured publication metadata, together with an analysis of when such metadata helps and when extraction noise cancels its benefit. An empirical evaluation on a gold set of 59 materials and a 100-question domain benchmark, plus a screening application that compares literature-derived experimental $T_c$ values with an independent theoretical reference set. The main research question is whether extracted and normalized domain data improve the accuracy of relevant evidence retrieval and answer groundedness compared with lexical-only or dense text-only methods. We separately evaluate the effect of context-aware temperature processing, correct material--$T_c$ pairing, and structured features in chunk ranking. Prior work spans scientific information extraction, superconductivity-specific corpora, PDF-to-text conversion, document retrieval, and retrieval-augmented generation . Below we focus on the aspects of each line that matter for a coupled extraction--retrieval pipeline. ChemDataExtractor~ and ChemDataExtractor~2.0~ established rule-driven pipelines that convert chemical text into database records, including units and property links rather than isolated entity spans. SciBERT~ and MatSciBERT~ later improved materials named-entity recognition and relation classification through domain pretraining. These systems focus on representation and record construction. They do not decide which compound is the primary object of a paper, do not repair PDF conversion artifacts in formulas, and do not test whether extracted fields help downstream retrieval. For our task, rule-based quantity handling and neural semantic matching are complementary. SC-CoMIcs~ showed that specialized annotation is required for superconductor terminology. SuperMat~ further treats critical temperature as a value linked to a material and, when available, to measurement conditions. Grobid-superconductors~ operationalizes that schema over PDF or plain text and powers the large SuperCon2 extraction database, and is the closest extraction analogue to our module. Our contribution is therefore not material--$T_c$ extraction as such, but the use of the extracted fields as retrieval and answering signals, evaluated end to end. The same domain ambiguity noted in these works---identical symbols can denote different physical quantities---motivates the explicit context checks around candidate temperatures used here. Extraction quality is bounded by the initial text representation. Multi-column layout, tables, and broken reading order can split formulas or merge unrelated spans. S2ORC~ demonstrates large-scale conversion of open scientific PDFs into unified full-text corpora. Our setting is narrower: we build a thematic arXiv superconductivity sample so that domain filters and $T_c$ heuristics can be specialized. Domain filtering does not fix layout errors, so formula-preserving PDF conversion remains a separate pipeline stage. Lexical methods such as BM25 remain strong when queries contain exact formulas, but fail under paraphrase. Dense Passage Retrieval~ recovers semantic matches without lexical overlap, yet provides no built-in chemical or physical constraints. Citation-informed encoders such as SPECTER~ and SciNCL~ improve document-level scientific similarity, but are not designed to locate fine-grained experimental claims inside full text. BEIR~ shows that no retriever is universally best across domains. Hybrid sparse--dense retrieval~ is therefore a natural baseline, and we extend it with structured material and $T_c$ metadata as an additional ranking signal. Lewis ~ established RAG as parametric generation conditioned on a non-parametric index. Correctness still breaks down when retrieval returns a thematically related but physically wrong span. QASPER~ evaluates supporting evidence as well as final answers, and PaperQA~ applies RAG to full-text scientific papers with source attribution. These systems handle domain structure implicitly through text and do not expose normalized superconductivity fields as filters or reranking features. Extraction systems typically end at databases, whereas scientific RAG systems typically start from generic full-text chunks. The effect of normalized material--$T_c$ fields on evidence ranking and grounded answering has therefore received little measurement. We evaluate extraction, hybrid retrieval with and without domain metadata, and answer groundedness in one pipeline. The proposed system transforms thematically selected arXiv superconductivity publications into a searchable corpus with structured metadata and retrieval-augmented question answering. Inputs are publication records containing an arXiv identifier, title, and topical categories, together with the corresponding PDF files stored in object storage. The system supports both full-prefix processing of all documents under a given storage path and selective reprocessing of individual arXiv identifiers, which avoids restarting the entire pipeline when only a subset of papers must be updated. The pipeline consists of four linked stages. First, a thematic superconductivity corpus is constructed from arXiv metadata and bulk PDF archives without downloading the full arXiv collection. Second, each selected PDF file is converted to text and processed by domain-aware extractors that recover material, critical temperature $T_c$, article type, dimensionality, unconventional-superconductivity cues, and Debye frequency. Third, bibliographic fields and normalized subject attributes are stored in PostgreSQL, with subject attributes kept as JSONB payloads linked to the source document. Fourth, publications with reliable material extraction are chunked, embedded, and indexed for hybrid retrieval: dense similarity over text fragments is combined with structured metadata filtering and domain-aware reranking, after which a local language model generates answers grounded only in the retrieved context and source identifiers. Extraction and retrieval are deliberately coupled. Extraction results determine which publications enter the main retrieval-augmented generation index. Extracted attributes are copied into chunk metadata for filtering and reranking. A shared PDF converter keeps chemical formulas and reading order consistent across extraction and indexing. The core implementation uses Python, PostgreSQL with pgvector, object storage for PDF files, and a local language model served through Ollama. The first stage performs thematic selection from arXiv metadata harvested through the Open Archives Initiative Protocol for Metadata Harvesting . Harvesting uses the resumption-token mechanism, which makes it possible to process large metadata volumes sequentially without loading the full archive at once. After metadata retrieval, publications are filtered by the category . For each retained paper, a normalized arXiv identifier and the bibliographic fields required for later processing are stored. Individual PDF downloads from arXiv were not used, because the complete PDF collection is distributed as TAR archives. Instead, a two-stage indexing mechanism locates target documents inside the bulk archive. First, the arXiv manifest file is processed to describe the available TAR archives and their parameters. Second, each TAR archive is scanned and an index is built that maps every PDF identifier to its location inside a specific TAR file. This index resolves the physical location of a required document without scanning all archives for every request. After the index is available, selective extraction is performed. For each paper retained by metadata filtering, the system identifies the corresponding TAR archive and extracts only the needed PDF file into the project object store. Compared with downloading the full arXiv PDF collection, this approach substantially reduces storage and transfer costs by retaining only target-domain publications. The resulting index contains 2\,921\,746 PDF identifiers distributed across 10\,847 TAR archives. Index correctness was checked by comparing the number of indexed records with the source arXiv manifest. After category filtering, 46\,746 superconductivity-related publications were obtained. Of these, 46\,177 PDF documents were successfully extracted from the bulk store and saved for further processing. The remaining 569 publications appear in arXiv metadata, but the corresponding PDF files were absent from the bulk store at download time. Additional verification confirmed that these files are missing from the source store and are therefore not pipeline extraction failures. Long-running extraction jobs are managed through a task-state model. Each document-extraction operation stores its current status, which enables parallel processing by multiple workers and safe continuation after transient failures. The design is intended to scale corpus construction without tying the method to a specific cloud vendor. After a PDF file is converted to text, an additional subject filter is applied. In the current superconductivity block, the beginning of each document is inspected for terms that indicate a mismatch with the target research scope, including mentions of qubits, Josephson devices, SQUID systems, and Majorana states. Papers that contain explicit pressure values in GPa or kbar are also excluded from the current analysis set, because high-pressure experiments introduce temperature and material behavior that fall outside the present extraction rules. Each document receives one of three outcomes: successful passage, exclusion by the subject filter, or processing error. The output of this stage is therefore a set of arXiv identifiers linked to publication metadata and to the location of the corresponding PDF files in object storage. For each selected publication, the PDF file is loaded from object storage and converted to text with a PyMuPDF-based extractor. Processing operates on text blocks, lines, and spans rather than as a naive character stream. The algorithm accounts for multi-column layout, hyphenated line breaks, and superscripts and subscripts. For two-column documents, reading order among text blocks is restored, hyphenated words are rejoined, and indices are preserved in the text representation through lightweight HTML markup. Preserving subscripts and superscripts is essential for chemical formulas. Warnings and critical errors raised during PDF processing are logged separately for each arXiv identifier. Failure on one document does not stop the pipeline for the remaining publications. The recovered text is passed to a set of domain-oriented extractors that determine: the studied superconducting material or material family, the superconducting critical temperature $T_c$, the article type, the dimensionality of the studied system, cues of unconventional superconductivity, Debye frequency values reported in the paper. Material and $T_c$ extraction are performed jointly in a coupled sequence. First, candidate material mentions are collected and ranked. Next, a preliminary $T_c$ value is extracted. The main material choice is then refined using the temperature evidence, after which $T_c$ is extracted again for the selected material. Together with the final numeric $T_c$ value, the system stores the supporting sentence, surrounding context, evidence type, and a confidence score. These diagnostics support later verification and error analysis. The output of this stage is the full publication text and a normalized subject payload containing $T_c$, article type, material, dimensionality, an unconventional-superconductivity flag, Debye frequency, material-determination status, and diagnostic information about $T_c$ extraction. {3pt} { }{|L{0.28 }|Y|} & \\ Material & Formula, alias, or material family, with determination status \\ $T_c$ & Critical temperature in kelvin, with supporting context and confidence \\ Article type & Experimental, theoretical, or mixed \\ Dimensionality & Bulk, two-dimensional / thin-film, or mixed \\ Unconventional SC & Presence of unconventional-superconductivity cues \\ Debye frequency & Reported Debye parameter with unit, kept separate from $T_c$ \\ The system separates storage of source documents, article-level attributes, and search representations. Source PDF files remain in object storage, while publication metadata, processing statuses, and subject-extraction results are stored in PostgreSQL. Stable bibliographic fields, such as the arXiv identifier and bibliographic descriptors, are stored as relational columns. Subject attributes are stored as a JSONB payload, which allows the set of extracted characteristics to evolve without creating a separate table for each new field. Before persistence, values are normalized: missing and invalid fields are mapped to a predictable schema, illegal control characters are removed, and material extraction receives an explicit status. Subject-extraction status and search-index status are stored separately. The result is a structured publication record keyed by arXiv identifier and containing bibliographic data, a normalized JSONB subject payload, processing status, and a link to the source PDF file. Only publications for which material extraction finished with an admissible status are included in the vector index. Their PDF files are converted again with the same text extractor used for subject extraction, which keeps chemical formulas and reading order consistent across pipeline stages. The recovered text is cleaned and split into overlapping fragments. In the current configuration, each fragment is approximately 1600 characters long, with a 250-character overlap between neighbors. When suitable boundaries exist, the split point is moved to the end of a paragraph, sentence, or other logically complete unit. Each fragment is embedded with a multilingual E5-family model. Vectors are stored in PostgreSQL using the pgvector extension. A fragment record contains the arXiv identifier, fragment ordinal, source text, embedding, embedding-model name, text-extractor version, and a JSONB metadata object. The metadata copies the publication title, categories, recovered materials, $T_c$, dimensionality, article type, and unconventional-superconductivity flag. Thus every text fragment remains linked both to its source publication and to that publication's structured attributes. User queries are handled in two complementary modes. Explicit structured requests, such as finding materials with $T_c$ above a given threshold or selecting publications of a given dimensionality, are answered with deterministic SQL over JSONB fields. In that case, the answer is produced directly from previously extracted structured data. For open scientific questions, semantic search over text fragments is used. The query is embedded with the same model used during indexing. Domain constraints are also inferred from the query text, including material, $T_c$ range, dimensionality, article type, and unconventional-superconductivity cues. These constraints are converted into metadata filters over fragment records. After the initial vector search, results are reranked. Fragments that consist mainly of bibliographies, figure captions, tables, corrupted text, or dense formulas without coherent explanation are down-weighted. Fragments that explicitly mention the requested material or confirm a critical-temperature value are up-weighted. To improve source diversity, the number of fragments selected from a single publication is limited. If strict metadata filtering returns too few candidates, vector search is repeated without mandatory constraints, and the same domain-aware reranker is applied. Selected fragments are grouped by publication and converted into language-model context. Each paper receives a source label such as or . Structured article attributes and the strongest supporting spans are passed together with the fragment text. A local language model receives the user question and the assembled context, with an instruction not to invent materials, temperature values, or conclusions that are absent from the provided sources. The system returns a textual answer together with the used publications and the identifiers of the fragments that support the answer. Information extraction and search are designed as interdependent parts of one pipeline. First, subject-extraction results determine membership in the main retrieval-augmented generation index: publications without a reliably determined material are not indexed. Second, extracted attributes are copied into the metadata of every text fragment, which enables SQL filtering, deterministic answers, and domain-aware reranking of semantic search. Final ranking therefore depends not only on vector similarity between the query and publication text, but also on subject attributes recovered earlier. Third, PDF conversion quality affects both subsystems: loss of chemical subscripts can corrupt material extraction, and broken column order can produce incoherent search chunks. Using one domain-aware PDF converter keeps textual representation consistent across extraction and indexing. Domain-aware processing is especially important for superconductivity literature because a single publication may mention several materials and many temperatures that belong to different physical processes. Contextual $T_c$ filtering is intended to exclude Debye, structural, magnetic, synthesis, and other non-superconducting temperatures. Material--$T_c$ binding addresses the additional problem of attaching a recovered value to the correct studied compound. The extraction experiments confirm a large gain over regex on material--$T_c$ pairs, but also show that the current final filters can over-reject correct candidates. Improving the precision--recall balance of these heuristics is therefore a priority for the extractor. Structured metadata also makes downstream retrieval depend on extraction quality. Correctly extracted material and $T_c$ fields can improve filtering and ranking of evidence, whereas an extraction error can be amplified later along the cascade defined in Section~. Empirically, hybrid retrieval with extracted fields did not outperform plain hybrid on aggregate source recall, which is consistent with noisy metadata. When gold evidence did reach the generator, hybrid with fields gave the highest conditional answer accuracy, although on small subsets. The NbB$_2$ failure further shows that document-level recall is not enough: incorrect intra-document evidence can still yield a grounded-looking but wrong temperature. This is the main difference from specialized extraction systems such as Grobid-superconductors~. The present work does not stop at constructing a database of extracted materials and $T_c$ values: those structured fields are used as signals for evidence retrieval and grounded question answering, and their value is limited by extraction fidelity. As a separate materials-informatics application, the pipeline was used to find materials whose literature-derived experimental $T_c$ disagrees with theoretical reference values. Two independent datasets were compared. Dataset~1 is a theoretical reference set of studied superconducting materials. For each compound it stores chemical composition and a theoretically computed critical temperature $T_c^{}$. Dataset~2 is a literature-derived experimental set produced automatically by the proposed pipeline. For each material the system collects experimental $T_c$ values found across publications, together with their sources. A physics expert then aligned the two sets. Materials were first reduced to a comparable representation using chemical formulas and aliases. The expert then verified correspondences between the theoretical reference set and the literature-extraction results. When a material appeared in more than one publication, the median experimental critical temperature was used. For observations $T_{c,1}^{}, ,T_{c,n}^{}$, _c^{} = . The median reduces the influence of outliers, incorrectly extracted values, and sample-to-sample experimental differences. After this expert matching, 200 materials were present in both datasets. For each of them the median experimental value was compared with the corresponding theoretical value. A material was treated as a potentially anomalous candidate if _c^{} > T_c^{}. Of the 200 matched materials, 20 had a median literature $T_c$ above the theoretical reference. These 20 compounds were retained as candidates for further investigation of possibly anomalous superconducting behavior. Such a discrepancy does not establish that a material is an unconventional superconductor. It may reflect limitations of the theoretical model, specifics of experimental samples, structural phase, stoichiometry, or source-data error. The selected materials are therefore candidates for additional physical analysis, not confirmed unconventional superconductors. The practical value of the experiment is that the pipeline can assemble a literature-derived dataset from a large publication corpus, after which an expert can compare it with independent theoretical data and inspect deviations of interest. The main limitations are specialization to a single domain, the modest size of the manually verified gold set, dependence of downstream results on PDF processing and extraction quality, noisy extracted metadata in hybrid field retrieval, weak intra-document evidence ranking relative to document recall, small subsets behind some per-type and conditional metrics, and dependence of the screening application on the theoretical reference dataset. There is also a trade-off between conservative $T_c$ extraction, which leaves more empty fields, and aggressive extraction, which increases false positives. This paper presented a domain-aware pipeline for automatic analysis of superconductivity literature. The system combines construction of a thematic arXiv corpus, processing of scientific PDF files, extraction of materials and critical temperatures $T_c$, structured metadata, hybrid retrieval, and retrieval-augmented generation. On the primary positive extraction set, domain-aware methods raise material--$T_c$ pair accuracy from $9.1\ Practical applicability was further demonstrated by matching the automatically assembled literature-derived dataset to an independent theoretical reference set. After expert normalization and verification, 200 materials appeared in both sources. For 20 of them the median experimental $T_c$ from the literature exceeded the theoretical reference value, which identified them as candidates for further physical study. These candidates are not interpreted as proven unconventional superconductors; the discrepancy serves only as a selection criterion. Future work includes tightening temperature filters without losing correct candidates, more precise intra-document evidence ranking, a larger gold set and benchmark, additional physical parameters, and application of the same approach to other materials-informatics tasks.
