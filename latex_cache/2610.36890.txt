Among various recommendation architectures, generative recommendation ~ has garnered significant interest from both academia and industry. For the ranking task in recommendation, some GR methods~ employ a discriminative loss for modeling, utilizing a streamlined Transformer~ architecture. In the setting considered in this work, user context and explicit candidate items are organized into a shared token sequence, while a candidate mask enables parallel candidate scoring without information leakage across candidates. This approach improves system utilization during both training and inference and has shown strong effectiveness in large-scale recommendation systems. However, these methods predominantly adopt a fast-thinking direct prediction paradigm, where the final result is obtained through a single inference step. Since ranking requires fine-grained scoring over candidate items, a key question is whether additional inference-time computation can further refine these scores beyond one-shot prediction. Inspired by the success of the Chain-of-Thought~ mechanism in Large Language Models~ , recent work has begun exploring the integration of reasoning into recommendation models by introducing multiple intermediate reasoning steps before generating final recommendations. Some of these methods~ employ LLMs as recommendation models and introduce mechanisms analogous to explicit CoT in LLM, allowing the recommender to perform reasoning in textual space before generating final recommendations. However, such methods are highly dependent on the reasoning techniques inherent in LLMs~, which come with a significant computational overhead~. In addition, LLMs have not completely replaced more widely deployed non-textual recommendation models, limiting the applicability of these approaches in widely deployed ranking scenarios. Therefore, a more practical direction is to introduce latent reasoning~, multi-step computation in continuous latent space, into widely used Transformer-based recommendation models, including ID-based recommendation settings. Existing attempts along this direction target autoregressive semantic-ID generation~ or refine user representations for next-item recommendation~ with Transformer-based recommenders~. These task interfaces differ from ranking with explicit candidates, where user context and candidate items are jointly encoded to directly produce candidate scores. The OnePiece framework~ further demonstrates the feasibility of latent reasoning in industrial ranking through block-wise candidate-representation refinement and progressive multi-task supervision. Despite this progress, designing latent reasoning for fine-grained candidate scoring raises three challenges. First, latent reasoning must be aligned with the learning objective of candidate ranking. Whereas semantic-ID generation and next-item recommendation optimize different objectives, ranking requires fine-grained scores for explicit candidates. Therefore, successive reasoning steps should be formulated and supervised to progressively improve candidate scores under the ranking objective. Second, in the ranking process, multiple candidate items are scored simultaneously. An intuitive solution is to independently assign one or more reasoning tokens to each candidate item, which we refer to as the candidate-specific reasoning process. However, such a design may fail to effectively leverage global user information, which plays a central role in existing retrieval-oriented latent reasoning. Third, existing latent reasoning frameworks predominantly rely on end-to-end training, which requires replacing or retraining well-established online recommenders and thus limits practical applicability. Consequently, another critical challenge is how to enable latent reasoning through parameter-efficient adaptation with minimal disruption. Generative Recommendation ~ has emerged as a promising paradigm by reformulating recommendation as a sequence transduction problem. Existing GR research can be broadly divided into two categories based on tokenization strategies and training objectives. The first category~ emphasizes semantic item identification, where items are tokenized into discrete semantic codes for autoregressive generalization. Representative works such as TIGER~ leverage RQ-VAE~ to tokenize items into hierarchical semantic IDs for autoregressive item prediction. The second category~ focuses on encoding item features into dense embeddings while adopting discriminative training objectives. Representative works in this line have demonstrated strong performance in large-scale recommendation settings. In ranking variants, user context and explicit candidate items are jointly encoded to produce candidate scores through parallel processing. Our work builds on this paradigm and introduces latent reasoning for progressive score refinement. Reasoning-enhanced recommendation broadly follows explicit or latent paradigms. Explicit methods~ use LLMs~ to generate textual reasoning chains, but incur substantial inference overhead. Latent methods instead perform multi-step computation in continuous hidden spaces~; for example, LatentR$^3$~ optimizes compact latent reasoning in LLM-based recommenders through reinforcement learning. Within the latent paradigm, existing methods target different recommendation interfaces and learning objectives. CARE~ refines autoregressive semantic-ID generation, whereas ReaRec, CaLR, and related methods~ refine user representations for next-item recommendation. OnePiece~ covers retrieval and ranking through grouped candidate interaction and progressive supervision. Its full ranking configuration trains a set-wise backbone end to end, allows interaction among candidates, and uses contextual inputs defined by the business setting. Refiner instead formulates each reasoning step as an additive correction to candidate scores, while preserving isolated candidate processing and training only lightweight modules. To address the key design challenges, incorporates three components: a residual reasoning mechanism that accumulates score corrections across reasoning steps to align latent computation with the ranking objective; history queries that retain compact global user context during candidate-specific reasoning; and a parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework that adapts established ranking backbones without retraining the full model. Experiments on two public datasets, a large-scale offline industrial dataset, and an online A/B test show consistent improvements over the evaluated baselines, while further analyses indicate gains across different user and item groups. For future work, one promising direction is adaptive reasoning depth selection. Refiner currently applies a fixed reasoning depth to all instances, although simple cases may require fewer refinement steps while harder cases may benefit from additional computation. Future work may investigate adaptive depth allocation or stopping mechanisms that balance refinement quality and inference cost. The evaluation has two main limitations. Differences in task interfaces and backbone requirements restrict the number of directly comparable methods for latent reasoning. A fair comparison with OnePiece~ requires a backbone that supports interaction among candidates and contextual inputs defined by its business setting, which are unavailable in our current production setup. Nevertheless, the industrial offline results and online A/B test provide evidence of Refiner's practical effectiveness in the evaluated production setting.
