Conversational recommender systems support item discovery through natural-language interaction. Unlike conventional recommender systems that rely primarily on static profiles or historical interactions, a CRS can progressively infer user needs from multi-turn dialogue and revise its recommendations as additional preferences and constraints are expressed~. Existing CRS research has largely focused on domains such as movies~, music~, restaurants~, and e-commerce products~, where the primary objective is often to identify items that align with subjective preferences. Job recommendation presents a substantially different problem because recommendation quality depends not only on preference matching, but also on candidate eligibility, occupational requirements, and the temporal validity of job postings. Traditional job recommendation systems commonly use content-based matching between candidate profiles and job descriptions , graph-based collaborative models , or combinations of structured and textual features. Although these approaches are scalable, they generally treat candidate qualifications and job requirements as fixed profile attributes and provide limited support for preferences that emerge incrementally during a conversation . This limitation is consequential because job seekers frequently express incomplete, evolving, or negotiable requirements. For example, a seeker may initially request an entry-level software position in a specific location, later indicate willingness to relocate, and subsequently specify a minimum salary or a preference for remote work. A conversational system must therefore integrate preferences and constraints distributed across multiple turns rather than match a job against a single static query. Job recommendation also differs from recommendation in lower-stakes domains in several important respects. First, unsuitable recommendations may affect a seeker's income, mobility, safety, and longer-term career trajectory. Second, job matching involves eligibility conditions such as education, experience, location, age range, and occasionally employer-specified demographic requirements. Third, the system must account for softer considerations, including salary expectations, industry preferences, work environment, career development, field-work requirements, and tolerance for sales targets. Fourth, job postings are temporally bounded, making application deadlines an important component of recommendation validity. Finally, real-world job metadata is frequently incomplete, inconsistently structured, or linguistically heterogeneous . These difficulties are amplified in low-resource settings, where job descriptions and user queries may combine English, Bangla, Romanized Bangla, local abbreviations, and domain-specific terminology . In this work, we study conversational job recommendation for Bangladesh in a seeker--expert setting. A job seeker communicates qualifications, preferences, constraints, and career concerns through a multi-turn dialogue, while the system must retrieve suitable jobs from a large and heterogeneous collection of postings. Our setting is motivated by the Bangladeshi labor market, where conversational queries may include standard English, Romanized Bangla, and Bangla--English code-mixing. Job postings may similarly combine structured metadata with free-text descriptions, English technical terms, local job titles, and abbreviations such as `SR' for Sales Representative or `FMCG' for fast-moving consumer goods. These characteristics create a retrieval problem in which semantic relevance, lexical overlap, structured compatibility, and linguistic variation must be considered jointly. A central challenge in this setting concerns the treatment of user constraints. A common strategy is to interpret extracted preferences as hard predicates and remove every posting that fails at least one requirement. Such filtering is attractive because it appears to reduce the search space and prevent infeasible recommendations. However, it assumes that dialogue-level preference extraction is accurate, that conversational phrases correspond directly to normalized database fields, and that every expressed preference should be treated as inviolable. These assumptions are often unrealistic. A free-text education preference such as `degree in accounting,' for example, may not match a structured field containing `Bachelor,' even though the two descriptions are compatible. Similarly, missing or unnormalized salary and experience fields may cause a suitable posting to be rejected. Once the ground-truth job has been removed by binary filtering, no subsequent retrieval model can recover it. We therefore investigate whether conversational job recommendation is better formulated as a soft-constraint ranking problem. Rather than eliminating candidates, our proposed Weighted Soft-Constraint-Aware Ranking framework, W-SCAR, assigns each posting graded utilities for experience, location, education, and salary compatibility. These utilities are combined with sparse lexical relevance and dense semantic relevance through a multi-criteria decision-making procedure. Criterion weights are determined using the Analytic Hierarchy Process, and candidates are ranked using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution. This formulation preserves the influence of user constraints while allowing a strong semantic or lexical match to remain recoverable under moderate preference mismatches, incomplete metadata, or extraction errors. Early job recommendation systems formulated recommendation as a profile matching problem between job seekers and job descriptions. Traditional approaches primarily relied on keyword extraction from unstructured job postings, clustering techniques, and historical user interactions~. Subsequent work incorporated natural language signals to model semantic similarity between resumes and job descriptions . As recruitment platforms expanded, graph-based methods became increasingly popular for modeling user--job interactions while alleviating data sparsity and cold-start issues . More recently, several studies have explored converting structured interaction graphs into textual representations to enable reasoning with Large Language Models . Despite these advances, job recommendation differs from traditional recommendation tasks because successful matching must satisfy the preferences of both employers and job seekers. Consequently, recommendation quality depends not only on semantic relevance but also on satisfying hard constraints such as salary requirements, geographic location, and working hours . Existing recommendation models often optimize similarity without explicitly enforcing such constraints, resulting in infeasible recommendations. Recent studies have therefore incorporated constraint-aware reasoning into job recommendation. Case-based reasoning frameworks have been proposed to jointly model soft and hard constraints while providing interpretable recommendations . In parallel, hybrid lexical-semantic retrieval methods improve candidate retrieval by combining exact term matching with semantic similarity . However, these systems remain largely static and cannot iteratively refine user preferences through interaction. CRS address the limitations of static recommendation by eliciting user preferences through multi-turn dialogue . Early CRS models demonstrated the effectiveness of interactive preference elicitation but often struggled with complex user intents and negative feedback . These challenges are particularly important in recruitment, where both recruiters and job seekers typically refine their preferences during the recommendation process . Although task-oriented dialogue systems have achieved promising results in constrained domains such as e-commerce , applying them to recruitment requires substantially richer contextual reasoning . Recent advances in LLMs have significantly expanded the capabilities of conversational recommendation . LLMs enable recommendation to be formulated as a language understanding and reasoning task rather than relying solely on interaction matrices . Prior studies demonstrate strong performance in explanation generation, conversational reasoning, and zero-shot recommendation . To further improve reliability, recent work has investigated chain-of-thought reasoning, reflective recommendation, multi-agent collaboration, and retrieval-augmented dialogue grounding . Nevertheless, these systems are predominantly evaluated on clean, high-resource datasets and do not explicitly consider noisy, code-mixed user interactions. Information retrieval remains a critical component of conversational recommendation because the quality of downstream reasoning depends on the relevance of retrieved candidates. However, retrieval in low-resource and code-mixed languages remains challenging . On many online platforms, users communicate using Romanized, code-mixed text such as Banglish, introducing spelling variation, transliteration ambiguity, and inconsistent language usage. The scarcity of annotated corpora and linguistic resources further complicates retrieval in these settings . Existing lexical retrieval methods are sensitive to transliteration and spelling variations, whereas dense retrieval models often struggle to align multilingual representations under code-mixed inputs . Moreover, benchmark studies have shown that retrieval performance on clean datasets does not necessarily generalize to noisy, zero-shot scenarios . Consequently, neither sparse nor dense retrieval models consistently support conversational recommendation over code-mixed user queries. Existing work has studied code-mixed retrieval and conversational recommendation largely as separate problems. However, limited attention has been given to how structured user constraints should be incorporated when preferences are expressed through noisy, code-mixed, multi-turn dialogue. In particular, hard filtering may be brittle when extracted preferences and job attributes are incomplete or lexically mismatched. Our work addresses this gap by jointly evaluating sparse and dense retrieval under English and Banglish queries and by introducing W-SCAR, which integrates lexical relevance, semantic relevance, and graded constraint compatibility within a unified ranking framework.
