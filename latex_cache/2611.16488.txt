However, this comes at a cost that is computationally and energetically prohibitive. We propose the LLM Active Approximation Model , a hybrid retrieval framework that introduces a lightweight learned predictor which serves as an intermediary between semantic retrieval and LLM retrieval. LAAM is initialized from a small set of LLM labeled documents and subsequently refined through an active learning loop which uses an LLM to label a small number of documents and incrementally update LAAM. By concentrating LLM evaluations on the most informative documents, the framework substantially reduces inference cost while preserving retrieval effectiveness. We evaluate the proposed framework on TREC Robust 2004 using Lucene with BM25 retrieval and DeepSeek v3 as the LLM, under three levels of query verbosity. Rather than committing to either inexpensive lexical retrieval or exhaustive LLM reranking, our framework exposes a continuous spectrum of operating points along the energy--accuracy curve, allowing practitioners to select how to trade retrieval quality with computational budget. This establishes energy consumption as a first-class retrieval metric and offers a practical foundation for cost-aware retrieval over verbose information needs. Search engines serve as the primary method for finding information on the World Wide Web. They employ complex algorithms to collect, index, and rank web pages based on various factors, aiming to provide relevant results to user queries. LLMs are increasingly embedded inside the search stack. They are used for query and document expansion , serve as relevance rankers, where fine-tuned LLMs outperform traditional retrieval models , and can completely replace search engines and provide summaries of the relevant information. Current user studies reveal a complementary division of labor between the two technologies: search engines are preferred for quick factual lookups, while LLMs are favored for language-intensive queries that require contextual understanding . The semantic gains of LLMs carry a steep cost in time and energy. Lexical retrieval answers in tens of milliseconds, whereas LLM inference can take several seconds per query . The energy gap is even larger. A single Google search emits an estimated 0.2 grams of CO$_2$ and consumes roughly 0.3~Wh, whereas a ChatGPT request has been estimated at 2.9~Wh, approximately ten times more . At the document level, processing a single document with a BERT-based model has been estimated to emit approximately 5 grams of CO$_2$ . Reranking hundreds of candidate documents per query with an LLM therefore multiplies the cost of retrieval by several orders of magnitude. This has motivated calls for ``green'' information retrieval research . Despite this, the trade-off between retrieval quality and computational cost is rarely treated as a first-order concern in retrieval system design. This tension is sharpest for long queries. Typical web queries average only about 2.4 words and are thus often ambiguous . Verbose queries can disambiguate the information need, but they clash with the keyword-matching strategy of lexical retrieval . LLMs, in contrast, are far better suited to interpreting such queries, owing to their ability to capture language semantics . As a result, the queries for which LLMs offer the greatest semantic benefit are those for which exhaustive LLM evaluation is most expensive. Here we focus on the retrieval model, which matches user queries to relevant documents in the index. We present a hybrid retrieval component that exploits the advantages of keyword-based search engines and of LLMs in different parts of the retrieval process so as to save energy while maximizing retrieval accuracy. To achieve this, we propose the LLM Active Approximation Model . In the proposed system, a query is first processed by a standard lexical retriever , which produces an initial ranked list of candidate documents. LAAM then acts as an intermediate layer between the lexical retriever and the LLM. A lightweight predictor learns to approximate the relevance judgments of the LLM from attributes of the query and the document, so that most documents never need to be evaluated by the LLM. Instead, a model initialized from a small set of LLM-labeled documents is refined through active learning, whereby at each iteration the system labels a single document using the LLM and updates the model. A final ranking that approximates LLM ranking is produced by the model, without having to label a large number of documents. A key property of the proposed system is that the number of LLM evaluations is a controllable parameter, allowing a balance of accuracy with energy use. Rather than committing to either cheap lexical retrieval or costly full reranking, the system therefore allows practitioners to tune the balance between quality, energy use, and budget for long-query retrieval according to their deployment constraints. We evaluate the framework on 50 TREC Robust 2004 queries using Lucene with BM25 and DeepSeek , under three query formulations: title, description, and narrative. We show that for verbose narrative queries LAAM reaches approximately 87\ In recent years, the integration of search engines with LLMs has garnered significant attention, especially with regards to retrieval models. Retrieval models are at the heart of search engine architectures, responsible for identifying and ranking relevant documents in response to user queries. Over the years, various methodologies have been developed, ranging from early probabilistic approaches to models that leverage neural models . Search engines rely on indexing and ranking algorithms to retrieve relevant documents based on user queries. Most of the time, they work well with short queries . However, they often struggle with long queries that require nuances and context . In such situations, long queries can help disambiguate, but such queries do not work well with the search engines' keyword-matching strategy. On the other hand, neural model can capture semantics, enabling them to interpret meaning of long queries more effectively . Early search engines relied on term-matching. Vector space models are a classical approach to document representation, where text documents are represented as vectors of words . This was later improved through the Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency weighting scheme, which is a probabilistic method to estimate the importance of a word within a document relative to the entire collection. Those Boolean retrieval models, which relied on exact matches of keywords, were gradually replaced by probabilistic models such as BM25 , which introduced statistical approaches to rank documents by computing a nonlinear formulation of document properties. With the rise of the web, search engines adopted more sophisticated techniques such as keyword-based search optimization and leveraging the web's link structure to rank retrieved results . LLMs enhance search engine retrieval beyond traditional algorithms. LLMs have been used for query expansion and pseudo-queries as well as document expansion . Ji et al. proposed using LLMs in two-phase method with offline keyword extraction and real-time query matching, improving efficiency and accuracy. By using an inverted index to narrow the search scope, their method substantially reduces the LLM's average response time while slightly improving answer quality. Feng et al. introduce INTER, a framework that combines a retrieval module and an LLM to refine queries iteratively. In their design, the retrieval module expands query knowledge using LLM-generated collections while the LLM refines its prompts using the retrieved documents, leading to more accurate retrieval. To address the limitations of LLMs, such as hallucinations and outdated data, Lei et al. developed Corpus-Steered Query Expansion , merging LLM expansions with BM25-extracted key sentences to enhance relevance and retrieval effectiveness. These advancements demonstrate that LLMs significantly boost retrieval performance. A comparison of the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs and traditional search engines shows the advantages of each, and highlights the potential for developing hybrid models that leverage the strengths of both semantic search engines and LLMs. User preferences regarding search engines or LLMs are influenced by numerous elements, including the kind of information desired, the length, context, and complexity of the query, and the user's expertise or comfort level with the technology . Analyzing these preferences entails evaluating user satisfaction based on the tools' speed, accuracy, and overall user experience. Caramancion et al. compared user preferences of search engines and LLMs in 20 different scenarios using a survey of 100 participants based in the US. The findings show that search engines are preferred for factual queries due to their breadth of indexed information and quick access to multiple sources, while LLMs are favored for subjective tasks and language-intensive queries due to their conversational and contextual understanding capabilities. LLMs and search engines differ in the quality of retrieval, especially for complex and long queries: lexical search engines struggle to interpret verbose queries whose terms do not contribute equally to the information need , while LLMs can follow the nuanced relevance criteria expressed in such queries and better capture their semantics . Sun et al. demonstrated that, when appropriately instructed, LLMs can act as effective zero-shot relevance rerankers, delivering competitive and in some cases superior ranking quality compared to state-of-the-art supervised retrieval models on standard IR benchmarks. Ma et al. showed that fine-tuned LLMs for retrieval outperform traditional retrieval models, demonstrating stronger generalization while directly processing long documents. This capability eliminates the need for the heuristic segmentation and pooling strategies commonly employed by conventional search engines to rank long documents. The aforementioned examples provide evidence that LLMs add a layer of quality to the search engine system. Xiong et al.~ noted that search engines excel in rapid indexing and retrieval, providing immediate access to information for fact-based queries, while LLMs take longer due to computational demands. Search engines typically deliver results within 10--100 milliseconds, while LLMs take 1--10 seconds or more per query. For example, OpenAI ChatGPT has an average response latency of 3 seconds . The cost of LLMs and of search engines can also be measured by their carbon footprint. Gultekin et al. found that BERT-based models consume approximately 5 grams of CO per document, while Gombiner et al.~ found that a Google search query generates 0.2 grams of CO. Active learning with LLMs is particularly beneficial in scenarios where labeled data is scarce or expensive to obtain, as it optimizes the use of available resources. For example, active learning can leverage the LLMs' retrieval quality and replace human feedback with LLM feedback in pseudo-relevance feedback scenarios. LLMs have been used as judges, with active learning utilized to reduce the cost of annotation. For example, Zhang et al. demonstrated that LLMs can act as annotators within an active learning framework, where the model selectively identifies informative samples for annotation. Their approach showed that task-specific models trained on only a few hundred LLM-generated labels can outperform the teacher LLM, significantly reducing annotation costs. Xiao et al. introduced a collaborative active learning framework that leverages LLMs as weak annotators to reduce human labeling requirements. Their approach combines model predictions with LLM-generated labels, enabling efficient training with fewer manually annotated examples. Similarly, we leverage selective LLM feedback through active learning to reduce the cost of LLM-based relevance estimation, but we use it for reranking, rather than annotation. Search engines face challenges in interpreting long queries because the terms within a long query do not contribute equally to the user's information need . As a result, users often submit short queries , which provide limited contextual information and make it more difficult for retrieval systems to infer the underlying search intent. For instance, an analysis of the MSN search log, which sampled roughly 15 million queries over a month, revealed an average query length of 2.4 words, with 90.3\ Throughout the years, short queries retrieval typically outperformed their longer counterparts when processed by the same information retrieval model. Recognizing their potential, recent research has focused on developing techniques to enhance search effectiveness for long queries. As an example, Yan et al. investigated long queries that contain sentence-level structure rather than isolated keywords and proposed an HMM-based approach that exploits this structure to estimate the importance of different query terms and improve query representation. Kumaran and Carvalho employ learning techniques to rank subsets of the original long query using quality prediction metrics. In addition, Bendersky and Croft show that extracting key concepts from long queries can improve ranking performance. A growing line of work treats the number of LLM calls as an explicit, controllable resource rather than an afterthought, echoing the framing we adopt for LAAM. Kim et al. propose BAGEL, which is applied to passages and models the relevance distribution over an entire embedding space with a query-specific Gaussian Process fit to sparse LLM relevance scores, then iteratively chooses which passages to submit to the LLM by balancing exploitation of high-confidence regions against exploration of uncertain ones. Huang et al. take a related but distinct angle with TS-SetRank, arguing that a document's relevance is not fixed but context-dependent, varying according to which other candidates it is batched with and how they are ordered, and use uncertainty-aware sampling to estimate this ``contextual relevance'' efficiently rather than scoring every batch composition exhaustively. Both frameworks, like LAAM, replace exhaustive LLM evaluation with a model-guided selection policy, but they target the sampling/ordering of what is shown to the LLM rather than learning a surrogate that predicts the LLM's judgment for unlabeled documents, as LAAM does. Other work reduces LLM cost by shrinking what is sent to the LLM rather than how many documents are sent. Li et al. propose KeyB2, which selects only the most informative blocks of a long document for LLM scoring instead of the whole text. Otero and Parapar instead constrain the LLM's role within a classical pseudo-relevance feedback pipeline and use the LLM as a lightweight filter over the initial top-$k$ documents, retaining documents it judges relevant before estimating an RM3 expansion model from the filtered set. These approaches illustrate several complementary strategies for controlling LLM cost in retrieval. Specifically, they select which documents a priori to evaluate, how much of each document to evaluate, and what role the LLM plays within a larger pipeline. None learn to approximate the LLM using a lightweight model, as LAAM does. This research proposes a document retrieval framework that combines traditional search engines with Large Language Models to improve long-query retrieval while controlling computational cost. The framework learns to rerank documents that were ranked using BM25 using a small number of LLM-ranked documents. The system reduces the reliance on the LLM while preserving high retrieval quality by iteratively refining the LAAM. The hypothesis underlying the proposed model is that it is possible to take advantage of the strengths of the individual systems without succumbing to their drawbacks. Specifically, BM25 requires low computational cost but has lower accuracy, while the LLMs are a superior ranking function, provided at significant computational cost. In the following we explain the proposed model, with reference to the flowchart in Figure~. The framework begins by processing a query through a traditional search engine . Here we use Lucene with BM25 scoring, though other engines and scoring functions are possible. The search engine ranks the document corpus based on keyword relevance, producing an initial ranking with associated relevance scores. The framework leverages an LLM to generate a high-quality relevance score for a subset of the documents returned by lexical retriever. In the first iteration we select an initial training set according to the methods described below, so as to ensure coverage across the ranking range. These sampled documents are scored by the LLM and are then used to train the LAAM. These tasks are shown in the Doc Selector and LLM blocks of Figure~. For each document--query pair, the system extracts a feature representation combining lexical and semantic signals. Lexical relevance is captured by the cosine similarity between the TF--IDF vectors of the query and the document, computed over a shared vocabulary of unigrams and bigrams with English stop-words removed, and the query-term overlap ratio . Semantic relevance is captured by dense sentence-transformer embeddings of the query and document. This block is denoted as Feature Extractor in Figure~. The core of the proposed model is LAAM, an active-learning-based approximation framework that reduces the cost of LLM-based relevance assessment. LAAM learns to approximate LLM relevance judgments via a model applied to the feature representation described above, thereby reducing the need for costly LLM evaluations of all documents. We selected a Gradient Boosting regression model for LAAM. The choice of Gradient Boosting was motivated by empirical evaluation against a Random Forest regressor , where Gradient Boosting demonstrated superior predictive accuracy. Following scoring of the initial document set, the system enters an iterative active learning refinement phase. For each unevaluated document, the LAAM estimates its LLM score , which is then linearly scaled into the scoring range of the lexical retrieval. The system calculates the disagreement between the rescaled predicted score and the original search engine score for all documents and selects the single document with the highest disagreement between the predicted and actual lexical retrieval score in each iteration . This pure exploitation strategy prioritizes documents where LAAM most strongly disagrees with the search engine ranking. The selected document is then evaluated by the LLM, and is added to the LAAM training set. Note that we also test an approach balancing exploration and exploitation. We prioritize documents exhibiting the highest predicted disagreement between the LLM and lexical retrieval scores to ensure LAAM receives training labels that target areas of maximum discrepancy between the two models. The rationale for this is to prioritize documents which are most likely to benefit from LLM-based ranking. Other active learning acquisition strategies, such as uncertainty sampling based on score variance, remain promising avenues for future work. After each LLM evaluation, the LAAM is fine-tuned on the newly labeled example using incremental learning. This continuous refinement improves prediction accuracy over time and reduces future disagreements. The iterative active learning cycle, consisting of greedy selection, LLM evaluation, score adjustment, and model refinement, continues until a convergence criterion is met. This convergence can be reached when discrepancies between predicted and actual rankings fall below a predefined threshold or the maximum number of allowed scored LLM documents is reached. However, these gains come at a substantial computational and energy cost, since LLM-based relevance estimation can be several orders of magnitude more expensive than traditional lexical retrieval methods . Existing retrieval pipelines therefore face a difficult trade-off: relying solely on lexical methods sacrifices semantic understanding, whereas reranking retrieved documents with an LLM is expensive. From this perspective, retrieval becomes a resource-allocation problem whereby, given a limited computational budget, which documents are worth spending an expensive LLM evaluation on, and which can be ranked adequately using inexpensive lexical methods? The proposed framework addresses this problem by learning to approximate LLM relevance judgments and allocating LLM evaluations only to documents where they are expected to provide the greatest benefit. The resulting system approaches the accuracy of exhaustive LLM reranking while consuming only a small fraction of the computational and energy cost. Our central finding is that selective LLM evaluation, guided by a disagreement-based LAAM policy, recovers most of the retrieval quality of exhaustive LLM reranking at a fraction of its computational cost and in some cases surpasses it. For example, on narrative queries the framework reaches $@10 = 0.412$ at $K = 50$ (78\ We attribute this to the hybrid system's ability to combine the lexical prior provided by BM25 with targeted semantic corrections from the LLM. Selective evaluation concentrates the computational effort on the documents most likely to be ranked dissimilarly to LLM rankings, capturing the majority of the gains available from exhaustive reranking while avoiding the cost of evaluating every candidate. The resulting performance demonstrates that a relatively small number of strategically chosen LLM evaluations is sufficient to recover most of the improvement obtainable from full semantic reranking. Performance differences across query types highlight an important limitation of purely lexical retrieval methods. For short title and description queries, BM25 is often sufficient because the information need is expressed through a small set of keywords. In these cases, even advanced semantic models have limited additional context from which to infer relevance. In contrast, narrative queries provide detailed descriptions of the user's intent, including relevance criteria, exclusions, negations, and nuanced distinctions that cannot be captured through term matching alone. LLMs are particularly effective in this setting because they can interpret these semantic constraints and reason about document relevance . Our framework is designed to exploit the arbitrage between LLMs and semantic retrieval. When this difference is unavailable, the advantage of our proposed system diminishes. We highlight the importance of evaluating retrieval effectiveness alongside energy consumption, as accuracy gains can come at substantial computational cost. The results reveal a clear energy--accuracy trade-off: increasing the LLM evaluation budget improves average P@10, but the improvements become progressively smaller relative to the additional energy required. This produces a non-linear curve characterized by diminishing returns rather than a proportional relationship between energy expenditure and retrieval quality. Our framework occupies an intermediate region between lexical retrieval and LLM reranking. While full reranking achieves the highest average P@10, it requires 1--2 orders of magnitude more energy than our approach. These findings suggest that retrieval systems should adapt their evaluation budget to query complexity. Short keyword-based queries can be handled efficiently with lexical retrieval, whereas long queries justify a larger semantic reranking budget because they benefit substantially more from LLM-based relevance assessment. This flexibility is a key advantage of the proposed framework, as it enables the accuracy on long queries to be scaled according to a predefined computational budget. Rather than committing to either purely lexical retrieval or LLM reranking, the system provides a continuous spectrum of operating points, allowing practitioners to select the most appropriate balance between effectiveness and energy consumption for their deployment constraints. Our work has several limitations. First, all experiments use DeepSeek , chosen for its favorable accuracy--cost trade-off. Better reasoning models may achieve comparable accuracy with fewer LLM calls, potentially enabling earlier stopping within the active learning loop. Models that emphasize energy efficiency may reduce the gap in accuracy between the proposed system and full reranking. TREC Robust 2004 is a public benchmark whose documents and relevance judgments are widely distributed online and are thus possibly included in the training corpora of contemporary LLMs. As a result, some of DeepSeek's relevance judgments may reflect memorized patterns rather than pure inference, potentially making absolute performance estimates optimistic compared to fully unseen corpora. This is a general limitation of LLM-based evaluation on widely available benchmarks. However, its impact is partially mitigated in our setting, as the framework does not consistently outperform BM25 on title and description query types, suggesting that any potential memorization does not lead to consistent improvements in every case. While per-query energy consumption for BM25 and the hybrid framework is measured directly at the hardware level using the Linux tool, the same is not possible for the full DeepSeek reranking configuration. Due to the lack of local access to the underlying model infrastructure, DeepSeek inference was executed via a remote API, preventing direct measurement under identical hardware conditions. As a result, its energy consumption is estimated using the infrastructure-aware methodology of Jegham et al.~. While this methodology provides a principled and reproducible estimate grounded in prior work, it cannot fully replicate direct hardware-level measurement under identical experimental conditions. Consequently, energy comparisons involving the exhaustive DeepSeek configuration should be interpreted as estimates rather than exact measurements, although the reported trends are expected to remain representative. Several directions exist for future extensions of this work. First, in the current framework, the LAAM is trained independently for each query. The LAAM is intended to identify documents for which the BM25 ranking may not accurately reflect true relevance and where LLM evaluation is most likely to improve ranking quality. Transferring knowledge from previously processed queries could provide a stronger initialization, enabling the LAAM to recognize such cases more quickly and reducing the number of LLM evaluations required to reach a given level of retrieval quality. Second, the dissimilar results across query types motivate an upstream classifier that routes short queries directly to BM25 and reserves the hybrid pipeline for long queries, translating the verbosity-sensitivity established above into a system efficiency gain. Third, the current evaluation was limited to TREC Robust 2004. Testing the framework on newer or private collections that are less likely to overlap with LLM training data would provide stronger evidence that the observed gains generalize beyond this benchmark. Finally, although this work focuses on document reranking, the proposed architecture could be applied within retrieval-augmented generation systems. Using LAAM to reduce retrieval costs while maintaining retrieval quality may improve the overall efficiency of end-to-end RAG pipelines.
