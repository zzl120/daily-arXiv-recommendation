We measure pretrained rerankers, a hosted typed decision model , eight open decision models and distilled students on GEO-Bench queries answered by ten engines that receive the same candidate sources in their prompt. Where several engines answer the same query, they agree with one another at a within-group Spearman correlation of 0.704. Jev and two open 27B decision models, used without training, are the strongest scorers at 0.575 to 0.578. A Qwen3-Reranker-4B fine-tuned with low-rank adapters on teacher labels for other GEO-Bench queries, released as GEO Citation Ranker 4B, reaches 0.542 to 0.549 across three seeds, and its differences with the 27B models and with Jev are not distinguishable from zero on 229 queries. Most of the remaining distance between the student and Jev disappears when Jev scores one passage at a time, as the student does. Students trained on teacher labels and on observed engine citations reach the same level, and labels from Jev alone match the two-teacher target at 0.6B. We release the model, the benchmark, the predictions of every reported scorer and the code. Work on generative engine optimization asks, for a given query, which of the candidate pages an answer engine will draw on, and how much . Measuring engines directly answers it, but each measurement is paid, slow and non-deterministic, so practitioners increasingly use a model to predict the answer before any engine is queried. Pretrained rerankers are free to run and were trained for topical relevance . LLM judges prompted with a rubric can rank sources, at the cost of one generation per group . Typed decision models, such as the hosted Jev , return a probability distribution over a fixed scale for each question asked about a state. Several open decision models were published on the Hugging Face Hub after Jev's release . We know of no measurement of decision models or distilled rerankers against the citations of answer engines. This paper measures them on one benchmark of 229 GEO-Bench queries . Each query is answered by one to ten engines that receive the same five candidate sources in their prompt, and the label of a source is its citation share in the answer. For the 150 queries answered by several engines, the benchmark also measures how far the engines agree with each other, which gives every scorer a reference that comes from the engines themselves. The paper makes three contributions. First, it reports the agreement between engines and the level of 36 scorer configurations on the same 1220 engine-query groups, with intervals clustered by query and paired differences . Second, it shows that two open 27B decision models match the hosted judge without training, and that presenting the five candidates together accounts for most of the distance between Jev and a single-passage student. Third, it distils teacher labels into Qwen3-Reranker students at 0.6B, 4B and 8B, releases the 4B student as GEO Citation Ranker 4B, and separates the effect of teacher choice, label volume, capacity and observed citations. introduced GEO and GEO-Bench and reported citation gains from content edits. Later work measured conversational SEO on its own benchmark , ranked the factors of competition between two sources , separated being cited from shaping the answer , and repaired document-specific citation failures with agents . Adversarial manipulation of conversational search rankings was demonstrated by . re-measured the 2023 content levers on ten current engines and found that query-agnostic content scores predict citation only weakly. This paper uses the engine answers collected for that study. Generated answers often cite sources that do not support them , and citation quality can be evaluated and trained for . LLMs also use the middle of a long context less than its ends , which predicts an effect of source position in our prompts. LLMs rerank well zero-shot , and their rankings can be distilled into smaller open models or used to generate training data , following the general recipe of knowledge distillation . Qwen3-Reranker frames relevance as a yes or no answer scored by token probabilities . We train with a pairwise logistic loss and low-rank adapters . LLM-as-a-judge is standard for graded criteria , and judges are sensitive to the order in which candidates are presented . Typed decision models were introduced for binary agent-evaluation criteria ; we apply them to a graded, comparative criterion. An engine $e$ answers query $q$ from five candidate sources $s_1, ,s_5$ given in its prompt in that order. A scorer sees $q$ and the source texts, never the engine's answer, and must rank the five sources by the citation share each receives in the answer. The label $y_{e,q,i}$ of source $s_i$ is its position-adjusted word count share: the words of each answer sentence citing $s_i$, weighted by the sentence's position in the answer, summed and normalised over the five sources . For a scorer with predictions $ y$, the score of a group $ $ is the Spearman correlation between $ y_{ }$ and $y_{e,q, }$ over the five sources, with average ranks for ties and zero for a constant prediction. The benchmark score is the mean over groups whose five labels are not all equal. For a group $ $, the cross-engine agreement is the score of the predictor $ y_i = _{e' e} y_{e',q,i}$, the mean label that the other engines answering $q$ give to source $s_i$. It is defined for queries answered by at least two engines. Cross-engine agreement uses information that no scorer has, namely other engines' answers to the same prompt. It measures how much of a group's ranking is shared across engines. It serves as a reference for the scorers and is not an upper bound on them. The 229 queries are the GEO-Bench test queries for which collected engine answers, with the prompt, decoding settings and citation parser described in that work . Each source is represented by the first 320 words of its raw text. The ten engines are listed with their vendor and coverage in Appendix~. Seventy-nine queries are answered by one engine, 50 by five, 23 by seven to nine and 77 by all ten, which gives 1220 non-degenerate groups over 1145 passages. Every engine receives the query and the five sources in its prompt, and no engine retrieves documents. The benchmark therefore measures citation under controlled retrieval, the setting of GEO-Bench. We score the NIM reranker , bge-reranker-v2-m3 and Qwen3-Reranker at 0.6B, 4B and 8B with their default inference code and no training, on the same 320-word passages. A typed decision model receives a state and a set of questions and returns, for each question, a probability distribution over the answer levels . We ask one six-level question per passage, from level 0 to level 5 , and the score is the expectation over levels. The wording is given in Appendix~. In the listwise protocol the state holds the query and the five passages and the five questions are asked in one call; in the pointwise protocol the state holds the query and one passage. Passages are truncated to 250 words in the state, which is shorter than the text engines and rerankers receive. We score the hosted Jev 1.13 (snapshot 20260917) through its API, and eight open models from the Hugging Face Hub with the runtime of their model cards. The eight were chosen before any was scored, as the most downloaded or best placed on their own model cards among those that accept an ordered score question. The same state and questions are used for every model, with no prompt tuning. The training data are 757 groups of five sources, one for each GEO-Bench test query outside the evaluation set that has five sources, prepared with the same 320-word recipe. None has engine labels. Two teachers label each group. Jev scores it in the listwise protocol, for 0.10 USD over the whole set. An LLM judge produces synthetic labels for batches of six groups, on a 0 to 100 scale, with the rubric of Appendix~. The two teachers agree at a mean per-group Spearman of 0.759. The default target is the mean of the two teachers, each rescaled to $ $; ablations use one teacher. Students are Qwen3-Reranker models at 0.6B, 4B and 8B scored by the log-odds of ``yes'' against ``no'' after an instruction asking whether an answer engine would cite the passage. A rank-16 adapter on all attention and feed-forward projections is trained with a pairwise logistic loss within each group, over the pairs whose targets differ by more than 0.05 and weighted by that difference, for four epochs at learning rate $10^{-4}$. The 4B student trains in about two hours on one RTX~3090. The 4B student trained with seed 42 is released as GEO Citation Ranker 4B. Intervals are 95\ resampled together , with 1000 resamples and a fixed seed. Every comparison between two scorers is a paired difference on the groups both cover, and we call two scorers indistinguishable when the 95\ tested by two one-sided tests, which require the 90\ margin . As a second metric we report top-1 accuracy, the share of groups where the scorer's top source is a most-cited source. Because the 77 queries answered by all ten engines contribute most groups, we also report the mean per engine and per query. Students trained on observed citations are evaluated by five-fold cross-validation over the evaluation queries, with a fixed assignment of queries to folds, and each group is scored only by the model that did not see its query. Engines receive five fixed sources in their prompt, so the benchmark measures which provided source an engine cites, and not whether a search engine retrieves a page. This removes retrieval noise from the label. It does not establish that the ranking transfers to engines that search the web, which would require answers collected from live search products. The student targets combine Jev and one LLM judge with one rubric. At 0.6B, Jev labels alone train a student as good as the two-teacher target. Jev is a hosted model versioned by its provider, and the open 27B decision models offer a teacher with frozen weights. Decision models see 250 words of each passage, against 320 for engines and students, which can only work against them. The benchmark is English, built from one source dataset, with five candidates per query, and four of the ten engines come from one vendor. Decision models were run with one state and one set of questions, written once and not tuned. Engines change, and the labels describe answers collected in 2026. Judge RELATIVE to the other passages. Direct relevance to the query dominates; then citable facts, statistics, quotability, apparent authority, and self-containment. Do not reward length by itself.'' The pointwise protocol asks the same question about a single passage. The judge receives a query and five passages in a seeded random order, truncated to 250 words, and returns a score from 0 to 100 per passage for the expected citation share relative to the other four. The rubric ranks, in order, direct relevance to the query, density of citable information, figures and dates, quotability, apparent authority and self-containment. It instructs the judge not to reward length, to give less to a passage redundant with a better one, and to ignore presentation order.
