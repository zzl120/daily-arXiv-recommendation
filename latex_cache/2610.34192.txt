Prior research concentrates heavily on the encoder mechanism to capture both local and global relationships among items~. .} The decoder mechanism, which ultimately determines how encoded information is transformed into user preference representations, has received comparatively little attention. Some recent research utilizes pooling-based methods~, which compress all items into a fixed-length embedding. While simple and efficient, this approach inevitably discards sequential and item-level distinctions, becoming particularly harmful in long sessions where diverse user interests emerge. As a result, pooling-based decoders struggle to preserve the temporal information necessary for accurate next-item prediction. Another line of work focuses on point-wise approaches~, which rely on attention or gating anchored on the last item to balance short-term and long-term intents. However, this design overweights recent interactions while underutilising earlier but semantically relevant ones, ultimately failing to capture the multi-interest nature of long sessions. These limitations raise an important concern: do existing decoders truly exploit the richer historical context available in long sessions? To investigate, we conduct an empirical analysis on representative models, as shown in Fig.~. Point-wise approaches assign disproportionate weight to the last few items while ignoring earlier ones; pooling-based approaches blur temporal signals across the sequence. Both weaknesses manifest in a consistent empirical pattern: when sessions are grouped by length, longer sessions produce lower accuracy across state-of-the-art models, despite containing richer historical information. Notably, this degradation is observed across architecturally distinct model families that differ in every encoder design dimension yet share the same decoder paradigm. This cross-architecture consistency cannot be attributed to any specific encoder design; rather, it implicates the shared decoder mechanism as the primary source of underperformance on longer sessions. To isolate the decoder's contribution, we adopt a controlled evaluation protocol : we hold each encoder fixed and vary only the decoder component across all baselines, enabling a direct assessment of decoder-side impact while factoring out encoder differences. The central research question is therefore: To answer this, we introduce (Multi-Interest Decoding with Adaptive Aggregation for Session-based Recommendation) }, a lightweight and encoder-agnostic framework that provides a reformulation of the decoding stage to tackle the above limitations by modeling both persistent and evolving aspects of user intent. The two empirical failure modes of existing decoders (pooling methods that conflate semantically distinct items and point-wise methods that discard all but the most recent interactions) translate into two concrete design challenges: ~How can we capture diverse persistent interests that are semantically related but temporally distant? ~How can we balance stability with plasticity in user behavior? To address these challenges, MIDA employs , which groups semantically related items via differentiable similarity thresholds, thereby preserving long-range dependencies and capturing diverse user preferences that pooling-based methods collapse. It also introduces , which disentangles user intent into stability and plasticity and fuses them through a gating mechanism conditioned on both branch representations and semantic cohesion signals , enabling fine-grained adaptation to consistent and evolving behavioral signals. Together, these components yield a decoder that efficiently utilizes history information, retains temporal structure, and models the multi-interest nature of complex sessions. We identify the in SBR, namely the systematic tendency of existing decoders to underutilize long-range historical context, and provide a lightweight, encoder-agnostic reformulation of the decoding stage that directly addresses this limitation. A differentiable clustering mechanism with regularisation is proposed for dynamic interest extraction, together with a stability--plasticity aggregation scheme that disentangles persistent and evolving user intents, emphasizing the . The framework incorporates a two-stage decoder design in which semantic similarity computation is decoupled from relational representation: Dynamic Multi-Interest Clustering operates on raw embeddings to identify similar items across temporal distances, while Adaptive Aggregation uses encoder outputs to construct attention-based session representations that capture sequential dependencies. The decoder is encoder-agnostic: it replaces only the final aggregation step of any backbone without modifying encoder parameters. Comprehensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets show that MIDA outperforms strong baselines across diverse encoder architectures. Traditional SBR methods typically rely on heuristic or shallow models such as Markov chains to model item transitions~. Recent studies increasingly focus on deep neural architectures, including recurrent networks~, attention mechanisms~, and graph neural networks~, which effectively encode complex transition dependencies within sessions. Several works aim to integrate both local and global item relations~, or leverage contrastive learning to enhance representation robustness~. Further explorations extend to Transformer-based frameworks~ and memory-augmented architectures~. Side information has also emerged as a promising direction, including temporal signals~, item descriptions~, user behaviors~, and category information~. Across these studies, the encoder receives the bulk of attention, while the decoder remains largely fixed. There is growing interest in capturing multi-interest structures within sessions. One approach segments item embeddings into multiple parts, each representing a distinct interest~. Another treats items as multi-aspect entities mapped into multiple candidate spaces corresponding to different aspects~. Dynamic interest adaptation mechanisms for automatically inferring the number of interests have also been explored~. MIDA is conceptually related to these lines of work, but differs in two important ways. First, it employs a dynamic, differentiable clustering mechanism based on within-session semantic similarity, grouping interests adaptively without a preset number or heavy overhead. Second, it applies multi-interest modeling at the and further disentangles persistent from evolving intents, enabling richer utilisation of historical information. Decoder-based recommendation methods differ in how user preferences are generated. Session-based decoders may form a user interest representation from the last interacted item~, global session aggregation~, or temporal exponential decay sum~. CTR prediction decoders score each item in a fixed candidate set via binary classifiers~. Sequential recommendation approaches such as LightGCN~ bypass explicit decoding by computing relevance via neighborhood embedding aggregation. Recent work employs Deep Q-Network agents to optimize graph connectivity~ or multi-scale rescaling of positional indices to mitigate the ``lost-in-the-middle'' problem~. Accurately capturing user interests is especially important because scoring every candidate item is impractical; imprecise interest representations can mislead the decoder~, particularly when user intents shift during a session~. MIDA addresses these challenges by reformulating the decoder as a dual-branch system grounded in within-session semantic structure. Unlike existing methods that either treat the encoder and decoder jointly or focus on encoder redesign, MIDA treats the decoder as a {first-class component} while remaining encoder-agnostic. Specifically, MIDA replaces only the final aggregation module of a backbone (e.g.\ the attention-based pooling in SR-GNN/GCE-GNN or the output projection in GRU4Rec), preserving all encoder parameters and training schedules. This modular design enables straightforward integration across heterogeneous encoder families. The combination of differentiable threshold-based clustering (without a pre-specified number of interests), a stability and plasticity dual-query design, and a semantically conditioned gating mechanism distinguishes MIDA from multi-interest methods that operate at the encoder level and from decoder methods that rely on a single fixed aggregation rule. In particular, unlike AttenMixer~, which introduces multi-level propagation layers that can conflict with rich encoders, MIDA's decoupled query construction explicitly separates semantic similarity from relational context , avoiding gradient interference while retaining the benefits of both representations. The essential novelty of MIDA lies not in assembling independently existing modules but in a principled {two-space decoupling}: clustering operates on raw item embeddings so that semantic groupings are not distorted by the relational biases the encoder has learned; encoder outputs are reserved for aggregation, preserving the sequential dependencies the encoder provides. This is an architectural consequence of separating {which} items are related from {how} they should be aggregated given session context, a distinction no existing decoder draws explicitly. We propose (ulti-nterest and Aaptive ggregation for Session-based Recommendation), a lightweight decoder framework that models both long-range, multi-interest dependencies and short-term preference shifts in SBR. We introduced MIDA, a lightweight decoder framework that addresses the decoder bottleneck in session-based recommendation by modeling both stable and evolving user intents. MIDA uses dynamic multi-interest clustering with learnable similarity thresholds to maintain semantic coherence over temporal gaps, and an adaptive aggregation scheme that separates persistent preferences from transient shifts via stability and plasticity branches. These components are fused through a gating mechanism conditioned on semantic cohesion signals, enabling the model to overcome recency bias while remaining computationally practical. Experiments on four benchmark datasets show that MIDA consistently outperforms strong baselines across encoder architectures, with particularly significant improvements on longer and semantically diverse sessions where existing methods struggle.
