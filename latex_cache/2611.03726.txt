Mobility routines can recur across users while being realized at different places and adapted to individual preferences. We present , a causal sequential recommender that composes two population-shared prototype dictionaries. At each observed prefix, soft routers retrieve pattern and spatial mixtures; a factorized multiplicative interaction couples them, feature-wise affine modulation adapts the shared representation, and a separate user-conditioned branch with an additive--projective gate retains individual variation. The prediction head combines this representation with a learned first-order transition term and ranks the complete training POI vocabulary. We evaluate on NYC and TKY using a training-only vocabulary and an original-final-target protocol that neither bridges unknown POIs nor substitutes earlier targets. The available single-seed results show competitive full-candidate ranking, while controlled analyses support the contribution of the compositional and personalized branches. Code is available at . Location-based Web platforms connect online place discovery with users' physical activities. Their ranking systems must select useful destinations from a city-wide catalog while accounting for the order, timing, and geography of past visits. Next point-of-interest recommendation formalizes one part of this problem: given an observed check-in history, rank the concrete POI that the user will visit next~. This work addresses the setting through a specific Web recommendation challenge: reusing behavioral regularities across users whose spatial contexts and individual preferences differ. The prediction concerns the immediate next place, not a route, a new-to-the-user destination, or a POI conditional on a supplied future timestamp. A useful distinction is between a routine and its realization. Consider an illustrative routine such as visiting a workplace, obtaining a meal, and then stopping at a shop. Two users may exhibit a similar ordering of activities without sharing a single POI. Conversely, the same neighborhood may host different routines at different times, and two users in the same neighborhood need not prefer the same restaurant. A model should therefore have a way to reuse population-level regularities, condition them on where a trajectory is unfolding, and retain user-specific variation. The example motivates an inductive bias; it does not presume that latent clusters are already validated semantic labels such as ``commuting'' or ``shopping.'' Existing methods provide complementary ingredients. Recurrent and attention-based models encode temporal and spatial dependencies~. Long- and short-term preference methods distinguish persistent tastes from recent behavior~. Graph and hypergraph approaches exploit transitions and collaborative relations across users~. Recent work also explicitly models latent preferences or personalized spatio-temporal clusters~. Consequently, neither sharing information across users nor introducing clusters is, by itself, the distinction of this work. Our focus is the : how two reusable dictionaries interact at each trajectory prefix, and how their joint representation is adapted to a user before ranking places. This focus yields three design questions. First, a trajectory may move between routines rather than belonging permanently to one cluster. The assignment should therefore depend on its current prefix and remain soft. Second, spatial context should affect the expression of a pattern, rather than simply contribute an unrelated feature to a concatenation. Third, shared structure should not force users to have identical representations. An additive residual and a user-conditioned interaction offer different ways to preserve individuality, and their relative influence can depend on the current state. We instantiate these ideas in . A causal LSTM encodes the observed POI, category, temporal, geographic, and user context. Two assignment networks retrieve mixtures from a pattern dictionary and a spatial dictionary. An elementwise product of their projected mixtures implements a factorized interaction over all pattern--space pairs. Feature-wise affine modulation then adapts this shared interaction to the user. A separate user-conditioned branch captures information not constrained to pass through the dictionaries. Finally, a scalar gate mixes additive fusion with an affine transform of the branches' multiplicative interaction. A full-candidate score combines the resulting preference representation with a learned first-order POI transition term. Evaluation must preserve what ``next'' means. If an unseen POI interrupts a trajectory, deleting it and joining the remaining visits introduces a transition that was not observed. If the final POI is unknown, replacing it with an earlier known POI changes the prediction target. We instead use a training-only candidate vocabulary and retain only the final continuous known suffix for each held-out trajectory. All compared implementations receive the same valid queries, candidate set, ranking metrics, and validation-based checkpoint selection rule. This defines a closed-set experiment; it is not an evaluation of unseen-user or unseen-POI generalization. The contributions of this study are as follows. We introduce two shared dictionaries whose assignments adapt to the observed prefix, together with a pattern--space interaction and separate personalized branches. We give an explicit factorization of its pairwise interaction and an additive--projective fusion rule, making the roles and limits of the shared and personalized components precise. We provide a unified-protocol comparison with nine local baseline implementations on two cities. The current single-seed results lead NYC Acc@1 and MRR and remain competitive on TKY; they do not establish uniform superiority across metrics or cities. Sequential recommendation combines personal preference with dependence on recent observations. FPMC factorizes personalized Markov transitions~; we adapt its singleton-target form for next-POI scoring. ST-RNN adds spatial and temporal context to recurrent transitions~. STGN and its coupled-gate STGCN variant control recurrent updates with spatio-temporal inputs~. PLSPL combines long- and short-term preferences~. STAN uses spatio-temporal attention beyond adjacent check-ins~. DeepMove attends over historical trajectories to capture mobility periodicity~, while Flashback retrieves past recurrent states according to spatio-temporal similarity~. LSTPM pairs a nonlocal long-term preference module with a geography-aware short-term state~. Together, these methods show that useful context may come from distant history as well as the latest transition. Historical attention and prototype composition represent different sources of reuse. Attention and state retrieval reweight representations tied to recorded visits, whereas our dictionaries are population-shared parameters that can be mixed even when users do not share POIs. Likewise, long- and short-term modules separate temporal horizons, while \ separates pattern and spatial axes and lets the spatial mixture modulate the pattern mixture. It uses a single causal recurrent encoder, does not require a supplied next-visit timestamp, and ranks the complete candidate vocabulary. This distinction motivates evaluating the interaction itself rather than treating memory, attention, and clustering as interchangeable mechanisms. GETNext adds a global trajectory flow map to a Transformer~. STHGCN captures higher-order trajectory relations with spatio-temporal hypergraphs~. DCHL learns disentangled collaborative, transition, and geographic views~. HVGAE studies hyperbolic graph representations and variational next-POI modeling~. These methods expose population-level information beyond one user's sequence, but construct their relations and representations differently. Our model does not perform graph message passing or hypergraph convolution. It shares prototype parameters across the training population and uses a prefix-conditioned combination of them. This is a different architectural bias, not a claim that dictionary sharing is a substitute for every form of collaborative graph information. In particular, the dictionaries are learned jointly with the recommendation objective and are not extracted as communities from a user--POI graph. Clustering and multi-interest modeling are closely related to our motivation. CLLP models latent preferences using preference prototypes, distillation, and contrastive learning~. DPRL investigates disentangled and personalized representations for next-POI recommendation~. These are important neighboring approaches: the use of a latent pattern bank or personalized spatial information is already established, so a novelty claim must concern the particular interaction and conditioning mechanism rather than clustering alone. \ separates two dictionary axes and combines their mixtures multiplicatively before user-specific modulation. The modulation follows the feature-wise affine conditioning principle of FiLM~, with sigmoid-constrained scales in our implementation. A second user-conditioned branch and a learned gate distinguish additive correction from multiplicative interaction. Unlike objectives designed to enforce independence between latent factors, our next-POI loss contains no decorrelation, contrastive, or cluster-balancing term. Accordingly, ``shared'' denotes shared parameters and reusable components, not an identified user-invariant subspace.
