Since the differentiable search index~ was proposed, this paradigm has demonstrated advantages in many scenarios~, offering end-to-end training, elimination of large-scale vector index maintenance, and natural integration of multi-objective ranking signals. Beyond the initial item SID construction, a more pressing challenge in dynamic settings is how to maintain the SID-to-Item mapping over time. In e-commerce search, a highly dynamic retrieval environment and the item set expands daily: new items are listed, prices fluctuate, and conversion rates evolve, all of which require periodic updates to the retriever model and SID-to-Item mapping. In production-grade systems in particular, SID encodings often incorporate a business-value dimension~, making the mapping unavoidably time-varying. After the model is trained on a historical mapping, it may generate the same SID at inference time, yet that SID now resolves to a different item under the updated mapping---a phenomenon we call . A deeper issue is that prior work has predominantly pursued collision-free SID assignments; in dynamic settings, however, the continuously growing item set inevitably introduces collisions with existing items. Forcing a full remapping to eliminate these collisions changes the SIDs of many existing items, triggering severe semantic drift. In practice, semantic drift is easy to overlook. At a large beam size, the model can still recover target items by exploring many alternative branches even when high-probability paths have become stale; offline evaluation at a single wide beam may therefore suggest that a mapping update is safe, while latency-constrained serving at a narrow beam suffers a far larger loss. Meanwhile, retraining is often assumed to repair the refreshed mapping. However, this assumption neglects the structural knowledge the model has already encoded during training, particularly the learned topology of the SID space. These considerations raise some deployment-oriented questions. How much damage does a SID mapping refresh cause, and how does the observed damage depend on beam size? Does retrieval quality vary systematically with the fraction of old items whose SIDs change? Can warm-start training repair the damage from remapping, or should the system preserve the old SID topology and only insert new items? Rather than proposing another algorithmic remedy, we take a step back and answer these questions empirically through extensive controlled experiments over 67 days of large-scale e-commerce search logs. Unlike prior studies that evaluate a single proposed method against baselines, we systematically vary the mapping policy as an experimental factor, spanning the full spectrum from complete remapping to complete freezing. By independently controlling the training strategy, this design enables a clean disentanglement of mapping-induced damage from training-induced repair. Our contributions are: We systematically quantify the degradation caused by SID mapping evolution and reveal that wide-beam evaluation masks the true severity of drift. Specifically, while mapping updates cause a notable reduction in HitRate and MRR at beam size~1000, the degradation is substantially more severe at beam size~1. We discover a strict monotonic negative relationship between the fraction of changed old-item SIDs~$ $ and retrieval quality. Under the same warm-start budget, full remapping remains substantially worse than the preservation policy, indicating that the model has internalized the implicit topology of the SID space and that the simple warm-start regime does not repair topology disruption. We find that topology preservation is a more fundamental determinant than retraining alone, and propose a simple incremental warm-start strategy feasible at industrial scale . Experiments show approximately 9\ Four findings are particularly counter-intuitive. First, evaluations conducted with wide beams can mask the vast majority of the actual drift severity, meaning that many production systems may be operating under a false sense of safety. Second, the relationship between remapping fraction and quality loss is strictly monotonic, leaving no useful middle ground between full remapping and complete freezing. Third, warm-start training, the default repair strategy assumed by most continual-learning approaches, cannot recover from topology disruption: Full-Remapping after warm-start still underperforms a no-retrain Frozen baseline. Fourth, rather than creating a 'model soup' by mixing the old and new models, it is more effective to directly use the new model trained from the old one. Together, these findings suggest that the problem prior work has been solving, namely how to repair mapping-induced damage, is better addressed by not creating the damage in the first place. Differentiable Search Index~ and GENRE~ established the paradigm of directly generating document or entity identifiers, replacing nearest-neighbor search with autoregressive sequence generation. Since then, generative retrieval has been applied across document and passage retrieval~, podcast discovery~, recommendation ranking~, and e-commerce search~. TIGER~ demonstrated that residual-quantized semantic identifiers can effectively capture item semantics for retrieval, and since its introduction, a wave of work has adopted semantic IDs in e-commerce. OneRec~ and OneSearch~ propose an end-to-end framework. In e-commerce search scenarios, CQ-SID~ introduces query-item contrastive learning and EG-GRPO for search-relevant clusters. Ling et al.~ encode business-value ranking into SID assignments, and Zhan et al.~ propose a unified generative framework that integrates retrieval and pre-ranking within a single model. These efforts focus on initial model design; the update-time implications of evolving item collections remain open. The quality of semantic identifiers is a first-order determinant of generative retrieval performance. RQ-VAE-based methods~ produce hierarchical codes through residual vector quantization of item embeddings. Alternative approaches include RQ-KMeans~, multi-identifier pretraining~, and self-improving tokenization~. A key concern is how quantization distorts neighborhood structure: topology-aware tokenization~, qualification-aware collision handling~, and topology-preserving tokenization~ all indicate that identifier structure affects downstream retrieval. Hybrid semantic/hash identifiers~ and constrained decoding~ address serving efficiency, while FORGE~ provides a systematic benchmark for evaluating SID construction methods. DREAM~ further identifies premature static SID commitment as a cold-start bottleneck and proposes counterfactual contrastive learning for progressive refinement. These efforts focus on constructing higher-quality initial SIDs; however, even when the initial design is effective, the impact of subsequent mapping evolution on retrieval quality remains insufficiently characterized. DSI++~ frames document addition as continual learning and documents catastrophic forgetting during sequential updates. CLEVER~ and PromptDSI~ address dynamic corpora under limited replay. More recent work directly targets semantic ID staleness: Baikalov et al.~ propose bijective alignment mappings to mitigate collaborative-SID drift, Feng et al.~ introduce drift-confidence-based selective tokenization to minimize unnecessary code changes. Alternative paradigms avoid weight updates entirely: Den et al.~ reframe incremental retrieval as an in-context generation problem, Mekonnen et al.~ attach parametric memory heads to frozen models, and Huynh et al.~ use dynamically expandable LoRA experts. These methods mitigate mapping staleness through increasingly sophisticated algorithmic designs such as alignment solvers, selective reassignment, and parameter-efficient adapters. However, most of these approaches introduce additional architectural complexity. Although recent methods have recognized the issue of drift and proposed algorithmic remedies, no study has treated the mapping policy as an independent experimental factor for disentangling the damage caused by mapping changes themselves from the effectiveness of repair algorithms. Nor has the monotonic relationship between the fraction of changed mappings and retrieval quality been established. The central tension is stability versus freshness. Several routine operations can trigger mapping drift: refreshing value-based positions to track shifting market conditions, retraining an RQ-VAE or other item-to-SID clustering model after a large batch of new items alters the codebook, or more generally any procedure that reassigns existing items to different SIDs. Each of these invalidates part of the mapping learned by $f_ $. Unlike flat class labels, each SID in a generative retrieval model corresponds to a sequence of autoregressive decoding decisions: the model first chooses $s_1$, then $s_2$ conditioned on $s_1$, and so on until the full identifier is produced. Every Item-SID association is therefore not a single label but a learned decoding path through a tree-structured output space. When a mapping update reassigns items to different SIDs, it does not merely relabel training data; it redistributes output probability across previously learned paths, potentially invalidating entire branches of the decoding tree. , keeping item-to-SID assignments unchanged, ensures that these learned paths remain valid. A wide beam may partially compensate for drift by exploring additional branches, whereas a narrow beam commits earlier and is more vulnerable. Once a new item accumulates sufficient exposure to become eligible for the item pool, it first receives a semantic prefix from the RQ-VAE quantizer and is then inserted into the value ranking of its corresponding cluster. The item is inserted directly at its value-rank position, regardless of whether that position falls in the top-$K$ head or the collision-bucketed tail. The top-$K$ dedicated assignment is maintained only in the training-time SID set; at insertion time, a new item landing in the head may share a previously dedicated token with an existing item, and no old-item SID is modified. This ensures that new-item insertion has zero impact on the existing SID space. Each update cycle poses two independent decisions: items receive new SIDs and the model is retrained to accommodate the updated mapping . We deliberately focus on simple, interpretable options rather than sophisticated continual-learning methods, for two reasons. First, production systems favor reliable, debuggable solutions over algorithmic complexity. Second, because the autoregressive retriever learns query-to-SID-path associations, any mapping change structurally invalidates part of these learned associations; the mapping policy is therefore expected to be the dominant factor, while the training procedure plays a supporting role. The underlying mechanism, however, is not specific to the value dimension: any operation that reassigns existing items to different SIDs—refreshing value positions, retraining the RQ-VAE codebook after a large ingestion batch, or de-duplicating collisions—invalidates part of the learned decoding paths, as formalized by $ $ in Eq~. More generally, the drift magnitude is governed less by the presence of a value dimension per se than by how extensively the SID-assignment model is retrained: fully retraining the quantizer/codebook produces an entirely new SID space and hence the largest drift, whereas fine-tuning only a subset of parameters reassigns far fewer identifiers and yields proportionally smaller drift. Across this spectrum the direction of the effect and the preservation principle are unchanged. We therefore position topology preservation as a general operating principle whose urgency scales with how much of the mapping a given update reassigns. The proposed policy, which involves freezing existing items, inserting new ones, and employing warm-starts, is intentionally designed for operational simplicity. We contend that this simplicity is a distinct advantage rather than a limitation. In production retrieval systems, update policies must inherently be reliable, easily debuggable, and broadly applicable across diverse item categories. Moreover, real-world deployments typically involve a temporal decoupling between serving model updates and item index table refreshes. During this transition period, any misalignment between the updating model and the item index can cause inference outputs to fail when mapping back to their corresponding items. This operational constraint also motivates our strategy of preserving the original SIDs for existing items. Therefore, the primary contribution of this work extends beyond the policy itself; it provides robust empirical evidence that such a straightforward approach is practically sufficient. We investigate strategies for updating generative retrieval systems in e-commerce environments where items increase and business-value signals continuously evolve. Our experiments reveal that full identifier remapping induces severe semantic drift, whereas wide-beam evaluation demonstrates significantly less degradation. Across all tested update policies, both HitRate and MRR decrease monotonically as the proportion of remapped identifiers for existing items increases. Under identical warm-start conditions, retaining original identifiers for existing items while selectively inserting only eligible new ones yields higher HitRate than full remapping—a trend that remains consistent across subsequent dependent update cycles. Furthermore, models fine-tuned from previous checkpoints outperform naive model souping. These findings distill into a concise operational principle: insert new items but freeze existing ones; apply warm-starting when feasible, otherwise freeze the model; and strictly avoid altering the semantic identifiers the model has already learned.
