Domain-specific scientific information retrieval from unstructured text is harder than general retrieval. Relevant passages may share the same tokens representing domain-specific entities --- surfactants, polymers, solvents, reaction conditions, performance descriptors, etc --- yet differ in the functional roles those entities play and the application context for which a system was designed. The discriminative signal is ontological, not topical: the same surfactant may act as emulsifier, dispersant, or wetting agent depending on context, and it often takes technical training to discern the difference. Language models trained on generic web data are not designed for this, and consequently existing text retrieval benchmarks do not expose this failure mode. We introduce , a large-scale retrieval benchmark of 55,352 queries built from US formulation patents. Distractors are functional near-misses --- passages sharing high chemical entity density with the anchor but differing in role, context, and function --- not topic distractors. Two corpus variants isolate distractor difficulty: ($ $997K random-distractor passages) tests coarse domain retrieval; ($ $994K ontologically adjacent near-miss passages) requires functional-level discrimination. Both share identical queries and relevance judgments and are released in BEIR format. We evaluate 10 model configurations spanning commercial API encoders , open-source encoders , and their task-adaptive fine-tuned variants, along with a BM25 lexical baseline. Three findings emerge. First, commercial APIs score below the BM25 lexical floor ( $ $ 0.009 vs.\ 0.375). Second, TAPT on taxonomy-derived triplets performs significantly better than the API baselines and exceeds the lexical ceiling . Third, the embedding modification produced by TAPT is task-specific: models that excel at retrieval gain nothing on classification or clustering, yet they retain their advantage under corpus-size ablation. BEIR~ and MTEB~ established zero-shot retrieval evaluation as a standard, but their predominantly general-domain corpora mask domain-specific failures. FinMTEB~, ChemTEB~, MLEB~, and SciRepEval~ each demonstrate that high aggregate MTEB scores do not predict performance on specialised professional text, motivating purpose-built benchmarks. follows this line for formulation chemistry; we focus on dense bi-encoder evaluation~, where embedding geometry is directly interpretable and domain adaptation is tractable. The closest prior work is in patents and chemistry. PatentSBERTa~ and PaECTER~ adapt sentence transformers to patent similarity via contrastive and citation-informed training. PatenTEB~ provides 15 benchmark tasks across 109 IPC subclasses; DAPFAM~ addresses patent-family prior-art retrieval. Both use binary, citation-derived relevance over title and abstract --- neither targets within-domain composition matching over full description text. ChemTEB~ benchmarks 34 models on chemistry tasks built from filtered QA datasets and finds that contrastive training matters more than domain pretraining; its retrieval evaluation rests on $ $33{,}000 corpus passages and 233 queries across two splits adapted from general-domain QA, both substantially smaller than the corpus and query set. ChemRAG-Bench~ evaluates end-to-end RAG for chemistry. The full file structure diagram is provided in the dataset card. 590,247 formulation patents were downloaded from the USPTO via the PatentsView API~, covering utility patents from 1995--2022. CPC subclass filtering was applied to retain patents covering pharmaceutics, cosmetics, adhesives, coatings, agrochemicals, and food formulations. Each patent description was segmented into overlapping chunks , producing approximately 3.1 million candidate passages. Named entity recognition was applied across 11 entity categories. A Llama-3-8B model with a LoRA adapter~ was trained via a semi-supervised procedure: Claude Sonnet 4~ generated initial labeled examples from a stratified patent sample, used to fine-tune the adapter across all 11 categories simultaneously. This approach follows standard practice in materials science NER literature~. The NER pass processed all 3.1M passages, extracting 234.7M total entity mentions spanning 3,090,473 unique surface forms. Refer to Appendix~ for NER details and the annotation schema. Entity normalization proceeded in three phases: ~deterministic normalization --- lowercasing, punctuation stripping, and plural collapse to merge lexically near-identical surface forms; ~embedding-based clustering using cosine-similarity thresholding to group semantically equivalent variants (e.g., ``PEG-400'' and ``polyethylene glycol 400''); and ~LLM adjudication for ambiguous clusters where embedding similarity was insufficient to resolve cross-category conflicts~. All queries are synthetic, generated by a three-stage LLM pipeline illustrated in Figure~. Starting from the entity-annotated passage pool, candidate anchor passages were identified based on entity density. For each anchor, one hard negative and one soft negative were retrieved from different patents, forming a triplet. All pairs are cross-patent by construction. Each triplet was submitted to Claude Sonnet 4 ~, which generated natural-language queries --- concrete, specific questions answerable by a target passage but not by the other two. Queries were required to address formulation-relevant content: composition, process conditions, performance properties, or application context. Questions about pure chemical nomenclature, device hardware, or patent boilerplate were explicitly excluded. Full generation details are provided in Appendix~. Candidate queries were then subject to two-stage verification by Claude Haiku 4.5~. The first stage screened each query for grammar and whether it represented a question a formulation scientist would realistically search for. The second stage presented the query alongside the three passages in shuffled order and asked the model to identify which passage answers the query. A query was retained only if the selected passage matched the original anchor; failure at either stage eliminated it from the qrel pool. After quality filtering, 55,352 queries were retained and assigned to train/dev/test splits at the level . All evaluations use the test split . We however use two different types of relevance scoring. treats only the anchor passage as relevant and all others as irrelevant ; this matches the evaluation protocol of BEIR and PatenTEB, enabling direct comparison with prior benchmarks. uses the full relevance hierarchy : score 3 for the anchor, score 2 for hard negatives, score 1 for soft negatives, score 0 for all others. Graded evaluation rewards partial credit for retrieving compositionally similar passages near the top of the ranking, revealing finer-grained ordering quality that binary evaluation cannot distinguish. Binary nDCG@10 is the primary reported metric; graded nDCG@10 is reported as secondary. All results are computed on the test split . All metrics are computed with the library against the split-specific qrel files. For binary evaluations, the same scoring infrastructure is used with binarized relevance vectors. All evaluations use a two-stage pipeline: corpus embeddings are stored in a FAISS flat inner-product index~ ; at query time each query is encoded and the top-100 results retrieved by exact nearest-neighbor search. As established in BEIR~ and corroborated in specialised domains such as legal case retrieval~ and patent prior-art retrieval~, lexical baselines remain a competitive reference point that neural retrievers frequently fail to surpass without task-specific adaptation. Since the queries are generated from the passages, lexical overlap between queries and anchor passages is expected, and the strong BM25 performance is not surprising. Each training example is a triplet $ $ where $Q$ is the generated query, $A$ is the anchor passage , and $$ is a hard negative from the Jaccard-tier pairs . MNRL treats $A$ as the positive for $Q$, uses $$ as an explicit negative, and additionally treats the other in-batch positives as negatives. MNRL directly trains the geometry of the embedding space, moving matched passages closer to queries and pushing hard negatives apart. TAPT-MNRL on the training split produces substantial retrieval improvements across all three architectures. Contrastive fine-tuning with task-specific $ $ triplets directly reshapes the embedding geometry in a way that MLM pretraining cannot~. The experimental results in Section~ establish a clear hierarchy: commercial API embeddings fail on while TAPT-fine-tuned open-source models are competitive or outperform the baseline. Two deeper questions motivate the analysis that follows. First, do massively pre-trained API embeddings---state-of-the-art on general benchmarks---score near zero on this task? The answer cannot simply be domain unfamiliarity, since these same models perform well on coarse patent classification . Second, what does TAPT training actually do to the geometry of the embedding space that enables successful retrieval? We examine both questions through a diagnostic geometric analysis and downstream task probes. To understand the geometric failure of commercial API models, we run a diagnostic experiment: for each model, we extract the per-query cosine similarity distribution over the top-100 retrieved passages from the C1 corpus for all 5,459 test queries. If the model has learned to discriminate within the formulation patent domain, we expect the ``discrimination gap'' $ $ between the top-ranked and rank-100 score to be large. If the embedding space collapses all within-domain passages to a narrow similarity band, $ $ will approach zero. The graded qrel tier structure is derived from entity-Jaccard similarity between passage pairs.
