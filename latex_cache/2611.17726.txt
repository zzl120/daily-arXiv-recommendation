The evolution of document retrieval has shifted from keyword-based indexing to dense semantic vector spaces, driven by advancements in transformer architectures and multimodal learning. Traditional document retrieval systems rely primarily on statistical techniques such as TF-IDF and BM25 , which utilize inverted indexes to match query terms against document corpora. While efficient and scalable, these methods are fundamentally limited by the ``vocabulary mismatch" problem and an inability to interpret non-textual elements like diagrams or tables. The rise of Large Language Models and dense retrievers introduced semantic search, where text is projected into a high-dimensional latent space. However, as noted by Karamolegkou et al. , these models often rely on memorization, raising significant copyright and privacy concerns in the context of research libraries. Multimodal retrieval seeks to bridge the gap between textual queries and visual content. Early Vision-Language Models like LayoutLM and DocFormer utilised ``early interaction," where visual and textual features are fused before encoding. While powerful, these models often struggle with fine-grained alignment between specific query tokens and localised image patches. The introduction of ColBERT and its multimodal successor, ColPali , introduced the ``late interaction" paradigm. By representing documents as a sequence of multiple vectors , these models allow for token-to-patch alignment at the scoring stage. This approach is particularly effective for textbooks where information is spatially distributed across diagrams and text. This study builds on the efficacy of ColPali but extends the research beyond simple retrieval quality to address the systemic overhead of managing such high-cardinality multi-vector data. As retrieval systems transition from laboratory experiments to production-grade tools, the role of the vector database has become central. Specialized systems such as Milvus, Weaviate, and Qdrant utilize Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs to achieve approximate nearest neighbor search. Recent research in High-Performance Computing environments has highlighted that vector database performance is frequently CPU-bound, particularly during the conversion of embeddings into database-specific batch objects. Furthermore, the storage of multi-vectors poses a significant memory challenge. Our work explores the use of on-disk Memmap storage and binary quantization within Qdrant to overcome these memory bottlenecks, providing a unique performance characterization that is currently missing from the literature on VLM-powered digital libraries. The resulting image is stored as a PNG image; PNG ensures lossless compression and layout fidelity, which is essential for downstream visual embedding. {The page images are encoded by ColPali using its PaliGemma-3B backbone, which combines a SigLIP vision encoder with a Gemma-2B language model. ColPali projects the resulting representations into a shared 128-dimensional embedding space, producing a sequence of vectors for each page that captures its textual, visual, and structural content.} The key to our scalable approach is a technique pioneered in 2020 by the ColBERT information retrieval model ~{} called . LI is a retrieval architectural design in which query and document representations are processed independently and only interact at the final stages . Unlike early interaction models that fuse query and document embeddings before or during encoding , LI models maintain separate encoding pipelines, enabling efficient storage and reuse of document embeddings. The core idea is to represent both queries and documents as sequences of vectors , and compute fine-grained relevance scores through token-to-token or token-to-patch similarity comparisons. Specifically, in ColBERT, and later ColPali, late interaction works as follows: a document is scored by taking the maximum similarity between each query vector and all document vectors, and then aggregating these maxima into a single relevance score. Applied to our system, each textbook page is represented by a fixed set of visual patch embeddings, while each natural language query is transformed into a sequence of dense token embeddings. At retrieval time, each query token interacts with all document patches via a configurable similarity metric , and a late aggregation operation produces the final document relevance score. The late interaction approach enables token-level alignment between query concepts and fine-grained visual features of the page , making it well-suited for visually dense documents like textbooks. Moreover, late interaction scoring allows us to isolate and evaluate the effect of different similarity functions within a controlled and interpretable retrieval {pipeline.} A critical requirement for successful multimodal retrieval is the cross-modal alignment of query and document embeddings within a shared latent space. In this pipeline, query vectorization is intrinsically coupled with the ColPali model to ensure semantic compatibility across modalities. As noted in foundational multimodal literature , maintaining a shared manifold is essential for zero-shot retrieval tasks. While it is theoretically possible to utilize disparate tools for query and image vectorization, such an approach would necessitate a complex transformation matrix or a shared contrastive training objective to map textual queries into the same high-dimensional manifold as the page images. In our implementation, we mitigate the risk of ``manifold misalignment" by utilizing ColPali’s internal language model backbone to vectorize queries. This ensures that the resulting textual token embeddings are natively comparable to the visual patch embeddings. This architectural coupling allows for the direct application of the MaxSim operator, as both the query and the document reside in a bi-modal projection space where distance represents true semantic similarity rather than just modal correlation. This alignment is what enables the model to resolve ``fine-grained" queries, such as linking a technical term in the query to a specific annotated component in a complex architectural diagram. To optimize the system for library-scale datasets on resource-constrained hardware, we implemented three specific database configurations: We utilize a Hierarchical Navigable Small World index to achieve sub-linear search complexity across the $>4$ million patch vectors in our corpus. We utilize Memmap storage to persist vector data directly on the disk. This allows the system to scale to datasets that exceed available RAM, with the operating system managing page caching for frequently accessed vectors. To reduce I/O bandwidth and increase throughput, we enabled binary quantization. After applying the similarity function, the system scores the retrieved pages based on how closely they match the query. This step helps identify the most relevant pages from the initial set of results. The system ranks the pages by comparing the query with each one and determining their relevance. For each query, returned are the top relevant pages . We configure the database to return 25 results, then use a local algorithm to identify the top 5. The local algorithm is simple; it merely removes duplicates where they occur. Max-sum pooling is used to look at each part of the page and select the part that matches the query the best. The best matches are combined to get a final score for the page. This ensures the system focuses on the most relevant parts of each page. Once the initial scoring is done, the next step is re-ranking, which fine-tunes the results. Here the order of documents is adjusted based on how highly relevant they are. This helps make sure that the pages that are most likely to be useful to the user are shown first. We conduct a series of experiments isolating the performance of the vector database from other system components. All experiments are performed on a locally hosted infrastructure to eliminate network latency variability and focus on computational performance. The system is deployed on a Jetstream2 cloud instance with containerization via Docker. We design three specific evaluation protocols to assess different dimensions of system performance: We utilize the same corpus used earlier in evaluating retrieval efficacy and step-wise grow it. We measure retrieval latency as a function of corpus size. The vector database index is incrementally populated in steps increases in numbers of documents . At each step, we execute a standard set of five benchmark queries three times to stabilize cache effects and record the average retrieval time. The performance of the vector retrieval system is evaluated using a controlled concurrency benchmarking pattern designed to isolate database efficiency from model inference overhead. The evaluation pipeline consists of an initial setup phase followed by incremental stress testing across multiple concurrency levels. During the initialization phase, the Vision-Language Model and Qdrant service clients are instantiated. The retrieval latencies measured in this benchmark do not include the time spent embedding the text query and is calculated for the time required to search the database and return the results, to focus majorly on the database performance. To ensure that recorded latencies accurately reflect the database’s search performance rather than the computational cost of feature extraction, a ``pre-encoding" strategy is employed. In our experimental environment utilizing the ColPali model, we establish a baseline for inference overhead by calculating the mean encoding time across 50 iterations for a representative benchmark query. Theoretical projections based on distributed vector database performance in high-performance computing environments suggest several critical shifts when moving toward a 6-billion entity scale. First, the data insertion phase, currently manageable via asynchronous clients at our current scale, would encounter a severe CPU-bound bottleneck during the conversion of raw data into Qdrant batch objects. Empirical profiling in similar large-scale scientific workloads on the Polaris supercomputer indicates that this CPU-bound task can require significantly more time than the actual network insertion RPC . Consequently, scaling to billions of vectors would necessitate a shift from to robust multiprocessing or GPU-offloaded batching to avoid index-building stalls, as runs in a single synchronous thread and cannot parallelize CPU-bound tasks effectively. Second, resource saturation during index construction becomes a limiting factor at massive scales. A single Qdrant worker has been observed to utilize 90-97\ Finally, the query performance in a billion-scale system must account for the broadcast-reduce communication overhead. In distributed Qdrant clusters, queries are broadcast to all workers, and results are aggregated. Evidence suggests that increasing the number of workers yields diminishing returns for datasets smaller than 30 GB due to this overhead. For extreme scales, the parallelization gains of horizontal sharding may be overshadowed by inter-worker communication costs unless the cluster can adaptively scale based on the data volume. Through a dual-axial evaluation, we established that a decoupled, three-tier architecture utilizing the ColPali VLM for high-fidelity semantic alignment and the Qdrant vector database for high-performance indexing offers a practical blueprint for transforming visually rich documents into discoverable entities. Our findings provide three critical insights for the future of digital discovery: Cosine similarity serves as the most reliable distance measure for high-dimensional, late-interaction scoring, successfully uncovering complex conceptual relationships in visually dense material. By implementing binary quantization and HNSW indexing, we achieved sub-linear latency growth, ensuring library-scale discovery remains responsive and well within interactive search limits. The utilization of on-disk Memmap storage allows consumer-grade hardware to achieve RAM-like performance with resilient throughput exceeding 3,500 QPS, making the system production-ready for massive multi-user concurrency. While successful, our ``Unbounded Analysis'' highlights that traditional, rigid ground truth labels may undercount the true semantic capabilities of VLM-based systems. Future work is to scale the work, focusing on GPU-offloaded index construction to overcome the CPU-bound bottlenecks identified during theoretical scaling projections.
