We present , a two-stage framework that first learns patent-domain representations and then optimizes X-category retrieval relevance. The first stage aligns complementary sections within patents and corresponding sections across different-language patent-family members, learning from both patent structure and multilingual expressions. The second stage learns X-category relevance using examination-report X citations as positives and negatives drawn from retrieval candidates and A-category citations. Matryoshka representation learning enables 64- and 1024-dimensional embeddings within one model. Evaluation on a common Chinese, English, and Japanese collection with four query views yields 89.09\ Patent prior-art retrieval seeks earlier disclosures relevant to assessing the novelty or inventive step of a claimed invention. Its relevance criterion extends beyond shared terminology or membership in the same technological field: two patents may describe similar components yet differ in the connections, operating conditions, or sequence of steps that define the invention. We focus on X-category references, which are particularly relevant when considered individually under the search-report convention . Retrieving these references requires a representation that connects the technical content of a query patent to examination-relevant disclosures, even when their wording and presentation differ. Patent retrieval combines lexical and semantic evidence in several ways. BM25 provides exact-term matching , dense retrievers learn correspondences beyond shared vocabulary, and hybrid retrieval and reranking combine or refine these signals . Exact-term matching is valuable for specialized terminology but cannot by itself resolve synonymous or different-language expressions. Learned semantic similarity addresses a broader range of expressions, yet proximity in an embedding space must still reflect the intended relevance criterion. For X-category retrieval, the central representation-learning problem is to distinguish examination-relevant disclosures from patents that are merely close in topic. Domain-specific training provides a means of connecting representations to patent evidence. PaECTER adapts an encoder using examiner-added citations , while patent-section self-supervision learns from related views within a document . QaECTER jointly learns citation retrieval and multi-view self-alignment . further study cross-section training and mixtures of taxonomy, citation, and cross-section pairs, including adaptation of Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B. Building on these studies, we investigate within-patent cross-section and within-family cross-language correspondence as a preliminary adaptation stage followed by X-citation relevance learning, assessing its benefit relative to direct supervised fine-tuning. The multilingual setting introduces variation in both document view and language. An abstract summarizes an invention, claims specify the subject matter for which protection is sought, and a description develops the technical disclosure in greater detail. The TAC view concatenates the title, abstract, and claim fields. Such differences in length, emphasis, and specificity are reflected in patent benchmarks that evaluate multiple query types and asymmetric text representations . Chinese, English, and Japanese expressions introduce further variation. Within-patent sections and different-language patent-family members therefore provide complementary opportunities to learn correspondence despite changes in how technical content is presented. The distinction between these correspondences and X-category relevance motivates a sequential approach. Sections within a patent and corresponding sections across its family members provide weak supervision for representing related technical content. Examination-report X citations provide task supervision for ranking relevant prior-art references above competing candidates. Our hypothesis is that the former can provide a useful initialization for learning the latter. Specifically, we ask whether cross-section and multilingual family alignment before X-category supervision improves retrieval over direct supervised fine-tuning under the same subsequent training conditions. We develop \ on Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B to examine this hypothesis. Stage I uses abstract--claim and claim--description pairs within patents, together with abstract--abstract and claim--claim pairs across different-language members of the same family. Stage II adapts the resulting encoder to X-category retrieval using examination-report X citations and selected negative candidates. Domain alignment thus precedes citation-supervised relevance learning. We assess this training sequence through comparisons with the base encoder, the intermediate Stage I model, and a direct supervised fine-tuning control with matched second-stage settings. Evaluation across query languages and views characterizes where the additional domain-adaptation stage improves retrieval. Our contributions are twofold: Stage I jointly learns cross-section correspondence within patents and same-section correspondence across different-language family members; Stage II learns retrieval relevance from examination-report X citations. The framework uses these two forms of patent-content correspondence to initialize task-supervised training, connecting multilingual patent representation learning to the X-category retrieval objective. Under the same second-stage training and evaluation conditions, the complete model improves overall macro Hit@100 by 3.19 percentage points over direct supervised fine-tuning at 64 dimensions, with gains in nine of twelve language--view combinations. Hit@100 gains are concentrated in English and Japanese, while Hit@10 and Hit@20 improve in all three query-language groups, characterizing the benefit across languages, views, and retrieval cutoffs. Patent representation learning addresses several objectives, including technological similarity, classification, and prior-art relevance. PatentSBERTa studies technological distance and classification , while PatentMatch constructs claim--passage matching examples from examiner citations . PaECTER adapts BERT for Patents using examiner-added citation supervision . Graph-based patent retrieval further represents invention features and their relationships explicitly and learns from examiner citations . These studies learn patent representations from technological similarity, localized text matching, and citation relationships. These tasks differ in both the unit of supervision and the meaning of relevance. Technological similarity concerns related inventions, claim--passage matching concerns localized textual evidence, and document retrieval ranks complete candidate references. Consequently, performance on one task does not directly characterize performance on another. We construct retrieval relevance labels from X-category citations recorded in examination reports. learn patent representations through self-supervision using document sections as related views. QaECTER jointly trains citation retrieval and multi-view self-alignment using X-, Y-, and A-category citation pairs and views of the same patent . Sophia-bench evaluates twelve query representations against title--abstract--claims--description candidates. Its relevance pool includes cited and citing documents in the XY and A categories; evaluation uses English text, including translated patent-family representatives. PatenTEB broadens patent embedding evaluation across task types and asymmetric query--document representations . DAPFAM studies family-level retrieval and cross-domain transfer . adapt encoders including Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B: R3 pairs each patent abstract with its first independent claim, whereas R4 pools taxonomy, citation, and cross-section pairs. Their study includes a data-volume-matched control and examines supervision across retrieval, classification, and clustering. These studies highlight the importance of specifying the relevance unit, query view, domain, and representation size. Multi-stage embedding training has also been explored in general-purpose retrieval. GTE combines unsupervised pretraining with supervised adaptation . Our approach assigns patent-content correspondence and X-category relevance to two successive training stages. Whereas QaECTER jointly trains citation retrieval and multi-view alignment and R4 mixes pairs from different sources , we first combine cross-section pairs with same-section pairs from different-language family members, then train for retrieval using X-category citations. The distinction concerns the organization of supervision and the retrieval target. A direct-supervision control with matched second-stage settings evaluates the aggregate benefit of adding patent-domain adaptation before X-category supervision. Our evaluation uses X-category relevance labels, Chinese, English, and Japanese queries, and TAC candidates, whereas Sophia-bench uses broader citation-based relevance labels, English queries, and TACD candidates . Our empirical conclusions follow from model comparisons on a common evaluation collection, rather than comparisons with scores reported by these studies under different collections and relevance definitions. This study defines patent prior-art retrieval as follows: given a query patent, retrieve X-category patents from a candidate corpus. We use the X category of PCT international search reports, as described in the USPTO examination manual: an X reference is particularly relevant when taken alone and may prejudice novelty or inventive step, with common general knowledge considered where applicable . Training positives are X-category citations recorded in patent examination search reports. For novelty, an X-category patent must disclose the necessary claim elements together in the arrangement required by the claim . The relevant technical disclosure includes the technical objects and components; their connections, relative positions, and functional interactions; numerical and geometric restrictions; ordered method steps; and the distinguishing feature combination of the claimed invention. The first stage combines two sources of weak supervision. uses ABS--CLAIM and CLAIM--DESC pairs drawn from the same patent. These pairs link a concise summary to the claimed subject matter and connect the claims to the detailed disclosure, aligning sections that differ in purpose and level of detail. uses ABS--ABS and CLAIM--CLAIM pairs drawn from different-language members of the same patent family. Pairing the same section type across languages is intended to teach the encoder how corresponding patent content is expressed in different languages. Family membership provides weak supervision for this correspondence. Stage I uses approximately 25.3 million weakly supervised patent-text pairs. The second stage uses training examples $ $, where $q_i$ is a query view, $d_i^+$ is an X-category citation recorded in an examination search report, and $|}_i|=8$. Negatives are drawn from retrieval candidates and A-category citations through score-stratified sampling followed by language-prioritized selection. Candidate patents are represented by TAC. Training queries must have at least one X-category positive and exactly 2,000 candidates before filtering. Retrieval uses ABS, CLAIM, DESC, or TAC queries, with the filing date as the upper bound on candidate publication dates. Candidates are deduplicated by patent identifier and simple family, excluding the query patent, X-category positives and their family members, and documents with invalid vector scores. A-category citations supply additional negatives: under the search-report convention, they describe the state of the art without prejudicing novelty or inventive step . Candidates are stratified jointly by vector score and BM25 rank, with high, medium, and low levels on each axis. Sampling up to four documents from each of the nine strata yields at most 36 retrieved negatives. The four strata combining the high and low extremes of both axes must contribute at least six documents in total. The pool combines these documents with filtered A-category citations, without a separate count cap for the latter. Among candidates with valid TAC text, random selection first prioritizes negatives in the query language; any shortfall is filled from the positive document's language and then from other languages. No fixed quota is assigned to the strata. Only examples with eight valid negatives are retained. Training also uses in-batch negatives. Language-level results describe performance on different patent query sets, so the larger English and Japanese gains do not by themselves isolate a cross-language transfer effect. Aggregate gains should therefore be interpreted alongside the language- and view-specific results. The evaluation is limited to one patent collection and 64-dimensional representations, with one training run per model variant. The reported differences do not establish statistical significance or variation across random seeds. The direct-supervision comparison holds the second-stage training budget fixed; adding Stage I also increases total training computation, so the results do not establish an advantage under equal total computation. Separate ablations of the two weak-supervision sources and MRL, evaluation on other collections, and retrieval at 1024 dimensions remain to be conducted. Document-level Hit@K assesses recovery of annotated references; it does not measure coverage of individual claim features, which requires claim-linked evaluation. Evaluation is stratified by query language, without separate estimates for query--candidate language directions. X-category annotations may be incomplete, and retrieval-derived negatives without an X label can therefore include relevant documents. Test-query patents and their family members are excluded from both training stages, but their presence in the pretraining corpora of the underlying encoder and RoBERTa is unknown. These limitations constrain interpretations of cross-language transfer, pretraining overlap, and retrieval beyond the annotated reference sets. We presented , a two-stage approach that combines weakly supervised patent-view alignment with X-category retrieval training.
