Traditionally, toponym resolution relies on gazetteers to provide an explicit index of place entities and spatial relationships. Recently, gazetteer-free approaches seek to reduce dependence on handcrafted searches: dense retrieval utilizes text encoders to capture rich context, moving beyond the limitations of lexical search. However, text encoders implicitly assume that learned representations can function as reliable spatial-semantic indexes. In this paper, we evaluate this assumption through a spatial-semantic indexing setup: given a contextualized toponym mention, we retrieve the corresponding gazetteer entity represented by text derived from a gazetteer knowledge graph. We benchmark five frozen text encoders under two retrieval strategies: brute-force nearest-neighbor retrieval over entity representations, and a neuro-symbolic hierarchical beam search that constrains retrieval . Experimental results reveal a distinct coarse-versus-fine trade-off. Unconstrained dense retrieval frequently incurs catastrophic spatial errors. Conversely, hierarchical constraints improve coarse geographic grounding, but still yield limited benefit for fine-grained localization metrics: vanilla text encoders fail to capture the fine-scale spatial fidelity encoded in gazetteers. Alternatively, recent pretrained text encoders provide rich contextual and semantic representations, and act as learned indexes . Indeed, these models map toponyms to continuous vector spaces, thereby minimizing the search operations required for toponym resolution. Currently, retrieving spatial information from text relies on two paradigms. First, uses strict symbolic mapping with a machine-executable form , but is brittle against underspecified queries . Second, employs dense similarity search over learned representations . For spatial tasks, dense retrieval is more generalizable: models rely on distributional semantics and contextual cues without requiring an exact symbolic match. Nonetheless, similarity-based retrieval does not inherently enforce geographic constraints: , and small representation errors can lead to large spatial errors . This limitation is critical in modern multi-objective pipelines, such as open-domain question answering or recommendation . Solutions rely on lightweight text encoders for rapid initial lookups first; then followed by resource-intensive LLMs for complex reasoning ; and toponym resolution only serves as an ancillary signal throughout the process. These dependencies create a severe performance bottleneck: the entire pipeline fails if the primary text encoders omit relevant geographic cues . Any place name is ambiguous. The dependency on both footprint extent and topical context has challenged the creation of integrated spatial and indexing methods for decades . Related work in sensitive domains enhances technical text understanding with semantic indexing : disambiguation maps an expression in context to external, unequivocal knowledge bases of reference, and ideally those curated with ontologies . Spatial interpretation is also particularly sensitive. Indeed, divergences across different individuals' spatial cognition or social conceptions are all potential sources of misinterpretation that impact retrieval quality from the perspective of spatial-semantic alignment. In GeoIR applications, gazetteers define the geographic reference: gazetteers index the vocabulary of toponyms, by integrating resources from local authorities, volunteered geographic information , or historical records . Semantic indexing task can help to standardize toponym understanding in text as a toponym resolution task: linking a spatial mention to a gazetteer entry {liebermanGeotaggingLocalLexicons2010}. To this end, two approaches are used for toponym resolution: knowledge-base question answering and information-retrieval question-answering . Traditional systems rely on handcrafted heuristics to query gazetteers as structured place databases . Current methods operate primarily as within the KB-QA paradigm, relying on sophisticated pattern matching models at early stages, and focusing on homonymy resolution in a second stage. In the SemEval 2019 task for , the best systems first pre-filtered candidates from the GeoNames database . Purely lexical matching fails if the mention does not closely match any canonical entry due to lexical variation or underspecification . Practical pipelines mitigate lexical mismatches by optimizing queries through fuzzy string matching or place normalization as preliminary steps, before employing BM25-style keyword retrieval or sophisticated rule-based geocoders . Such optimizations improve matching performance but remain overly tailored to specific toponym resolution tasks ; to applications requiring exact locations ; and to the quality of the chosen gazetteer . For extensive retrieval, these methods incur prohibitive computational costs , or operate as binary filters rather than . Early work on latent semantic analysis and subsequent neural representation learning aimed to capture synonymy beyond exact term overlap. Modern transformer encoders provide contextual representations to enable dense retrieval over candidate entities. Consequently, the widespread adoption of text embedding models as retrieval encoders positions learned representations as ``approximate learned'' indexes for retrieval . In GeoIR, encoder-based retrieval systems utilize end-to-end models to embed geographical structure within latent representations, disambiguate toponyms, and generalize across lexical variants . Hence, IR-QA methods are gazetteer-free during inference. Early methods can even bypass traditional gazetteers and map text directly to geometric indexes, e.g. discretized global grid cells. For example, CamCoder and multi-level geocoding models utilize text representations and CNN classifiers to activate spatial cells. These approaches reduce toponym resolution to a coordinate retrieval task . However, geocoding focuses on only one spatial property and does not fully resolve spatial ambiguities. For example, a single pair of coordinates can represent multiple spatial objects at different resolutions or precisions, neglecting gazetteer semantics. More recently, contrastive fine-tuning of sentence encoders improves toponym resolution at fine-grained and global levels; however, evaluations rely on narrow geographic setups: the spatial scope is limited to a single country ; or methodological choices artificially inflate model confidence by partitioning highly specialized datasets and testing with limited variability compared to the training dataset. Finally, current research often treats toponym resolution as a standard entity linking task, where exact string matching conflates data memorization with true spatial generalization. Evaluations face two main gaps: the capability of models to resolve entities across complex gazetteer is not assessed as an essential requirement for spatial-semantic indexing; and the narrow geographic scope of most benchmark datasets prevents accurate assessment of global . State-of-the-art text encoders demonstrate strong generalization across IR-QA benchmark datasets for knowledge . Our analysis of zero-shot toponym understanding yields three key insights for GeoIR. Lightweight gazetteer-free architectures have received less attention because recent studies report near-perfect toponym resolution in KB-QA setups . However, these encoders remain critical performance bottlenecks in modern architectures, such as retrieval-augmented generation systems. In response, our retrieval framework prioritizes qualitative spatial plausibility over marginal quantitative gains. The proposed methodology values conceptual spatial matches over exact lexical memorization to enforce geographic fidelity. Spatial generalization requires models to maintain structural stability, notably during gazetteer pruning with a cut-off resolution; and adapt toponym recognition for downstream similarity matching. dense retrieval via text encoders is insufficient for spatial similarity matching here: lexical models remain strong baselines, while text encoders rely primarily on matching surface strings, rather than navigating spatial hierarchies. Text encoders struggle with direct toponym retrieval. In contrast, generative language models traditionally employ structured reasoning strategies to guide search . To bridge this gap, our neuro-symbolic search introduces a comparable sequential approach for text encoders: the hybrid method constrains the continuous neural representations through a chain of discrete, deterministic geographic steps. Empirically, combining gazetteers with embeddings improves macro-level accuracy for continents and countries. However, inconsistent local representations from text encoders mislead search, leaving retrieval prone to geographically clustered errors that gazetteers cannot resolve. HBS improves spatial grounding over brute-force search at coarse levels, but chaining retrieval with an explicit spatial index yields limited fine-grained gains. Consequently, embedding models suffer from spatial inaccuracy at fine resolutions, indicating a performance ceiling for neuro-symbolic indexing through administrative hierarchies and frozen vanilla text encoders. Conceptually, language models and gazetteers represent two structurally different knowledge bases: language models rely on repeated contextual associations to learn representations, whereas gazetteers enforce unique spatial identification. The limited size of human vocabulary restricts expressivity in contextual repetitions, limiting rich toponym representations in training corpora. For comparison, GeoNames indexes 25 million toponyms, whereas an average adult knows fewer than 50,000 lemmas and the English vocabulary contains roughly 1 million words . Consequently, language models have shown strong performance on commonsense knowledge, but toponyms represent a specialized vocabulary that lies at the boundary of language model capabilities. Conversely, digital gazetteers are also incomplete, capturing a strictly administrative perspective that omits historical records, vernacular names, and multilingual variants. Alternative perspectives like holonymy , sense of place , or discretized geometries also model geographic entities. Text encoders remain -focused and align primarily with semantic views . Designing spatially efficient text encoders requires balancing vocabulary distributions and integrating divergent perspectives---e.g., knowledge graphs---to bridge gazetteers and natural language. We identify four main limitations: evaluating frozen encoders measures baseline representations without testing whether gazetteer fine-tuning yields specialized models; space is inherently multi-perspective without a unified metric: point-based distance metrics diverge from coverage-based metrics; skewed spatial hierarchies violate i.i.d. assumptions, complicating statistical significance testing; and gazetteer pruning does not completely eliminate toponym--entry overlap , risking lexical leakage and score inflation. In this paper, we introduced a spatial-semantic indexing framework to across administrative hierarchies rather than relying on exact toponym matching. Unconstrained embedding retrieval often exhibits limited spatial fidelity. Conversely, incorporating explicit gazetteer structure via hierarchical constraints substantially improves coarse geographic grounding. However, these constraints offer limited benefit---and occasionally degrade performance on---fine-grained point-based metrics; single-scale metrics create artificial discrepancies when evaluating multi-scale geographic entities. Developing and evaluating models therefore requires new methodologies that directly account for hierarchical or multi-perspective spatial structures. These evaluation frameworks are critical as text encoders increasingly serve as primary interfaces in GIS, facing two core challenges in practice. First, training models solely on distributional semantics leads to mismatches with human spatial reasoning. Second, language models represent a manipulated digital geography subject to intentional alterations and malicious threats . Structured gazetteers thus remain essential to calibrate learned representations or evaluate model robustness against geographic incoherence. Nonetheless, human spatial language diverges from normative gazetteers through local subtleties . Future work will therefore explore how spatial knowledge graphs can bridge this gap by integrating richer semantic perspectives alongside gazetteers, and guiding the development of more grounded models.
