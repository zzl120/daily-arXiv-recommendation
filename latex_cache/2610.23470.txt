We extend this paradigm to recommendation for decision-making, focusing on decisions where the set of possible choices is fixed but the reasons for choosing them are not. In high-stakes decisions, e.g., which company to acquire or which customer to target, comparing diverse, well-reasoned options can be more useful than a single ranked list. We formalize as building a diverse, high-quality set of recommendations, each an item together with a , a natural-language reason why the item fits the needs of a decision maker. We propose QD-Rec, a quality--diversity method that selects items with a contextual bandit and generates hypotheses with a foundation model from earlier ones kept in an archive as stepping stones. A new recommendation enters the archive if it is novel or better than its nearest neighbor. Across M\&A, customer targeting, and paper and book recommendation, QD-Rec gives the highest mean quality and builds the largest archive in most settings. Open-ended search continually produces novel and increasingly capable or diverse discoveries, and each can serve as a stepping stone for further discovery . Foundation models can act as the generator in this search, drawing on earlier candidates to write new ones. This approach has found candidates that no single query to the model would produce, including creative text , adversarial prompts for red teaming , tasks that reveal a model's capabilities , loss functions , faster algorithms , new mathematical constructions , agent designs , and self-improving agents . We ask whether open-ended search can extend to recommendation for decision-making. Unlike open-ended generation, where the model generates new candidates, many decisions are choices from a fixed pool of candidates: an acquirer chooses among the companies that exist, a reader among the papers already written, a marketer among the customers. At first glance, this leaves no room for open-endedness, since the pool is finite and known in advance. However, only the items are fixed. The same item can be chosen for many different reasons, so the search over reasons is still open-ended. We therefore treat recommendation for such decisions as an open-ended search over item--hypothesis pairs. A is a natural-language explanation of why an item fits the decision maker's profile, such as the profile of one acquirer or one reader. The search returns a diverse, high-quality set of such pairs for a single profile, rather than one ranked list of items. We call this problem . In high-stakes decisions, people need to compare candidate items together with the reasons for choosing them . We evaluate the set by its quality and diversity, following the quality--diversity view of a space of alternatives . Open-ended recommendation has two properties that make open-ended search a good fit. First, evaluation is costly: we learn a recommendation's value only by writing its hypothesis and judging it, so we cannot simply score every possible recommendation in advance and sort them. The system must therefore decide which item to evaluate next and learn from each result. Second, the space is larger than the pool: each item allows unlimited hypotheses, so even evaluating every item once would leave most of the space unexplored. Good hypotheses often come from adapting or combining earlier ones, so keeping modest intermediate hypotheses as stepping stones can help . We briefly review quality--diversity methods and novelty search, evolutionary loops built around foundation models, and recommender systems. Quality--diversity methods aim to discover a of high-performing yet diverse solutions rather than a single optimum. Multi-dimensional Archive of Phenotypic Elites ``illuminates'' a search space by maintaining an archive and retaining elites across behavioral niches . Novelty search emphasizes exploration by rewarding behavioral novelty, providing a mechanism for stepping-stone trajectories in which intermediate solutions enable later progress . Recent work continues to revisit local competition and novelty definitions in QD, including dominated novelty variants , and extensions that reduce dependence on hand-designed behavioral descriptors, such as dimensionless MAP-Elites . A growing family of systems places a frozen large model inside an evolutionary or QD loop as a proposal operator or mutator, keeping an archive of artifacts that the model conditions on to propose the next: diverse creative text in QDAIF , adversarial prompts in Rainbow Teaming , code and program search in ELM and FunSearch , algorithm discovery in AlphaEvolve , sample-efficient program evolution in ShinkaEvolve , open-ended evaluation tasks in Automated Capability Discovery , and agent designs in ADAS . and AC/DC , they are themselves the artifacts being evolved. In a replication of Picbreeder , vision--language models take the place of its human evaluators.} A separate line of work is : STOP , the Darwin G\"odel Machine , the Huxley--G\"odel Machine , and Hyperagents turn the search on the machinery itself, so the improved scaffold or agent performs the next round of improvement. QD-Rec belongs to the first group, with item--hypothesis pairs as the artifacts. However, these systems create new artifacts, while QD-Rec must choose its items from a fixed pool. Recommenders learn either , from a fixed dataset of past interactions , or , as a sequential decision problem with an exploration--exploitation trade-off, usually modeled as a bandit . The evolutionary loops above are sequential as well, but they generate their candidates rather than choose them from a pool. QD-Rec does both, generating hypotheses for items selected from a fixed pool, so it selects items with a contextual bandit; unlike a recommender's bandit, however, it is judged, as in QD, by its final archive. Orthogonally, a long line of work treats diversity, novelty, coverage, and serendipity as first-class objectives , pursued offline by re-ranking and online by diversity-aware bandits . These methods diversify the list shown to a user; QD-Rec instead enforces diversity at archive admission, following recent QD systems : an LLM judges whether each new pair is novel relative to its nearest archive neighbors. We introduced QD-Rec, an archive-based quality--diversity method for open-ended recommendation, where the items are chosen with contextual bandits, and the reasons for recommending them are searched open-endedly. Across four domains, QD-Rec gave the best mean Quality in most settings and usually built the largest archive. As a first work along this line, we kept it relatively simple, and several directions follow. First, our bandit uses Bayesian linear regression to model reward from fixed item embeddings. Neural contextual bandits could capture nonlinear relationships between these embeddings and reward. More radically, the bandit could be discarded altogether in favor of a coding agent that reads the archive directly, reasons over which regions look exhausted and which look promising, and writes its own selection rule, replacing a fixed parametric reward model with a policy that can be inspected and revised in natural language. Second, exemplars are sampled uniformly from the archive, and could instead be selected by quality, recency, or how often they have already served as exemplars . Third, this work focused on the : which item to evaluate next, and which pairs to retain.
