Every item is cheap; the accompanying package implements the first three in a few lines of code. Include popularity, the course order , container continuation and population transitions , evaluated on exactly the same cases and candidates as the proposed method. They need no training and no hyperparameters, so there is no reason to omit them. Give $S_{}$, the lift $ $ of each method over it with a confidence interval, and the structural share $ $. A raw NDCG@10 of 0.7 means little if the course order alone reaches 0.7. Add the structure-corrected accuracy of Section~, which weights each test step by the inverse of its transition probability, to show how a method does on the steps the structure does not explain. Report the share and the metrics of steps along transitions never seen in training and, where containers exist, of off-path steps: this is where a recommender has a choice to make. When the logs record who initiated each activity, split by initiator as well. For LLM rankers and any evaluation on candidate sets, report results with structural negatives in addition to random ones, and always score the structural predictors on the same sets. Leave-last-out and a global temporal split agreed here on the best structural predictor and on most comparisons, but absolute scores were far lower under the temporal split and the structural share fell on two datasets. The temporal split also avoids training on the future of other learners . Next-activity accuracy measures how well a model imitates the sequencing policy that produced the data. Claims about learning require outcomes: randomised sequencing data, field experiments, or at least policy evaluation under several student models . On public educational logs, next-activity accuracy is dominated by how well a method reproduces the sequencing that teachers and platforms imposed. This does not make offline evaluation useless, but it changes what a result means. A method that beats the structural predictors by a clear margin predicts logged choices better than the platform's typical paths; a method that does not, whatever its absolute score, has shown nothing about personalisation. Conversely, a policy designed for learning, such as practising at a desirable difficulty, is penalised precisely because it departs from the logged policy, so offline accuracy can rank pedagogically motivated recommenders below simple imitators. The simulation of Section~ shows how far this can go: when the platform decides most steps, a recommender that serves every learner's needs ranks near the bottom by offline accuracy, and a recommender selected by offline accuracy learns the platform. The consequence for the field is not to stop offline evaluation but to report it against the right floor and on the right steps. Educational platforms are designed to sequence. Mastery-based problem sets draw the next problem from a small pool , applications serve questions part by part , knowledge maps suggest the next exercise from prerequisites , teachers assign quizzes within schemes of work , and a MOOC lays out its activities in a course. Each of these mechanisms produces low-branching, highly repeatable transitions, the very structures that make sequential benchmarks shortcut-solvable . In addition, the datasets most used for educational recommendation were collected for knowledge tracing, a task for which the sequencing was irrelevant; they were never meant to evaluate the choice of activities. Peer-based methods such as MCLD were designed for a class following one course, with recommendations that come with a one-sentence explanation . Next-new-activity accuracy rewards neither their transparency nor their remediation component, and on long, strongly sequenced histories their set-based view of the learner is outperformed by the transition heuristic. On A15, where activities are whole problem sets, MCLD is close to the best methods under leave-last-out and the best method under the global temporal split. Their value has to be established on the steps where a choice is made, and ideally in deployment. For LLM-based recommenders, our results with open models of up to 7 billion parameters are a caution rather than a verdict: much larger models may well rank candidates better than the ones we could run, although within our range size made no difference. Whatever the model, its accuracy on candidate sets is uninterpretable unless the structural predictors and a simple lexical baseline are scored on the same sets, and unless structural negatives are reported. The study is offline and observational: it shows how much of the measured accuracy is attainable from the structure, not which recommendations would help learners. The container information differs across datasets , and in A12 and A17 the skill stands in for the problem set, which probably understates the structural share there. The LLMs are open-weight models of up to 7 billion parameters run on CPUs, the two largest 4-bit quantized and scored on cases per set, chosen so that the experiment can be reproduced without special hardware or paid services; much larger models may behave differently, although the dependence of every method on the type of negatives does not depend on the LLM. The MOOC activities are anonymised, so the MOOC has no container or course metadata and cannot enter the LLM comparison, and DKT was trained with fixed settings and a short budget. EdNet is represented by a public sample of a few thousand learners and Junyi by a random sample of 20,000 learners. SASRec reached its training budget on , its main results come from one training per dataset , and under P2 the A09 test set is small . Finally, the hyperparameter grids are modest; longer training and better-tuned personalised models could reduce $ $ somewhat, but they would have to close the large gaps observed on off-path and learner-chosen steps to change the conclusions. Recommenders and LLMs evaluated on educational logs largely learn the platform: they beat the syllabus, or more exactly the typical paths that teachers and platforms impose, by much less than their absolute scores suggest. Across eight datasets from five platforms, a predictor that only knows the last activity delivers between and of the best offline accuracy, mostly through the population's typical paths. Personalised recommenders improve on it significantly on datasets, by to NDCG@10, but none does well on steps along unseen transitions where these are frequent enough to measure, and none closes the gap on steps that leave the current container or that learners choose themselves, and small open LLMs do not beat the best structural predictor on the same candidates except where every method is close to the random level. On-path steps are typically decided by the platform or the assignment ; off-path steps are those where a teacher, the platform or the learner switches to something else, which is exactly where a recommender would have to make a choice. When the logs record who initiated an activity, as EdNet and Junyi do, we also compare activities started by the platform with activities chosen by the learner. Recommender systems for learning propose the next exercise, problem set, video or quiz to a learner, and they are increasingly built with the same machinery as recommenders in other domains: collaborative filtering, sequential neural models and, most recently, large language models . Most of them are developed and compared offline, on logs collected by tutoring systems, learning applications and MOOCs: a model sees what a learner has done so far and is rewarded when it ranks high the activity that the learner did next . In educational logs, however, the next activity is seldom a free choice of the learner. A teacher assigns problem sets in a chosen order; a tutor draws the next problem from the current set until a mastery criterion is met; an application's engine selects the next question; a course has a syllabus. The logged sequence is the output of a that the platform, the teacher and the curriculum mostly control . A model can therefore score well offline by reproducing that policy, without learning anything about what a particular learner needs. This is a shortcut in the sense of : a decision rule that succeeds on the benchmark but not on the intended task. Recent work found such shortcuts in general sequential-recommendation benchmarks, where a training-free transition heuristic matches modern generative recommenders . In educational data the shortcut is not an accident of dataset construction; it is the design of the platform. Early MOOC studies already noted that following the syllabus predicts a sizeable share of next resources , but no study has measured how much of the reported accuracy of educational recommenders the structure explains, on which steps personalisation adds anything, and what this implies for LLM-based recommenders, which are evaluated on small candidate sets where the choice of negatives is decisive . This paper asks a simple question: when a recommender or an LLM scores well on such logs, has it learned the learner or the platform? Put differently, , where by the syllabus we mean, broadly, the sequencing that the course and the platform impose: the course order, the grouping of activities into problem sets, parts or topics, and the typical paths that assignments produce. A series of reproducibility studies has shown that progress claimed for new recommendation algorithms often disappears against well-tuned simple baselines. could reproduce only a minority of recent neural approaches, and most of those were outperformed by nearest-neighbour or linear methods; found that a carefully tuned dot product beat a learned similarity; and reported that nearest-neighbour heuristics were in most cases preferable to neural session-based recommenders. Other work has examined the evaluation procedure itself: sampled metrics can rank methods inconsistently with full rankings , splitting data without respecting the global timeline leaks future information , and many offline studies use flawed datasets, splits or metrics . Recorded interactions are themselves shaped by the system that produced them: recommendations act as treatments whose exposure biases the logs , feedback loops make the data more homogeneous , and offline protocols built on such logs need to be interpreted with care . Our study applies this line of scrutiny to a domain in which the exposure is not incidental but designed. Shortcut learning describes models that succeed on a benchmark through decision rules that do not reflect the intended task and that fail to transfer . In recommendation, recently showed that a training-free transition-graph heuristic, using only the last one or two items, matches or beats modern generative recommenders on many standard sequential benchmarks, and traced this to low-branching transitions, feature-smooth transitions and weak dependence on long histories. Educational logs are an extreme case of this situation, because their transitions are produced on purpose by curricula, teachers and adaptive engines. To our knowledge, no study has quantified this source of predictability across educational datasets, separated it from personalisation, or examined how it affects LLM-based recommenders. Recommender systems for technology-enhanced learning have been reviewed repeatedly , and surveys of their evaluation document a wide variety of methods and goals beyond accuracy . Instructional sequencing has also been studied as a policy-learning problem: the robust evaluation matrix compares instructional policies under several student simulators , and a review of reinforcement learning for instructional sequencing examines when data-driven sequencing policies have improved on simpler baselines . A public preprocessed version of OLI Engineering Statics was examined and discarded: its items appear to be problem steps, as 74\ Given a history, a method ranks all activities the learner has not done yet, and the target is the next activity the learner started that was new to them. Activities already in the history are removed from the ranking of every method. This matches the purpose of an activity recommender and removes the trivial gains of re-recommending past activities. For each learner, the last new step is the test target and the previous new step the validation target; models are trained on all learners' sequences cut just before their validation target. This is the protocol of most sequential recommendation studies, and the one under audit. Hyperparameters are selected on validation cases by NDCG@10, and the selected configuration is evaluated on all test cases. Because leave-last-out mixes time periods across learners , we repeat the study with a global cut-off at the 80th percentile of step times: models are trained on all steps before the cut-off, and each learner active on both sides contributes one case whose target is their first new activity after it. Hyperparameters are selected with an earlier cut-off on the training period only. We report HR@$K$ and NDCG@10 over the full ranking of new activities. Since an LLM cannot rank thousands of activities in one prompt, all methods are compared on candidate sets of 20 activities: the target and 19 negatives the learner has not done. We use two kinds of negatives. negatives are drawn uniformly. negatives are drawn from the 20 activities ranked highest by MC and the 20 ranked highest by CONT, so that every candidate is plausible from the course structure alone. By construction they penalise MC and CONT, whose own top-ranked activities they are; their purpose is to test the other methods on decisions that the structure cannot make. The LLM scores candidates by likelihood. The prompt describes the platform, lists the learner's ten most recent activities with their names and outcomes, and ends with ``The next activity the student starts is:''. The score of a candidate is the mean log-probability of its name as the continuation, minus the mean log-probability of the same name after a prompt that contains no history, a pointwise mutual information that removes the model's preference for short or frequent names. Activity names are those provided by the datasets: skill names for A09, part names for EdNet, exercise and topic names for Junyi and subject names for Eedi .
