Despite these advances, existing retrieval paradigms are typically target-centric: a query either directly specifies the target or points to a relatively well-defined target concept. Real-world e-commerce search, however, often begins with a need rather than a target. Users may upload an image of their situation and ask how to fulfill a need or solve a problem without naming any desired product. Retrieving useful products for such queries requires reasoning from the user's intent to possible solutions. Importantly, a single intent may admit solutions from multiple product categories that differ substantially in appearance and function. Consider a shopper who uploads a photo of a rusty kitchen knife and asks, ``How can I restore it to look like new?'' Rather than pointing to a single target, this need admits solutions from multiple product categories. A food-safe rust remover and a knife rust eraser offer effective solutions from different categories, whereas fine-grit sandpaper is applicable but less specialized. Other candidates may appear relevant but are not valid solutions: an industrial rust remover may remove the rust but violate implicit food-safety constraints, while a new knife is related to the query but fails to fulfill the user's intent. Thus, evaluating such candidates requires fine-grained relevance judgments based on whether and how well they fulfill the underlying intent. Existing benchmarks do not adequately support the evaluation of this retrieval setting. Semantic-matching benchmarks such as M-BEIR~, MERIT~, and MCMR~ primarily evaluate retrieval based on direct semantic correspondence between queries and relevant items. Reasoning-intensive benchmarks such as RMIR~ and MRMR~ require reasoning toward an unstated target, but their relevant items typically belong to a single target category and use binary relevance labels. To our knowledge, no existing benchmark jointly supports reasoning-intensive retrieval, multi-category solutions, and fine-grained relevance labels. To fill this gap, we introduce MIPR , a benchmark grounded in real Chinese e-commerce search logs, comprising 3,522 multimodal queries and a shared corpus of 30,731 products. Each query is an image--question pair that expresses a user need without directly specifying a target product, requiring models to reason from the multimodal intent to suitable products that may span multiple categories. Each product consists of an image and a textual description. Unlike target-centric retrieval, a query in MIPR may be fulfilled by products from multiple categories. We distinguish two levels of positive relevance and three types of negative relevance: products that directly and effectively fulfill the user's intent while satisfying all applicable constraints; products that provide a usable but less effective, less specialized, or auxiliary solution; products that could otherwise fulfill the intent but violate a critical explicit or implicit safety, compatibility, or applicability constraint; products that are visually, semantically, or categorically related to the query context but do not fulfill the intent; and products that neither fulfill the intent nor constitute an N1 or N2 negative. Because free-form solution boundaries are difficult to define and annotate consistently, we use Taobao's existing product taxonomy as an operational proxy. Each product is associated with a three- or four-level hierarchical category path, whose leaf category serves as the evaluation unit. This platform-defined taxonomy provides consistent and measurable units while preserving meaningful functional distinctions among products. MIPR targets queries that express a product-fulfillable intent through the combination of an image and a question, without directly specifying the target product. Real search logs, however, are of mixed quality and contain many queries that do not meet this requirement. We therefore curate queries that require joint multimodal understanding, provide sufficient information for product selection, and cannot be resolved through direct matching to an explicitly specified target. Details are provided in Appendix~. We first apply conservative rules to remove clearly ineligible queries, followed by a lightweight model that filters additional obvious mismatches, reducing the initial pool to approximately 30K queries. To derive a consistent representation of each remaining query, Claude Sonnet 4.6 produces a structured query analysis comprising an image description, the inferred user intent, and explicit product-selection constraints. This analysis is reused throughout the subsequent construction stages. GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro~ then independently assess query eligibility based on both the original image--question pair and the structured analysis. Only queries accepted by both models are retained, resulting in approximately 9.8K queries. To reduce such redundancy, we embed the inferred intents, cluster them using a cosine-similarity threshold of 0.7, and retain one representative from each cluster. This step yields approximately 3.7K queries for the subsequent construction stages. For each curated query, we discover candidate products in two steps: first generating product terms that cover plausible solution types, and then retrieving concrete products through multiple complementary routes. To reach solutions spanning multiple categories, we adopt a two-stage enumeration-and-reflection procedure using Qwen3.6-27B. The model receives the original question and the structured query analysis, including the visual context, inferred intent, and product-selection constraints. It first enumerates product terms from four perspectives: products designed for the need, that achieve a similar outcome, products that support a primary solution, and required for a solution, where the latter two mainly contribute auxiliary solutions. The model then reflects on the initial enumeration to recover omitted solution types and remove redundant or off-topic terms, retaining at most six product terms per query. Human validation on 50 queries shows that the reflection step increases coverage of human-identified valid product categories from 81\ To avoid restricting candidate discovery to the generated product terms, we combine term-based retrieval with direct intent-based retrieval. For each generated term, we retrieve candidate products using BM25, a fused ranking produced by two text embedding models, and a vision--language embedding model. In parallel, we directly use the inferred query intent to retrieve products through the text and vision--language embedding routes. Candidates from all routes are merged by product ID and deduplicated, resulting in approximately 50 candidate products per query on average. The retriever configurations, fusion procedure, and retrieval depths are detailed in Appendix~. Rather than evaluating against the full catalog of approximately 1M products, we construct a shared corpus from the union of the candidates discovered for all curated queries. This design concentrates annotation effort on a broad but tractable set of potentially relevant products, while the shared corpus preserves cross-query competition: products introduced through one query remain retrievable for every other query. Merging all query-specific candidate sets produces an initial corpus of 53,786 unique products. We further reduce near-duplicate product variants, which would otherwise dominate retrieval results without meaningfully increasing solution diversity. Specifically, we encode each product's image and title using Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B~ and cluster products within each leaf category. Each cluster is represented by the product closest to its centroid, and query--candidate pairs involving other cluster members are redirected to this representative, yielding a shared corpus of 30,731 products. Finally, Claude Sonnet 4.6 generates an image description for each retained product for use in subsequent relevance annotation. Clustering thresholds and detailed statistics are provided in Appendix~. We present MIPR, a multimodal benchmark for intent-to-product retrieval constructed from real Chinese e-commerce search logs. MIPR jointly captures reasoning-intensive retrieval, multi-category solutions, and fine-grained relevance labels. Experiments show that embedding retrievers often retrieve useful candidates but struggle to rank optimal products highly and cover multiple solution categories. Although reranking substantially improves performance, the large gap to the candidate-pool oracle indicates considerable room for improvement. We hope MIPR and its construction pipeline will facilitate future research on this challenging retrieval setting. MIPR is constructed from Taobao search logs and product listings collected and processed under the platform's user agreements, privacy policies, and applicable data-governance requirements. The data are used solely for research on multimodal retrieval. Query and product identifiers are replaced with hashed IDs, and personally identifiable information and user-identifying metadata are removed. The benchmark data, annotations, and code are undergoing Taobao's internal privacy, security, copyright, and compliance review, which will be completed prior to release. This review covers both textual and visual content, including potential personal information and intellectual-property concerns. Upon acceptance, the processed queries, product data, relevance judgments, construction code, and evaluation code will be publicly released. Each product term is issued to all three routes, and the inferred intent is additionally issued to the fused text embedding route and the multimodal embedding model. Every route retains its top-5 products, so a query with $n$ product terms yields at most $5 $ candidates. After merging by product ID, each query retains 50 candidate products on average. To reduce redundancy in the union of query-level candidates, we encode each product's image and title using Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B~ and perform similarity-based clustering. Within each leaf category, we apply leader clustering with a cosine-similarity threshold of 0.60. This reduces the corpus from 53,786 to 30,731 products and produces 7,347 non-singleton clusters. We retain the product with the highest average similarity to the other members as the representative and redirect the associated query--candidate pairs accordingly. As a sanity check, one annotator inspected the 20 largest non-singleton clusters. These clusters primarily contain near-duplicate or interchangeable listings of standardized products, such as screwdrivers, shoe cleaners, and insoles, often differing only in packaging or vendor. No inspected cluster contains functionally distinct products. Qwen3.6-27B performs conservative binary screening designed to retain ambiguous, partially applicable, visually related, and potentially constraint-violating candidates while assigning N3 to clearly unrelated products. Using the evaluated models as pooling systems ensures direct judgment coverage of the ranked lists reported in our experiments. The models also differ in architecture and training data, providing complementary candidate sets. We merge their retrieved products by product ID and annotate every previously unjudged query--product pair using the same coarse-screening and five-way annotation procedure described in Section~. Pooling adds 299,479 judgments, increasing the average number of judged products per query from 42.1 to 127.1. The additional judgments contain, on average, 1.44 L1, 3.70 L2, 1.77 N1, 15.11 N2, and 63.00 N3 products per query. In addition to identifying previously unjudged positive products, pooling therefore provides broader coverage of challenging negatives, particularly constraint-violating and related non-solution products. Because the pools are constructed from a fixed set of evaluated models, future systems may retrieve relevant products that remain unjudged and may therefore be affected by incomplete-judgment bias. As in standard pooled evaluation, pairs outside the judgment pools are treated as non-relevant. We release the complete pool memberships, per-model contributions, and final qrels to enable future judgment expansion.
