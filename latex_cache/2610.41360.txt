{>{ }X} { } {{#1}} } } } In modern information retrieval , documents are increasingly encountered as structured sequences of visually rich, paginated units, rather than as flat text. Example documents encountered in our daily life include Word files, PDFs, and slide decks. Free text-centric retrieval systems either ignore this structure entirely or destroy it through arbitrary passage chunking. In this paper, we formulate page-centric Visual Document Retrieval with pages as the fundamental indexing units and document context available for assessing page relevance. The formulation distinguishes two retrieval granularities, page and document, and three retrieval paradigms: independent-item, evidence-set, and iterative retrieval. We then assess 42 benchmarks using eight criteria covering corpus characteristics, query construction, relevance annotation, and ranking support. Our analysis shows two main findings on current VDR research. First, evaluation remains concentrated on page-level independent-item retrieval, with fewer resources supporting document-level, evidence-set, and iterative settings. Second, queries originating from real user information needs independently of target corpus content and graded relevance judgments are uncommon, while the coverage and verification of relevance judgments vary substantially. Both findings reveal gaps between the capabilities applications require and those existing evaluation protocols can establish. The layout, typography, and embedded figures or tables carry meaning that is inseparable from the surrounding text in each or a few pages in sequence. Traditional text-centric IR systems handle such corpora poorly: they either treat a document as an unstructured stream of text or rely on arbitrary passage chunking, both of which discard the spatial pagination and visual structure that document authors use to convey relevance-bearing information~. In fact, this points to a more basic mismatch. In classical IR, a "document" is a of information defined for the purposes of indexing and retrieval: a convenient abstraction, not a claim about how information is actually packaged. That abstraction often diverges from the documents we produce and read day to day, which are , artifacts. This calls for serious consideration of a page-centric view of information retrieval: treating the page rather than the document as the fundamental unit of indexing and retrieval. In IR, documents are often represented and retrieved as whole documents or smaller passages using sparse or dense representations. Passage-level retrieval commonly relies on chunking documents into fixed-length token spans, disregarding their original layout. In VDR, pages serve a similar functional role as passages, providing sub-document units suitable for embedding, retrieval, and downstream processing, such as answer generation. However, the two approaches differ in how retrieval boundaries are determined. Token-based chunking imposes artificial boundaries, often dictated by model constraints, which may split semantically coherent elements such as tables or figure captions. In contrast, page boundaries are inherent to fixed-layout documents, such as PDFs and slide decks, and often reflect layout decisions intended to preserve the semantic coherence of visual and textual content. Page-level retrieval thus leverages the document's existing layout structure rather than imposing arbitrary token-based boundaries. This makes pages a natural retrieval unit for visual documents, as reflected in the predominance of page-level evaluation among existing VDR benchmarks . Nevertheless, page boundaries do not always strictly align with semantic boundaries. Identifying the relevance of an individual page may require contextual information from neighboring pages or the entire document. Document-aware passage retrieval provides a useful precedent for addressing such dependencies. DAPR~ and ConTEB~ investigate text retrieval settings where identifying relevant passages requires information beyond the passages themselves. More recently, CMDR~ extends this perspective to visual documents by introducing context-dependent page retrieval and contextual multimodal embeddings within an intra-document retrieval setting. Building on these studies, our task formulation explicitly accounts for document context when assessing page relevance . Accordingly, our benchmark analysis examines whether existing evaluation settings can meaningfully assess such contextual dependencies . Early VDR benchmarks often repurposed document visual question answering datasets by treating questions as retrieval queries and their source images as relevant targets, as exemplified by ViDoRe V1~ and VisRAG~. Subsequent benchmarks have expanded evaluation along several dimensions, including multi-page and multi-document collections~, more realistic query construction~, graded relevance judgments~, and complementary evidence retrieval across reasoning hops~. More recent efforts further explore whole-document relevance~ and iterative evidence acquisition~. Collectively, these developments have broadened VDR evaluation beyond conventional single-item retrieval toward more diverse retrieval objectives and settings. Building on these advances, we systematically examine benchmark design choices in and assess their suitability for different retrieval requirements in . Recent surveys have extensively reviewed VDR and document RAG from different perspectives. Zhang~ categorizes MLLMs by their roles in VDR as captioners, multimodal embedders, and end-to-end representers, examining retrieval granularity and fidelity--efficiency trade-offs. Gao et al.~ organize document RAG models by domain, modality, and granularity, while summarizing benchmark characteristics, including corpus scale, content types, and reported retrieval and generation performance. Most relevant to ours, is Yan et al.~. In this dedicated survey of VDR, authors analyz existing benchmarks in terms of corpus and query sizes, multilingual coverage, reasoning requirements, and evaluation metrics under a page-image ranking formulation. They further distinguish retrieval methodologies from their applications in RAG and agentic systems. Not focusing on methodological aspects of VDR, our work complements these surveys by systematically assessing benchmark suitability against an explicit VDR task formulation and its application requirements. Specifically, we distinguish page-level and document-level ranking, and three retrieval paradigms: independent-item, evidence-set, and iterative retrieval. Based on this formulation and the practical applications, we establish 8 operational criteria covering corpus structure and scale, query construction, relevance coverage, result cardinality, relevance grades, and ranking support. We then evaluate 42 benchmarks against these criteria and use the resulting assessments to provide task-specific benchmark recommendations. Rather than merely cataloging benchmark characteristics, our analysis aims to clarify which retrieval capabilities each benchmark can meaningfully evaluate, establish a common vocabulary for describing benchmark properties and identifying potential evaluation gaps and biases. A benchmark's ability to faithfully evaluate a task depends not merely on whether its input/output format matches the task's specification, but on whether the process by which it was constructed actually reflects the task's underlying requirements. The comparison of SQuAD2.0~ and Natural Questions~ in~illustrates this point: SQuAD2.0 contains questions written from passages that already contain the answer, whereas Natural Questions is based on real search queries posed before the answer is known. Consequently, despite sharing the same format, the datasets represent different tasks, reflected in differences in lexical diversity, question specificity, and syntactic variety. This distinction generalizes into two broadly different paths for benchmark construction. The first built for a different task, by reformatting it to match the input/output signature of the target task. This path is inexpensive, which is why many benchmarks accumulate around a convenient format rather than a carefully specified task, but it offers no guarantee that the repurposed dataset reflects the target task's actual constraints, query distribution, or notion of relevance. The resulting benchmark may end up testing whether a model fits the idiosyncrasies of the source dataset rather than whether it solves the intended task. The second path . The dataset is developed by enumerating the task's requirements first: who the intended users are, what constitutes a query, what relevance judgments should capture. Then, the collection and/or annotation process is guided to meet the task definition. This is the same discipline that motivates datasheets for datasets~ and data statements~, which require a dataset's creators to document its motivation, the situation in which the underlying content was produced, and its collection and annotation process, precisely so that a downstream user can judge whether the dataset actually matches the task they intend to evaluate. However, we argue that benchmarks for generic visual document retrieval should not overemphasize non-textual visual evidence at the expense of textual content. In real-world documents, text remains a primary carrier of semantic information, and effective retrieval should leverage both textual and visual signals. Moreover, under our framework, the document page serves as the fundamental retrieval and indexing unit, with its layout, typography, and structural organization also contributing to the evidence available for retrieval. Therefore, we do not require every test case to involve non-textual visual evidence, nor do we score visual dependence. Among all evaluated benchmarks, only TaR-ViR~ adopts a query-to-source . Specifically, it reuses queries from the textual retrieval dataset NQ-TABLES~, whose queries originate from the Google search logs used in Natural Questions~, and identifies relevant items for those queries within a newly constructed multimodal corpus. All remaining benchmarks follow a source-to-query paradigm, in which human annotators or LLMs generate queries after inspecting the corpus content. This process introduces two potential biases. First, the resulting queries may not reflect how users express information needs in practice, where queries are often underspecified, informal, and shaped by varying levels of domain knowledge~. As a result, benchmark performance may not accurately predict real-world retrieval effectiveness. Second, because source-to-query construction begins with a known source, it guarantees the existence of supporting evidence by design. This may underrepresent realistic scenarios in which the corpus contains only partially relevant evidence or no relevant item at all. Although source-to-query annotation is widely used because real search queries are difficult to obtain, some benchmarks explicitly seek to reduce its associated bias. For example, ViDoRe V2~ and V3~ recognize that annotators often have access to corpus content when generating queries, unlike real users. To mitigate this discrepancy, they restrict annotators to document summaries and metadata rather than the full source content. Text and image descriptions are first extracted from PDFs, summarized by an LLM, and then clustered through embedding and dimensionality reduction. Selected single-section and cross-section summaries are provided to annotators as the basis for query creation. ViDoRe V3 also supplements human-written queries with LLM-generated synthetic queries. Future benchmarks could further reduce source-induced bias by adopting query collection procedures that more closely reflect real-world information-seeking conditions. We next examine whether benchmarks attempt to identify all relevant pages or documents for each query. Under our criteria, only a small number explicitly assess relevance across the entire corpus and therefore support . For example, REAL-MM-RAG~ applies an MLLM to every page in the corpus to determine whether it contains sufficient information to answer a query. Queries are retained only when no page other than the annotated target is identified as answer-bearing, yielding a dataset in which each query is associated with a single verified relevant page. The benchmark further samples top-ranked retrieval results for human review to detect potential false-negative annotations. More scalable approaches approximate corpus-wide assessment by first constructing a bounded candidate set through retrieval, clustering, knowledge-graph traversal, or model-based pre-screening, followed by human or model-assisted relevance annotation. Examples include DocViRe~, ViDoRe V3~, PaperScope~, and MultiDocR~. MultiDocR, for instance, uses a retrieval-reranking-annotation pipeline: a retriever generates candidate pages, a reranker refines the set, and an MLLM assigns graded relevance labels. Such approaches improve scalability and reduce the likelihood of missing relevant items. Beyond explicit corpus-wide relevance assessment, several benchmarks adopt indirect strategies to reduce annotation ambiguity and improve label reliability. A common approach is to make queries sufficiently discriminative so that the intended target is unlikely to be confused with other candidates. Some benchmarks achieve this by incorporating multiple entities or constraints into each query~, while others rely on LLMs to enforce specificity. For example, IRPAPERS~ prompts the model to generate questions that are answerable by a unique page and to provide supporting justification, whereas SeaDoc~ filters generated queries based on their relevance and exclusivity to the designated target. Other benchmarks apply post-hoc validation after query construction. IRPAPERS~, for instance, evaluates query discriminativeness by replacing the gold document with a hard negative and measuring the resulting change in downstream RAG performance. Although performance degradation suggests that the annotated document contains more useful evidence, it does not eliminate the possibility of false negatives, since the alternative document may still contain partially relevant information. KoViDoRe~ instead uses a consistency-based filtering strategy, retaining a retrieved page as relevant only when it belongs to the same document from which the query was generated. While this improves annotation consistency, it may overlook relevant pages from other documents. {!}{} The third group of dimensions examines the number of annotated , whether is binary or graded, and the : page-level, document-level, or both. The first category follows the traditional IR setting, in which each query-item pair is annotated independently without explicitly modeling relationships among relevant items. Such annotations are most appropriate for the independent-item retrieval paradigm discussed in . Representative benchmarks include DocViRe~ and LitTraceQA~ for document-level retrieval, and ViDoRe V2/V3~ and MultiDocR~ for page-level retrieval., some benchmarks incorporate multi-hop queries but retain item-wise relevance annotations and evaluation protocols. The second category focuses on complementary evidence retrieval. Benchmarks hence assess whether the retrieved results collectively provide sufficient evidence to answer the query. For example, Double-Bench~ defines multi-hop retrieval success as retrieving at least one supporting page for every reasoning hop within the top-$K$ results, measured by Hit@$K$. Although this setting imposes additional requirements on retrieval success, it still involves annotating multiple relevant items per query, typically spanning at least two distinct pages or documents. We therefore include it within the broader category of benchmarks supporting multiple relevant items per query. These categories reflect two distinct retrieval paradigms. However, both fit naturally within the retrieval framework introduced in ~, which returns a ranked list of relevant items. Their primary distinction lies in the evaluation criterion: retrieved items are judged either independently by individual relevance or collectively according to the completeness of the evidence they provide. Most VDR benchmarks adopt binary judgments, while only few very recent benchmarks provide graded relevance annotations. This is partly attributable to the prevalence of single-positive annotations. Among benchmarks with graded relevance, ViDoRe V3~ and KoViDoRe~ distinguish among irrelevant, key-relevant, and sufficient-relevant pages. Nayana-IR~ also adopts a three-level scheme, assigning the highest relevance to the exact target page, partial relevance to structurally related pages from the same parent document, and zero relevance otherwise. MultiDocR~ introduces a finer-grained five-level scheme, distinguishing fully, partially relevant, inference-relevant, topically relevant, and irrelevant pages. Collectively, these schemes move beyond binary relevance judgments to characterize how directly and sufficiently each item addresses a query. We advocate a flexible annotation setting that allows a variable number of relevant items per query, with graded judgments capturing their different degrees of relevance. Such a setting avoids assuming a fixed number of relevant results or treating all relevant items as equally sufficient to satisfy the query. Page-level retrieval remains dominant in VDR benchmarks. A common special case treats self-contained single-page images as nominal ``documents''~. Although such settings technically support document-level retrieval, they do not evaluate selection among intact multi-page documents. Furthermore, most benchmarks based on multi-page documents treat pages as independent retrieval units rather than modeling cross-page dependencies. As a result, they offer limited assessment of document-level retrieval and reasoning over information distributed across multiple pages. An emerging direction that addresses this limitation is , in which information from intermediate pages is required to identify the target page, even when those pages provide little direct evidence for answering the query. CMDR~ introduces this setting for intra-document retrieval, while VisDocAgentBench~ extends it to VDR. In VisDocAgentBench, queries provide an initial semantic clue without directly specifying the target, requiring the retriever to navigate through supporting pages linked by latent semantic relationships. Although these pages may not contribute directly to answer generation, they act as essential intermediaries for locating the final evidence. The benchmark is explicitly designed for iterative retrieval, making it particularly suitable for agentic approaches that interleave planning, retrieval, and reasoning across multiple steps rather than relying on a single retrieval pass. Contextual retrieval differs from conventional multi-hop evidence retrieval. In multi-hop retrieval, multiple pages jointly provide the evidence needed to answer a query. In contextual retrieval, by contrast, intermediate pages primarily help locate the target evidence. As noted in~, many existing multi-hop queries explicitly mention concepts associated with individual evidence pages, allowing those pages to be retrieved directly from the query without exploiting dependencies between pages. Consequently, strong performance on this parallel multi-hop retrieval does not necessarily indicate contextual retrieval capability. For document-level retrieval, native document-level supervision remains relatively uncommon, with some document-level evaluation protocols derived directly from page-level annotations. For example, MMEB-V2~ infers document-level relevance from page-level labels, even though its queries target localized page content rather than document-level information. More direct document-level formulations include whole-document topical retrieval in DocViRe~ and research-paper discovery in PaperScope~. Beyond page- and document-level ranking, recent benchmarks have begun to incorporate finer-grained evidence localization. ViDoRe V3~ augments page relevance with region grounding, M4DocBench~ links relevant documents and pages to supporting layouts, LitTraceQA~ associates pages with evidence quotations, and BenthamQA and HW-SQuAD~ combine page retrieval with answer-snippet localization. Overall, existing VDR benchmarks remain dominated by independent page-level ranking. Native document-level supervision and fine-grained evidence localization are comparatively rare, while contextual retrieval highlights the need to evaluate dependencies across pages rather than only the direct relevance between individual pages and queries. We now introduce a structured framework for characterizing and comparing VDR benchmarks. The framework is not intended to define a single notion of an "ideal" benchmark, and the resulting scores should not be interpreted as evidence of universal superiority across retrieval scenarios. Rather, its goal is to provide a systematic lens through which benchmark design choices can be examined. It serves two complementary purposes. First, it brings together considerations that are often discussed in isolation, including corpus construction, annotation methodology, relevance granularity, and application alignment. Second, it makes explicit the trade-offs inherent in benchmark development. By synthesizing practices observed across existing VDR benchmarks, the framework provides a common vocabulary for describing benchmark characteristics, identifying potential gaps or biases, and facilitating more transparent comparisons. Organizations store institutional knowledge in PDFs, slide decks, and scanned reports whose relevant content is frequently non-textual: a pricing table, an architecture diagram, or a chart of quarterly results. Many such reports are long documents with tens of even hundreds of pages. A user searching an internal knowledge base needs the exact page containing the answer, not a document-level pointer that still requires manual skimming. This application is primarily : it requires large, heterogeneous document corpora with page-granular gold labels, and its practical value is proportional to how large and visually diverse the underlying corpus is. A complementary need arises for holistic requests such as ``find the deck that introduces product X'', the contract that is a lease, or the paper that surveys a topic . Here no single page need be relevant by itself, so page scores can only approximate document relevance . Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems that answer questions over PDF corpora fail silently when their retriever discards visual evidence. A chart, a signature block, or a table cell could be the only place an answer lives, and no amount of generation quality compensates for a retriever that never surfaces it~. In practice this application needs granularities jointly. Page-level retrieval supplies the generator with the specific evidence pages. Document-level retrieval determines which whole documents are even worth pulling into the retrieval pool for answer generation, which matters once a corpus grows beyond what fits in a single context window~. Without document-level filtering, page-level retrieval alone may not come with the necessary context for citation-backed generation, critical for enterprise corpora. Financial filings, contracts, and regulatory reports are simultaneously long, numerically dense, and high-stakes, in the sense that an incorrect or ungrounded answer has direct legal or financial consequences. Practitioners in this domain routinely need two distinct capabilities: filings or contracts are relevant to a query, and , within a relevant document, the specific clause, number, or table appears. The former is document-level ranking, since an analyst may need to triage hundreds of candidate filings. The latter is page-level ranking, since citing the correct source location is often a regulatory or professional requirement. Text-only retrieval is a particularly poor fit here because financial and legal documents rely heavily on tabular and structured layout to convey numerical relationships. Some information needs cannot be answered from a single page or even a single document. This section explains how the evolving requirements of RAG have shaped the three VDR retrieval paradigms introduced in . RAG enhances large language models by incorporating external information into the answer generation process. In a typical RAG pipeline, a retriever identifies relevant content from a knowledge corpus, and a generator uses the retrieved evidence to formulate a response. Because the quality of the retrieved content directly influences the relevance, completeness, and factual grounding of generated answers, retrieval is a critical component of RAG. From a page-centric indexing and search perspective, RAG can be viewed as a two-stage process comprising visual document retrieval to identify relevant pages, followed by answer generation. Indeed, with the development of RAG, VDR has increasingly extended beyond retrieving individually relevant pages toward acquiring multiple pieces of evidence to support complex question answering. However, such multi-page retrieval can involve different objectives, leading to distinct retrieval and evaluation settings. Two key distinctions emerge: , and . Based on these distinctions, we discuss three retrieval paradigms commonly considered in existing VDR benchmarks. , where each retrieved item is evaluated independently according to its relevance to the query. Even when multiple pages or documents are relevant to the same query, their relevance labels are assigned individually, and retrieval is evaluated based on the resulting ranked list. Standard IR metrics are therefore applicable. For example, nDCG@$K$ is commonly used when graded relevance judgments are available, whereas Recall@$K$, MAP, and MRR are widely adopted when relevance is treated as binary. The second paradigm is motivated by RAG scenarios where answering a complex query requires combining complementary evidence distributed across multiple pages. The objective is to recover a sufficient set of evidence that supports answer generation. This requirement has motivated the introduction of multi-hop queries and corresponding set-level evaluation protocols. For example, M3DocVQA~ provides query-level evidence-set annotations, allowing evaluation through metrics such as All@$K$, as adopted in , where retrieval succeeds only when the complete annotated evidence set is covered by the top-$K$ results. Notably, this metric was not part of M3DocVQA's original evaluation protocol. Double-Bench~ further accounts for the substitutability of evidence by defining multi-hop retrieval success as retrieving at least one supporting page for every reasoning hop within the top-$K$ results, measured by Hit@$K$. Nevertheless, the presence of multi-hop queries does not necessarily imply set-level retrieval evaluation. Several benchmarks, including KoViDoRe~, UniDoc-Bench~, and ViDoRe V3~, incorporate multi-hop questions but continue to adopt conventional item-wise IR protocols, although their definitions of multi-hop queries differ. The recent emergence of agentic RAG has introduced a third paradigm: iterative retrieval. Unlike conventional RAG pipelines that typically retrieve evidence once before answer generation, agentic systems can interleave planning, retrieval, evidence inspection, and reasoning across multiple steps. Accordingly, evidence acquisition becomes a sequential and history-dependent process, where observations from earlier steps inform subsequent retrieval decisions and determine when sufficient evidence has been collected. Several VDR benchmarks explore this paradigm from different perspectives. VisDocAgentBench~ introduces latent semantic bridges that must be resolved to locate the final target; MADQA~ emphasizes acquiring minimal grounded evidence from heterogeneous collections; M4DocBench~ incorporates conversational dependencies and annotated evidence chains; FinMRAGBench~ focuses on iterative financial analysis; and PaperScope~ provides a tool-based environment for scientific information seeking. Additionally, these benchmarks do not share a unified evaluation protocol. For instance, VisDocAgentBench evaluates final-target retrieval using Recall and MRR, MADQA measures the quality of retrieved citation sets using F1, and PaperScope separately reports direct-retrieval Recall for Topic Induction and downstream reasoning accuracy. Overall, RAG has broadened the objectives and evaluation settings of VDR, from ranking independently relevant items to recovering complementary evidence sets and supporting iterative evidence acquisition. These distinctions highlight the importance of selecting benchmarks that align with the intended retrieval objective, considering not only the document domains and corpus characteristics but also whether relevance is defined at the item or evidence-set level, whether relevant items are interchangeable or complementary, and whether evidence is acquired through single-shot or iterative retrieval.
