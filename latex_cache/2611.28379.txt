Beyond identifying relevant items, these systems must also provide diverse recommendations to broaden content exposure and avoid repetitive user experiences~. This challenge is particularly important in visually oriented domains, where users interact with items through both textual metadata and visual presentations~. For example, movie cover art and product images convey visual characteristics that may not be captured by titles or categorical labels alone. Importantly, : items from different categories may share similar visual appearances, while items within the same category may exhibit distinct visual styles. This distinction motivates exploring visual information as an additional signal for relevance- and diversity-aware recommendation. Diversity-aware reranking has been extensively studied through methods based on predefined categories, item representations, and explicit relevance--diversity trade-offs~. While effective, these approaches typically depend on predefined representations or similarity measures, which may not fully capture the visual and semantic characteristics of recommended items. More recently, large language models have demonstrated promising capabilities in recommendation and listwise reranking~. By incorporating user interaction histories and candidate metadata into prompts, LLMs can refine rankings without task-specific training. However, many existing LLM-based rerankers primarily rely on textual descriptions, leaving the potential contribution of visual content to relevance and diversity insufficiently explored. Multimodal large language models , which jointly process images and text, offer a promising opportunity to address this limitation~. In principle, visual information may help distinguish items with similar textual descriptions but different appearances, while also revealing similarities that are not reflected in category labels. Nevertheless, incorporating additional modalities does not necessarily guarantee better recommendations: visual signals may be redundant when textual metadata is sufficiently informative, and their utility may vary across domains and model backbones. Moreover, it remains unclear whether MLLM-based reranking can improve recommendation relevance and diversity simultaneously. These considerations motivate three research questions: When does visual information improve recommendation relevance? Can MLLM-based reranking simultaneously improve relevance and category-diversity-sensitive ranking quality? How do these effects vary across recommendation domains and MLLM backbones? Beyond incorporating multimodal inputs, explicitly introduces relevance and diversity considerations into the ranking task through natural-language instructions. Unlike conventional methods that combine relevance and similarity scores with an explicit trade-off parameter , our framework requires neither an explicit diversity score nor a manually specified coefficient. Instead, we investigate whether pretrained MLLMs can incorporate multiple ranking considerations directly through structured prompting. Specifically, we design four complementary guidelines $=\{g_1, ,g_4\}$ that encourage the model to examine visual characteristics, interpret user preferences, consider exploratory recommendations, and balance relevance with diversity. The first guideline $g_1$ instructs the MLLM to examine visual characteristics of both historical and candidate items, including color palettes, artistic styles, composition, and other appearance-related attributes, alongside textual metadata. This instruction is motivated by the observation that semantic descriptions and visual appearances capture different aspects of item content. For instance, two movies categorized as action thrillers may exhibit substantially different visual styles, while movies from different genres may share similar poster designs. By explicitly directing attention to visual information, we aim to encourage the model to use item images as complementary evidence rather than relying exclusively on titles or genre-related semantic cues. This guideline also provides a basis for comparing multimodal and text-only prompting, allowing us to examine whether access to visual information improves ranking quality. User preferences may evolve over time, and recent interactions can provide information that differs from long-term consumption patterns. Accordingly, the second guideline $g_2$ asks the MLLM to consider the chronological progression of the user's interaction history when interpreting preferences. As described in Section~, historical images are arranged from oldest to most recent, with corresponding titles following the same ordering. This representation allows the prompt to communicate temporal context without introducing a separate temporal modeling component. For example, a user may have historically consumed action movies but recently shown greater interest in comedy or family-oriented content. The guideline encourages the MLLM to consider such changes when evaluating candidates, rather than treating the interaction history as an unordered collection of preferences. A recommendation list consisting entirely of items similar to a user's past interactions may provide high immediate relevance but limited variety. Conversely, excessive exploration may introduce items that are insufficiently aligned with the user's interests. The third guideline $g_3$ therefore encourages the MLLM to balance familiar content with potentially relevant items that extend beyond previously observed preferences. Rather than treating exploration as random diversification, the instruction emphasizes identifying candidates that may introduce new interests while remaining consistent with the user's broader consumption history. This provides a qualitative mechanism for encouraging diversity without requiring an explicit exploration probability or a predefined similarity threshold. The final guideline $g_4$ explicitly instructs the MLLM to consider both recommendation relevance and genre or category diversity when producing the ranked list. Relevance is assessed in the context of the user's interaction history, while diversity encourages coverage of different content categories among highly ranked candidates. Unlike a post-hoc diversification procedure that first constructs a relevance-based ranking and subsequently adjusts it using a separate diversity score, our framework presents these considerations within a single ranking instruction. The MLLM is therefore asked to generate one candidate ordering that accounts for both objectives, without an explicit mathematical optimization step or a fixed relevance--diversity weighting parameter. Together, these guidelines define a consistent diversity-aware prompting protocol across the evaluated MLLMs and recommendation domains. Their purpose is to investigate whether pretrained models can use multimodal context and natural-language instructions to produce rankings that perform well under both relevance and diversity-sensitive evaluation. Importantly, the instructions do not guarantee that the model optimizes either objective or learns an optimal user-specific relevance--diversity trade-off. We therefore assess the resulting rankings empirically, using relevance metrics and category-based diversity-sensitive metrics, rather than assuming that the intended prompting behavior is achieved. We examine the inference cost of using InternVL3-38B with 4-bit quantization. These measurements highlight the computational overhead of applying large MLLMs to recommendation, even when reranking a relatively small candidate set . Our framework limits MLLM inference to a bounded candidate list rather than applying the model to the entire item catalog. Additionally, concatenating item images into composite visual inputs simplifies multimodal prompt construction by reducing the number of separately supplied images, although the actual visual-token cost depends on the model's image processing strategy. Nevertheless, the observed inference latency remains a substantial challenge for real-time recommendation. More efficient MLLMs, optimized inference, or offline reranking may help improve practicality, but evaluating these alternatives is beyond the scope of this study. In this work, we present , a training-free framework for diversity-aware recommendation reranking using pretrained MLLMs. Experiments across product and video recommendation domains reveal three key findings: ~the benefits of visual information are domain-dependent, with substantial gains in video recommendation but limited improvements on product datasets, ~MLLM-based reranking can simultaneously improve recommendation relevance and category-diversity-sensitive ranking quality without task-specific training and ~the magnitude of visual gains and the consistency of reranking improvements depend on the MLLM backbone, with larger visual gains not necessarily yielding a stronger reranker. Our results demonstrate the potential of pretrained MLLMs to incorporate multimodal information and diversity-aware instructions into existing recommendation pipelines, while highlighting differences across domains and model backbones. Future work includes fine-tuning MLLMs for recommendation-specific objectives, exploring user-controllable diversity preferences, and improving inference efficiency for larger-scale reranking. *{Ethical Considerations} This work aims to reduce repetitive, narrow recommendations, which can mitigate filter-bubble effects; however, diversity measured by category coverage may not reflect what individual users value, and diversity objectives should not override user intent. Pretrained MLLMs may encode social biases from their training data, and relying on visual appearance could amplify stereotypes or favor items with professionally produced imagery, disadvantaging smaller creators or sellers; we recommend auditing exposure across item groups before deployment. All experiments on the proprietary Prime Video data were conducted within internal company infrastructure: InternVL3-38B was run on local GPUs, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 was accessed through an internal enterprise API under which user data is not exposed externally and is not used for model training. We report only relative improvements on this dataset and release no user-level data. Finally, large MLLM inference is energy-intensive; restricting reranking to small candidate sets limits, but does not remove, this cost.
