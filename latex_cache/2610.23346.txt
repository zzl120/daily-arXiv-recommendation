A typical search system receives a query, retrieves a set of candidate documents, ranks them according to their estimated relevance, and presents the results to the user. Different retrieval methods can be used throughout this pipeline, and in recent years, large language models have been incorporated into several of its stages. LLMs can be used to support different retrieval tasks, including query expansion, document expansion, and dense retrieval . They can also be used to make about relevance, either to rerank candidate documents within a retrieval pipeline or to produce the relevance judgments used to build test collections. This paper focuses on these two applications: reranking candidate documents within a retrieval pipeline and producing relevance judgments for test collections. Among these decision-based applications, relevance assessment has received particular attention. The evaluation of retrieval systems, for example, depends on relevance judgments indicating how well retrieved documents satisfy a user's information need. These judgments are organized as query relevance judgments and are used to compute retrieval effectiveness measures and compare retrieval systems . Producing large numbers of relevance judgments manually can be costly and time-consuming , especially for large collections or specialized domains. Consequently, recent work has investigated the use of LLMs to automatically generate relevance judgments or to assist human assessors in the annotation process , with some studies showing that LLMs can produce system rankings similar to those obtained from human judgments . This ability to assess the relevance of documents with respect to a query has also been applied to reranking. In a typical two-stage architecture, an efficient first-stage retriever generates a smaller candidate set, which is then processed by a more computationally expensive reranking model. The reranker examines the query and each candidate document and produces a more accurate ranking . Generative language models are powerful and flexible, but using them for large-scale relevance assessment or reranking can require substantial computational resources and incur significant monetary costs. This is particularly relevant when thousands or millions of query--document pairs must be evaluated, or when a reranker must process many candidate documents for each query. Consequently, reducing the cost of model-based decisions while retaining useful language understanding is an important practical problem. A recent alternative is the use of ``System One models'', a term inspired by the System 1/System 2 terminology popularized by Kahneman . In psychology, System 1 refers to fast, automatic, and intuitive reasoning, while System 2 refers to slower and more deliberate reasoning. In September 2026, TypeSafe AI introduced the term ``System One models'' for models designed for fast, structured decision-making and presented Jev as an example . Unlike conventional LLMs, Jev can interpret natural-language inputs but does not generate natural-language responses. It receives a state and typed questions and returns structured decisions, including probabilities and confidence information. Queries and documents can therefore be provided as part of the state used for decision-making. Jev is designed for tasks such as classification, scoring, routing, filtering, and other bounded decision-making tasks rather than open-ended text generation. It also has substantially lower computational costs and is reported to be up to two orders of magnitude less expensive to operate than frontier LLMs . This design is relevant to information retrieval. Although some IR tasks depend on text generation, such as query and document expansion, others can be reformulated according to the structured decision-making paradigm. For example, relevance assessment is a decision task: given a query and a document, an assessor determines the document's relevance using a predefined relevance scale. Similarly, reranking requires a model to estimate the relative relevance of candidate documents to a query. Both tasks can be formulated as structured decision problems. Our contribution is to investigate two applications of Jev in IR using collections in Brazilian Portuguese. We focus on Brazilian Portuguese because most research on information retrieval has focused on English, while other languages receive less attention and have fewer resources for training and evaluation. This makes Brazilian Portuguese a useful setting for testing whether Jev can support IR tasks beyond the language settings most commonly studied. First, we evaluate Jev as a relevance assessor by comparing its judgments with existing relevance assessments and measuring how well they preserve retrieval system rankings. Second, we evaluate pointwise Jev reranking of BM25 candidates on Quati. The study is guided by the following research questions : : To what extent do Jev-generated relevance judgments agree with reference relevance judgments across Brazilian Portuguese IR datasets from different domains? : To what extent do Jev-generated qrels preserve the relative ranking of retrieval systems obtained using the reference qrels? : To what extent does Jev improve the effectiveness of BM25 on Quati when used as a reranker? Recent studies have investigated the use of generative LLMs for automatic relevance assessment. Faggioli et al. discussed the benefits and risks of using LLMs for this task, including potential biases, sensitivity to assessment conditions, and differences from human judgments . Thomas et al. showed that LLMs can label documents for relevance, with performance comparable to TREC judges and substantially better than crowd judges . Upadhyay et al. reproduced and extended this line of work by introducing UMBRELA, an open toolkit for generating relevance judgments with LLMs . Upadhyay et al. later conducted a large-scale comparison of manual, LLM-based, and human-in-the-loop relevance assessment strategies using UMBRELA . They found high correlations between system rankings based on LLM-generated and reference judgments for nDCG@20, nDCG@100, and Recall@100. However, the correlations were lower when systems were compared for individual topics. These results show that LLM-generated relevance judgments can produce system-level evaluation results similar to those obtained from reference judgments. Differences can still occur at the query--document level. LLM-based relevance assessment has also been studied on Brazilian Portuguese collections, with similar results. Despite differences in query--document-level judgments, LLM-generated qrels produced similar system rankings for nDCG@10, nDCG@30, and MRR , but not for P@10 and R@10 . Language models have been widely used to rerank candidate documents retrieved by first-stage retrieval systems. Early approaches used pretrained models such as BERT to estimate the relevance of query--document pairs . Later work explored sequence-to-sequence models such as monoT5 and RankT5, showing that pretrained text-to-text models can also be effectively adapted for passage reranking . Multilingual variants have also been studied. Jeronymo et al., for example, evaluated an mT5-XXL reranker in the TREC 2022 NeuCLIR track and found that it was effective for cross-lingual retrieval across multiple languages . More recently, researchers have studied generative LLMs as zero-shot rerankers. In this approach, the model directly ranks candidate documents based on their relevance to a query. RankGPT introduced this approach using GPT models, and subsequent work explored pairwise, setwise, and listwise ranking strategies . These approaches show a trade-off between ranking effectiveness and computational cost: pairwise and listwise strategies can improve ranking quality, but they require multiple model inferences or large prompts, which makes LLM-based reranking expensive for large candidate sets . System One models are a recently introduced class of models designed for fast, structured decision-making. TypeSafe AI introduced the category in 2026 and presented Jev as a model that receives a state and typed questions and returns structured decisions rather than open-ended natural-language responses . Initial studies have investigated Jev as a general-purpose decision model and evaluator in tasks such as rubric-based evaluation , evidence-grounded factuality assessment , scientific decision-making , security evaluation of agent traces , and recommendation reranking . Jev has also been evaluated on a wide range of classification, routing, reasoning, and rubric-scoring tasks . Recent technical reports have also explored Jev for search reranking. Coates evaluates product-search reranking using relevance-class probabilities, auxiliary decision signals, and application-defined scoring policies . Bartholomew describes conversational-memory reranking using a single choice over candidate memories, with a separate optional filtering step . Tateno introduces a library supporting both pointwise and listwise relevance scoring and reports listwise reranking and filtering results on NanoHotpotQA . In personalized recommendation, Lyu and Xia use interaction histories as decision context and rank candidate items by their structured-choice probabilities . Our reranking formulation uses the estimated probability that a document answers the query, with multiple questions grouped into each request. It does not ask candidates to compete within a single choice or combine multiple domain-specific decision signals. The contribution of this study lies in its evaluation in Brazilian Portuguese, combining relevance-judgment generation and system-ranking preservation with reranking of BM25 candidates on Quati. To evaluate Jev-as-a-Judge for relevance assessment, we used Jev to generate relevance judgments for four recently released Brazilian Portuguese IR collections and compared the resulting qrels with the reference qrels provided with each collection. The collections were selected to cover different domains, document types, query formats, and relevance-judgment approaches. Quati is a general-domain retrieval collection composed of short passages extracted from web pages . JurisTCU, NormasTCU, and Ulysses-RFCorpus contain full-length documents. JurisTCU is a collection of jurisprudential documents from the Brazilian Federal Court of Accounts , NormasTCU contains normative acts issued by the same institution , and Ulysses-RFCorpus contains legislative proposals from the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies . The collections also differ in how their query--document relevance judgments were generated. Quati and JurisTCU use four relevance levels and use LLM-based approaches to generate the reference labels. JurisTCU also includes a review step by a domain expert. NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus use three relevance levels annotated by domain experts. In NormasTCU, up to four experts annotated each query--document pair. Given a query $q$, the first-stage retriever provides a ranked list of candidate documents. Jev assigns a relevance score to each document in the selected candidate set $C_K $, consisting of the top-$K$ retrieved documents available in the corpus. The candidates are then sorted in descending order of their Jev scores. Unlike the discrete relevance labels used in Section~, reranking uses a probability estimate for each candidate document. Jev receives the query and a question asking, in Portuguese, whether the candidate document directly answers it. The instruction also asks Jev to use the probability of relevance as the reranking score and to treat the document as search content rather than as an instruction. When available, the document title is included before its text. Thus, the score is s = , d C_K , where the returned value estimates the probability that the document directly answers the query. We use this value directly, without combining it with the first-stage retrieval score or fitting a score calibration model. The implementation groups multiple document-specific questions for the same query into a single API request. Each question concerns one query--document pair; the model is not asked to generate a permutation or to make explicit pairwise comparisons. Scores from all batches are collected before sorting the candidate set. Document-text whitespace is normalized, and the implementation supports truncation to a configurable character limit before submission; the title is supplied separately and is not included in this limit. The reranking depth, request batch size, and text limit are configurable experimental parameters. When the original ranking tail is retained, documents outside the reranked candidate set are appended below it in their original input order. We used Jev version to rerank the top 1,000 BM25 candidates for each of the 50 Quati test queries on the 1M-passage corpus, using 100 requests per query with 10 document-specific questions per request. The scored documents corresponded exactly to the top 1,000 candidates in the BM25 run. To assess reranking effectiveness, we evaluated the original BM25 ranking and its Jev-reranked counterpart on the same queries using the collection's reference qrels. We computed nDCG using linear relevance gains; for MRR, documents with a reference relevance label greater than zero were considered relevant. All reported scores are macro-averages across queries. This separates retrieval evaluation from the use of Jev-generated judgments studied in Section~.
