Unlike conventional recommendation methods that primarily rely on discrete item IDs and user--item interactions, language-model-enhanced recommenders can exploit rich textual information to construct more informative representations of items and user behaviors. Recent studies have explored different ways of incorporating such knowledge, including formulating recommendation as a language modeling task~, learning semantic representations from item content~, developing semantic item representations from different domains for LLM-based recommendation~, integrating collaborative signals with LLM representations~, and reasoning over historical evidence for preference understanding~. These advances provide a stronger semantic foundation for personalized recommendation; however, richer item representations alone do not necessarily lead to a better understanding of how user interests evolve over time. Accurately understanding users remains challenging because user preferences are inherently dynamic and may exhibit different temporal characteristics. Some preferences can persist over long periods, whereas others emerge from recent behaviors and may rapidly change. Sequential recommendation methods have been developed to capture behavioral dependencies over interaction histories~, while several studies further distinguish long- and short-term user interests~. Although these approaches have demonstrated the importance of modeling multiple temporal signals, they mainly focus on how historical behaviors are encoded or combined into user representations. A less explored question is how each newly observed interaction should the user model itself. Treating all new behaviors with the same update mechanism may respond too slowly to emerging interests, or conversely, allow a small number of recent interactions to excessively affect useful preferences that have been repeatedly observed over a longer period. {figures/motivation.png} Users exhibit both persistent preferences and newly emerging interests over time. Existing strategies may overemphasize either long-term history or recent behavior, motivating a balanced user model that preserves what lasts while adapting to what changes.} Interestingly, recent advances in LLM inference provide a useful perspective for addressing this issue. Recent studies have shown that LLM performance can be further improved after offline training by allocating additional computation during inference, for example by searching over candidate reasoning paths, revising intermediate predictions, or adaptively refining the model state~. The key insight is that inference need not remain a single passive forward process: newly available information can be used to refine the internal state on which subsequent predictions are made. In-Place Test-Time Training further demonstrates that such adaptation can be implemented by updating a subset of the model's existing weights using the observed context~. This principle naturally aligns with sequential recommendation: as new user interactions become observable, they provide additional evidence for refining the current understanding of the user, which can in turn improve subsequent recommendations. This motivates us to rethink user modeling as a continuous adaptive process rather than solely as a fixed encoding of the observed interaction history. Experiments on three real-world datasets show that } improves mean NDCG@10 over the strongest external baseline by 7.0--13.2\ Recent advances in language models have enabled recommender systems to exploit rich semantic information beyond conventional item IDs. Early language-based recommendation frameworks, such as P5, formulate recommendation tasks in a unified text-to-text paradigm~. Other studies leverage textual item information to learn transferable or semantically enriched representations for sequential recommendation, including UniSRec~ and Recformer~. More recent approaches further integrate collaborative signals with language models~, employ LLMs for candidate ranking~, or represent items using semantic identifiers for generative retrieval and scalable item tokenization~. LLMs have also been used to reason over historical evidence for more explicit and verifiable preference modeling~. These studies demonstrate that language models provide rich semantic representations for modeling items and user--item interactions. In this work, we use frozen language-model embeddings to represent item semantics and instead focus on how such semantic evidence can support a more adaptive understanding of users. Specifically, we study how persistent preferences and recently emerging interests can be accumulated and selectively utilized as the user's interaction history evolves. Sequential recommendation has extensively studied how user preferences evolve over interaction histories. Representative models employ recurrent networks~, self-attention~, bidirectional Transformers~, and explicit temporal intervals~ to capture sequential dependencies. Beyond modeling a single evolving representation, several studies explicitly distinguish preferences at different temporal scales. SLi-Rec models long- and short-term user preferences and adaptively aggregates them for recommendation~. Particularly related to our work, CLSR disentangles long- and short-term interests using separate encoders and constructs history-derived proxies to supervise the two representations~. This demonstrates that temporal interests benefit from explicit modeling rather than being implicitly compressed into a single user state. However, CLSR primarily separates and aggregates . Our work investigates a different question: whether temporal evidence should also determine . Instead of assigning interactions exclusively to one temporal representation, an observed interaction may update both persistent and recent memories with different strengths according to cross-period recurrence and recent behavioral consistency. Recent inference-time learning methods provide a complementary perspective in which observed context is not only encoded as input, but also used to update an internal model state. Test-Time Training introduces self-supervised adaptation during inference~, while TTT layers formulate the hidden state itself as a learnable model that is continuously updated as new tokens are observed~. In-Place TTT further implements this idea within standard Transformer MLP blocks by treating existing projection matrices as fast weights and updating them with prediction-aligned local learning objectives~. Earlier fast-weight models similarly demonstrate that matrix-valued associative memories can store and retrieve recent contextual information~. The idea of adaptive inference has also been explored in recommendation. Related efforts have investigated advanced generative modeling and post-training optimization, including diffusion-based collaborative filtering~ and reinforcement-learning-based fine-tuning of diffusion recommenders~. More directly, TTT4Rec introduces TTT-style state updates into sequential recommendation, allowing newly observed interactions to modify the internal sequence state during inference~. Related work further studies adaptation under distribution shift~, temporal alignment for changing user interests~, and retrieval-based adaptation without parameter updates~. Existing temporal recommenders model user interests at different time scales, while adaptive inference methods update user states from newly observed interactions. However, how temporal evidence should guide such adaptation remains underexplored. We therefore maintain two user-specific fast-weight memories for persistent and recent interests, using cross-period recurrence and recent-window consistency to control memory updates, while the current context determines their contributions to the next recommendation. We proposed }, a memory adaptation framework that uses temporal evidence to update two user-specific memories with different adaptation dynamics. Long-term evidence captures recurring behavior across periods, while short-term evidence reflects recent consistency. Context-dependent reading determines their contributions to the current recommendation. The shared model is trained offline, and user memories are updated only after real interactions become observable. Experiments on MovieLens-10M, Amazon Luxury Beauty, and KuaiRec show that achieves the best mean performance across the reported recommendation metrics. Equal-capacity ablations support the roles of temporal evidence, temporal supervision, and dynamic reading. Real-sequence analyses further show improved prediction of both continued recent interests and actual returns to earlier recurring themes. However, Frozen Online Memory matches 's reported Top-$K$ metrics, so these experiments do not establish an additional benefit from continued test-time writing. Efficiency measurements show that adaptation introduces additional computation and user-state storage. Together, the results support temporal evidence as a useful control signal for memory adaptation, while leaving its incremental online benefit open to further evaluation. MATE offers a framework for personalized recommendation that combines persistent information with adaptation to recent behavior. This design may also inform other interactive systems with evolving user states. Its deployment nevertheless requires careful handling of behavioral histories and persistent memories. Privacy protection, memory management, and safeguards against reinforcing narrow or outdated interests remain important considerations. Predictive improvements should not be interpreted as direct understanding of users' motivations, and practical evaluation should consider user benefit alongside recommendation accuracy and computational cost.
