Scholar recommendation aims to identify relevant scholars for a given user based on academic interests, social interactions, and attribute signals. Current research on scholar recommendation includes collaborative filtering methods and deep learning methods. For example, NNRW~ integrates a nearest neighbor filtering with a PageRank to mitigate the data sparsity issue. HeteroRWR~ integrates heterogeneous random walks and multi-relational weight adaptation for interdisciplinary collaborator recommendations. Deep learning methods enhance the modeling of linear and nonlinear interactions through GNNs. For example, CNEacR~ constructs a textual similarity measurement module based multi-head attention and a dynamic fusion strategy. HNERec~ designs a hierarchical graph attention and a semantic fusion coefficients for improving long-tail scholar matching. To enhance robustness in sparse or noisy scenarios, recent advancements have incorporated contrastive learning and self-supervised learning paradigms into heterogeneous graphs. For example, SGL~ constructs multi-view graph structural augmentation strategies and a contrastive learning mechanism. HGCL~ encodes the heterogeneous user-item interaction and integrates a meta network with contrastive learning to advance the recommendation. To further capture rich semantic relations, HSGL~ introduces a heterogeneous self-supervised graph learning framework that maps relation-specific embeddings into a unified space via cross-relation contrastive learning. However, these methods often neglect explainability and fine-grained semantic paths essential for transparent and user-trustworthy recommendations. GNN-based models have shown strong capabilities in capturing complex relations in ASNs. Nevertheless, their effectiveness is susceptible to the integrity of the input graph topology, which is often noisy, sparse, or incomplete in real-world ASNs~. To address this, Graph Structure Learning has been proposed to jointly optimize graph topology and node representations. SLHRec~ jointly models heterogeneity and logical dependencies by collaborating on a structure-aware dual-GNN with a logic-aware Markov network through a contrastive objective. GCEN~ formulates a self-supervised contrastive framework to refine graph connectivity via integrating multi-branch generative modules with feature enhancement. DSL-MRS~ enhances recommendation through a structured diffusion process that counteracts sparsification-induced information loss by preserving global structural integrity. Furthermore, to address the temporal nature of interactions, DGEL~ explicitly models how node neighborhoods and structural contexts evolve over time to capture changing user preferences. Similarly to these works, our PAGSL framework adopts the structural refinement to combat sparsity and improve recommendation quality. However, our attribute-sharing augmentation strategy provides richer signals for the path encoding module and creates explicit semantic bridges that form the basis for interpretable path-based explanations. This effectively resolve the reachability problem for cold-start scholars. Furthermore, rather than flattening interactions into sequential linear paths, our path-tree-based architecture strictly preserves the branching propagation of academic information. This overcomes the expressive limitations of linear paths while simultaneously providing a transparent, tree-structured topological foundation that directly facilitates our end-to-end explainable recommendation. Explainable recommendation seeks to provide human-understandable rationales for prediction outcomes. Subgraph-based methods focus on identifying local substructures important to prediction. For example, GNNExplainer~ learns parameter-driven masks to extract edge-induced subgraphs and critical features. Its objective is to maximize the mutual information between the masked structure and the original predictive output. PGEXplainer~ uses the same mutual information, but employs a neural mask generator to facilitate the production of explanatory structures. However, they often fail to capture long-range semantic dependencies. Path-based methods provide more intuitive explanations by tracing relational chains between users and items. For example, ERKM~ generates the path between entities as an explanation basis, and optimizes the explanation list based on multi-objective algorithm. CaGE~ integrates structural causal models to mitigate confounding bias and generates meta-path explanations that highlight true causal pathways. However, they suffer from redundancy and often rely on domain-specific meta-paths~, limiting generalizability. Attention-based methods learn importance weights over multiple paths based on attention mechanism. For example, TGAMER~ utilizes proximity-aware selection to retrieve meta-path samples that contribute significantly to the model, and processes path information based on an attention mechanism. TMER-RL~ generates useful instance paths based on reinforcement learning, and learns the weights of instances that contain reasoning information with multi-head attention. DCCMA-Net~ separates features into content and style components to align semantic correlations weight between different entities. However, these methods often suffer from the hub-node bias inherent in scale-free academic networks, frequently yielding generic, uninformative explanations because they fail to disentangle topology from popularity. Like subgraph-based methods, we employ learnable edge masks to identify task-relevant structures in a differentiable manner. Like path-based and attention-based methods, our final explanations take the form of concrete relational paths between scholars and we also evaluate path importance. However, our differentiation lies in actively intervening in the graph topology. By integrating a $k$-shell decomposition to eliminate topological redundancy, and employing a centrality penalty within the mask learning objective, our framework explicitly suppresses the interference of generic hub nodes. Consequently, PAGSL does not require manual path filtering or domain-specific meta-paths; it automatically navigates the informative middle shells of the network to output concise, specific, and semantically meaningful reasoning paths. While this paper focuses on scholar recommendation in ASNs, the proposed framework possesses a degree of generality that enables adaptation to other domains. This generalizability stems from two architectural properties. First, the explanation module operates post-hoc on the trained model without modifying its structure or parameters, making it an instance-level explainer that is decoupled from the specific recommendation task. Second, explanation paths are extracted from the augmented graph's typed edges rather than from domain-specific relational schema, avoiding the need for expert-designed meta-paths in each new domain. To apply the framework in a new domain , the primary adaptation involves defining domain-specific node attributes and attribute-sharing rules for graph augmentation. The explanation module requires no structural modification across domains, as it operates on the general abstractions of typed edges and node centrality. The hyperparameter controlling the centrality penalty may require domain-specific tuning to adapt to the unique degree distributions of the target network. This adaptability and instance-level flexibility make PAGSL a highly versatile framework for providing transparent and faithful explanations across diverse complex network applications. This paper introduces a path-augmented graph structure learning framework for explainable scholar recommendation in academic social networks. PAGSL addresses two core challenges: achieving accurate scholar matching and providing path-based explanations. It consists of four main steps. First, textual attribute information of scholars is processed to construct attribute-based relationship paths, augmenting the original user-following relationship network. Then, a graph relation learning model based a path neural network is developed to learn the embedding representation of each scholar, and the appropriate prediction head is selected for scholar recommendation based on the embedding results. During the explanation of recommendation results, we utilize $k$-shell decomposition to identify multiple paths of nodes, and subsequently apply mask learning to heterogeneous paths among scholars to determine critical explanatory paths. By processing heterogeneity and explaining edges, the method identifies significant paths linking source nodes to target nodes. Future work will involve optimizing path selection algorithms~ to reduce path quantity and complexity. In addition, multi-objective optimization techniques will be explored to determine the optimal balance between explainability and predictive accuracy, thereby maximizing interpretability without sacrificing predictive performance.
