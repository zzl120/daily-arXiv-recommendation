 This work aims to raise awareness of the overlooked risk of harmful content propagation in recommender systems and to provide resources and methods for developing harmfulness-aware recommendation technologies. All datasets used in this study are publicly available and already contain harmful content. Our contribution lies solely in the annotation of such content from a harmfulness perspective; we do not create, modify, or amplify any harmful material. All data and annotations are intended strictly for scientific research purposes and are prohibited from any commercial use. We fully comply with the data usage policies and terms of service of the original platforms from which the datasets were derived. The content, views, or statements included in the annotated datasets do not reflect the perspectives of the authors or affiliated institutions. To protect annotators involved in the harmfulness labeling process, particularly when reviewing harmful or distressing content, we implement the following ethical safeguards: obtain informed consent about the nature of the task, including potential exposure to disturbing material; enforce reasonable annotation workloads with weekly limits to manage exposure levels; recommend pausing or discontinuing annotation if discomfort arises; and conduct regular mental wellness check-ins with annotators. These measures are intended to minimize psychological risks and ensure a responsible annotation process. Our definition of harmful content is developed by synthesizing existing studies, platform policies, and preliminary analysis. However, harmfulness is inherently context-dependent, and perceptions of what constitutes harmful content may vary across cultures, regions, and time, potentially introducing subjectivity in certain borderline cases. Moreover, harmful content is constantly evolving alongside emerging social trends and new forms of expression, such as novel misinformation topics and evolving slang for offensive language. Therefore, periodic refinement and updates to the definition will be necessary to maintain its relevance and ensure that future benchmarks can adapt to the evolving landscape of harmful content. {./img/cpropmt.pdf} This work extends three public datasets to examine RS in terms of content harmfulness, including , and . Current methods in recommender systems are dedicated to improving prediction accuracy via capturing users' interest, involving the utilization of various techniques, such as recurrent neural networks~, attention mechanism~, graph neural networks~, reinforcement learning~, and self-supervised learning~. Additionally, recent efforts attempt to incorporate LLMs into the task, using LLMs to enhance the understanding for item content~. However, all of these methods focus on catering to user interest, usually overlooking the harmfulness of the recommended content. In addition to accuracy-oriented works, some efforts have attempted to enhance the quality of content recommendation. Here, the ``quality'' covers a wide range of aspects within recommendations, such as eliminating fake news~, improving recommendation fairness~ or interpretability~, addressing the biased popularity of items~, mitigating unwanted recommendations~, avoiding radicalization~, as well as increasing business revenue~ or recommendation diversity~. Moreover, some methods aim to recommend high-quality items to users. Typically, they rely on explicit or implicit user feedback to assess an item's quality, such as scroll information~, ratings~, and dwell time~. More recently, highlights the existence of harmful content in the movie domain. However, their analysis assumes that recommender systems never recommend harmful content, an assumption that is inconsistent with real-world observations and the empirical findings of our study. In this work, we take an initial step toward investigating recommender systems from the harmfulness perspective, an important yet under-explored dimension. We first construct harmfulness-annotated datasets through a LLM-human collaborative annotation paradigm and introduce two harmfulness-related metrics, HP@$k$ and HO@$k$, to quantitatively evaluate recommendation harmfulness. Through empirical analysis on these resources, we reveal three alarming findings: harmful content is widespread in recommendation data, users tend to engage with such content, and current methods often facilitate its propagation. To address the identified issue, we propose , a generic and model-agnostic framework that can be seamlessly integrated into existing neural models to mitigate harmful content propagation. We sincerely hope that our benchmark, findings, and framework can inspire future research toward building more responsible, safer recommendation techniques.
