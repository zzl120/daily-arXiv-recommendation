Across three experimental settings --- BM25-then-reranking task, corpus-wide dense retrieval task, and dense retrieval then re-ranking task --- the results demonstrate that competitive ranking performance can be achieved without complex architectural modifications, custom loss functions, or cross-encoder distillation. Instead, substantial gains stem from data-centric strategies: corpus-targeted continued pre-training, rigorous data curation, hard-negative mining, training--inference distribution alignment, and weight merging via Model Soup. In particular, Model Soup delivers marked performance gains while preserving the inference efficiency of a single model. These findings highlight how data curation and weight merging can push sub-billion-parameter models to state-of-the-art performance. MS MARCO passage ranking is an information retrieval task built on top of the MS MARCO dataset . This dataset contains 1 million real-world Bing search queries and 8.8 million candidate passages. For each query, human annotators have labeled zero or more relevant passages. Prior work has primarily focused on two benchmark tasks: passage re-ranking and passage full ranking. : For each query, systems are provided with the top 1,000 candidate passages retrieved by BM25. The goal is to re-rank these candidates based on their relevance to the query---specifically, the likelihood of a passage answering the question. : While re-ranking performance is inherently bounded by the recall of the top 1,000 BM25 results, the full ranking task requires retrieving and ranking relevant passages directly from the entire corpus of 8.8 million documents. In practice, this setup typically requires developing both a first-stage retrieval model and a second-stage re-ranking model. For MS MARCO retrieval and re-ranking, BERT-family models have long served as the dominant paradigm. Most existing studies build upon BERT , RoBERTa , ELECTRA , or ERNIE , with some---such as SimLM and CoT-MAE ---further exploring specialized pre-training objectives. More recently, with the rapid advancement of Large Language Models , recent studies have begun examining the utility of state-of-the-art generative language models such as T5 and LLaMA for superior ranking performance . In this report, I follow this line of work by leveraging LLMs to improve ranking quality. Specifically, in collaboration with Claude Code , I explore effective strategies for utilizing the Qwen3-0.6B model in both passage retrieval and re-ranking. I find that even without special pre-training, distillation from larger teacher models, loss function modifications, or architectural changes, rankers and retrievers built on Qwen3-0.6B can achieve state-of-the-art performance solely through deliberate training data curation and model merging. In particular, the Model Soup algorithm ---which averages weights across multiple fine-tuned model checkpoints---proves to be an exceptionally effective strategy for boosting model capability. During this research, I also noticed that performance metrics reported across existing studies are not directly comparable: while most prior works omit passage titles, a subset incorporates them. In my experiments, appending passage titles consistently yields an improvement of 2--3 percentage points in MRR@10 for retrieval and 1--2 pp for re-ranking. This discrepancy aligns with observations previously noted by . Consequently, to ensure fair and rigorous comparisons, all baseline metrics from prior literature in this report are systematically reproduced using their open-source checkpoints. Another noteworthy aspect of this work is that throughout the entire project, I did not write a single line of code myself. In fact, given the sheer volume of code generated, comprehensively inspecting its correctness by hand was practically impossible. Claude Code provided actionable guidance during execution and demonstrated strong capabilities in diagnosing model performance issues. For instance, upon identifying the discrepancy between published results and my reproduced results, Claude Code deduced the inconsistent usage of title content and autonomously designed an experiment suite to validate the hypothesis. Nevertheless, the high-level intuition and direction behind each experiment still relied on my input, as Claude Code tended to stick to more generic solutions. In this technical report, I study three distinct experimental setups: the BM25-then-Reranking pipeline, which re-ranks the top 1,000 candidate passages retrieved by BM25; the Dense Retrieval task, which retrieves the most relevant passages directly from the entire corpus; and the DenseRetrieval-then-Reranking pipeline, which re-ranks the top 1,000 passages retrieved by an initial dense retriever. All of my experiments are conducted by fine-tuning two Qwen3-0.6B based models : Qwen3-0.6B-Embedding for retrieval and Qwen3-0.6B-Reranker for re-ranking. According to their technical report , both models are developed via multi-stage training pipelines built on top of the Qwen3 foundation model, implementing dual-encoder and cross-encoder architectures, respectively. More specifically, both models are initialized directly from the pre-trained Qwen3-0.6B language model, thereby inheriting strong multilingual, long-context capabilities. The Embedding model adopts a dual-encoder architecture optimized through contrastive pre-training, supervised fine-tuning on high-quality text pairs, and model merging to maximize generalization. It extracts semantic representations from the final end-of-sequence token and supports Matryoshka Representation Learning for flexible vector dimensions. The Reranker model employs a cross-encoder that jointly processes query-document pairs via deep cross-attention layers. It is trained with supervised fine-tuning on labeled relevance pairs to predict query-passage relevance. Due to GPU resource constraints, I restrict my focus in this report to sub-1B models, leaving larger-sized models such as Qwen3-Embedding-4B/8B and Qwen3-Reranker-4B/8B for future exploration. Nevertheless, I expect that ranking performance could be scaled further with larger parameter sizes, as observed in the original Qwen3 Embedding report . I evaluate on the MS MARCO passage ranking benchmark , which contains 501,233 queries in the training set and 6,800 queries in the development set, evaluated over a corpus of more than 8.8 million passages. In this dataset, each query is paired with one or more human-labeled relevant passages. The official benchmark provides the top 1,000 candidate passages per query retrieved by an un-tuned BM25 system . For each of the three tasks mentioned above, I construct dedicated training datasets as follows: The BM25-then-Reranking task: The dataset is constructed in two steps. First, I sample $N$ queries from the training partition. Next, for each sampled query, I take the top 1,000 passages retrieved by the official BM25 model, keep all labeled relevant passages as positive samples, and randomly sample $M$ irrelevant passages as negatives. Here, $N$ and $M$ are the primary parameters controlling the training scale. Then, I sample $N$ training queries, retaining all relevant passages as positives and sampling $M$ negative passages from the remaining non-relevant candidates. The resulting data schema is exactly the same as that used in the BM25-then-Reranking pipeline. In this report, all of my experiments are trained and evaluated on an $8 16$GB NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU cluster. Due to hardware resource constraints: for the re-ranking task, I set the maximum sequence length to 384 and the effective batch size to 256; for the retrieval task, I cap the maximum query and document lengths at 64 and 144 tokens, respectively, with effective batch sizes varying across different retrieval models. Following standard language model fine-tuning practices, I adopt the AdamW optimizer with a default learning rate of $1{-5}$. Both the retrieval and re-ranking tasks are evaluated using three metrics: Recall@K , MRR@10, and nDCG@10. Formal definitions of these metrics can be found in . In general, Recall primarily assesses corpus-wide retrieval coverage, whereas MRR and nDCG quantify top-rank precision for the re-ranking tasks. Another important finding concerns benchmark evaluation comparability. Existing MS MARCO results are often not directly comparable because certain prior works incorporate passage titles while others omit them. In my experiments, prepending passage titles consistently improves retrieval MRR@10 by approximately 2--3 percentage points and re-ranking MRR@10 by roughly 1--2 percentage points. Therefore, to ensure a fair comparison, I reproduced all major baselines using their publicly available checkpoints under a unified evaluation protocol.
