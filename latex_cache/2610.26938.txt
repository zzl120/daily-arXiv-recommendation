 Retrieval-Augmented Generation is increasingly served across geo-distributed cloud and edge infrastructures, where wide-area latency, cross-region egress cost, and data-residency rules compete with retrieval quality. Conventional pipelines rank by semantic similarity alone and ignore content lives, inflating tail latency and cost. We formalize the , a drop-in re-scoring layer that decays relevance by latency and adds an optional cost penalty under a cardinality budget and per-region caps, with no retraining. We first settle its complexity: the base formulation is a linear objective over a partition matroid, solved by an $O $ greedy algorithm, rather than NP-hard as a knapsack framing suggests, with hardness entering only under an additive egress budget. On a five-node CloudLab testbed emulating a four-region WAN over the Natural Questions corpus , GRaSP's benefit is : under uniform placement it cuts fetch latency at negligible quality cost; under topical placement it traces a recall--latency Pareto frontier that dominates relevance-only, nearest-$k$, and a latency-budgeted baseline. At a moderate latency setting, GRaSP lowers P90 fetch latency by $41\ For the base GRaSP problem, Proposition~1 gives an exact $O $ solution: sort candidates by net utility and greedily select them while respecting the global and regional caps. We use this as the default policy. We consider two extensions: a Lagrangian variant for harder budgeted or compliance-constrained formulations, and a simpler latency-decay function for high-throughput serving. When additional constraints break the matroid structure, we use regional multipliers $ _r 0$ and rank candidates by _i \;=\; _i\,e^{- _q\,_i } \;-\; \,_i \;-\; _{r }. Candidates are ranked by $ _i$ and selected under the global budget $k$. The multipliers are then updated using a subgradient step: regions that exceed their desired capacity receive a larger penalty, while penalties are reduced for under-used regions. For the base problem, this machinery is unnecessary because the direct greedy algorithm is already optimal. We use the Lagrangian form only for extensions with hard budgets or more complex compliance constraints, where it provides a lightweight and parallelizable approximation. For very high-throughput settings, we approximate the exponential latency decay with e^{- _q\,_i } \; \; {1+ _q\,_i }, which gives the score _i \;=\; _i}{1+ _q\,_i } \;-\; \,_i . We then apply the same cap-aware selection procedure using $_i$. The rational form is cheaper to evaluate and slightly under-penalizes large latencies compared with the exponential decay. As a result, it tends to preserve slightly more recall at the cost of a somewhat heavier latency tail. Both variants require $O $ time per query when implemented with sorting, or $O $ with selection-based implementations. For candidate pools of a few thousand documents, this overhead is small relative to retrieval and LLM inference, as shown in Section~. GRaSP may initially appear to be a nonlinear 0--1 optimization problem because relevance is decayed by latency and combined with retrieval cost. In the base formulation, however, each candidate's utility is fixed before selection, reducing the problem to a linear objective with structured constraints. As a result, it is solvable exactly in polynomial time; NP-hardness appears only under additional constraints such as a hard egress budget. For each candidate, define its u_i \;=\; _i\, e^{- _q\,_i } \;-\; \,_i . For a given query, all terms in $u_i$ are known before selection. Thus, Eq.~ becomes linear in $x$: $ _x _i u_i x_i$. The exponential term only affects the fixed coefficient $u_i$; it does not make the optimization nonlinear. The regional caps in Eq.~ define a partition matroid because each document belongs to exactly one region. The global top-$k$ constraint in Eq.~ is a truncation of that matroid. Therefore, a linear objective can be optimized exactly by greedy selection. $ and the global limit $k$. Stop once $k$ documents are selected or no positive-utility candidate remains. Because Eq.~ is an inequality, an optimal solution never includes a candidate with $u_i<0$. GRaSP may therefore select fewer than $k$ documents, or none, when latency and cost make all remaining candidates unattractive. The base GRaSP problem is therefore not NP-hard. It admits a simple exact algorithm suitable for the retrieval path, which we validate against an integer-program oracle in Section~. Hardness appears when the model includes an additive resource constraint, such as a hard egress budget _{i=1}^N x_i\,_i \; \; B . This constraint couples candidate choices through their costs. Maximizing $ _i u_i x_i$ under Eq.~ contains 0--1 knapsack as a special case and is therefore NP-hard. More complex compliance rules that introduce dependencies across regions or documents can likewise break the matroid structure. Approximation or Lagrangian methods are therefore needed only for these harder variants, not for the base GRaSP formulation. In our evaluation, an exact integer-program solver serves as an offline oracle for measuring their optimality gap. We presented GRaSP, a latency- and cost-aware re-scoring layer for geo-distributed RAG. GRaSP adds location awareness to retrieval without retraining or re-indexing, and its base formulation remains simple: the selection problem is solvable exactly with an $O $ greedy algorithm, while NP-hardness arises only in extensions with hard additive budgets. Our evaluation on a five-node CloudLab testbed and 2.68M passages shows that the benefit of geo-aware retrieval depends strongly on data placement. When relevant content is uniformly distributed or replicated, GRaSP reduces latency and cost with little loss in retrieval quality. When relevant content is concentrated in remote regions, latency and relevance are in genuine tension, and GRaSP exposes a tunable trade-off between them. Replication provides a practical way to reduce this tension by bringing useful alternatives closer to the serving region. GRaSP adds sub-millisecond selection overhead, matches the exact solution for the base problem, and remains stable up to 200~queries/s. More broadly, our results show that retrieval quality alone is not sufficient for geo-distributed RAG: where relevant content resides can be just as important as how relevant it is. The optimization itself is deliberately simple; our contribution is to identify and empirically characterize the conditions under which location-aware context selection helps rather than designing a sophisticated solver. GRaSP is designed to fit into an existing RAG pipeline with minimal changes. It requires only a latency estimate for each candidate's hosting region---from a static RTT table, recent measurements, or a learned predictor---and, optionally, a regional cost map. After an upstream retriever or re-ranker produces a candidate pool, GRaSP re-scores them and enforces the configured global and regional limits before payloads are fetched. It therefore requires no retraining, embedding changes, or re-indexing. Our results show that the value of geo-aware selection depends primarily on where useful alternatives are located. When relevant content is uniformly distributed or replicated across regions, GRaSP can reduce latency and egress cost with little loss in retrieval quality. When relevant content is concentrated in a remote region, as may happen under residency or ownership constraints, locality and relevance are in genuine tension. In this regime, GRaSP does not eliminate the trade-off; instead, it exposes a controllable Pareto frontier through $ _q$ and $ $. This also suggests that placement and query-time selection should be considered together. Replicating frequently accessed content into even a small number of additional regions increases the chance that GRaSP can choose a nearby high-quality passage. Our replication experiments show that this can move the system back toward the regime in which latency and cost reductions are obtained with little quality loss. First, the current formulation assumes a point estimate of network latency. Real wide-area networks exhibit burstiness, asymmetry, congestion, and time-dependent behavior. These effects could be incorporated using percentile latency estimates, moving averages, or time-of-day profiles, but we do not evaluate those variants here. Second, our cost model is additive at the document level. Real cloud pricing may include batching effects, volume discounts, minimum charges, or tiered pricing. Some of these can be incorporated into the objective or added as linear constraints; a tight aggregate budget leads to the budgeted variant discussed in Section~, where exact greedy selection no longer applies. Third, our systems evaluation uses an emulated WAN on a single CloudLab cluster rather than a live multi-region deployment. We also use one retrieval setup and one primary large-scale corpus, supplemented by three smaller BEIR datasets. These experiments are sufficient to expose the placement-dependent behavior we study, but they do not capture all production effects, such as routing variation, regional failures, provider-specific networking, or interactions with different retrievers and corpora. Fourth, the replication experiments model an optimistic setting in which the lowest-latency replica is available and can be selected without additional coordination. We do not account for replica consistency, update propagation, storage overhead, or the cost of maintaining those copies. The measured replication gains should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound on what a production system could achieve. Several extensions follow naturally from this work. The parameters $ _q$, $ $, and even $k$ could be chosen dynamically from workload characteristics or learned from user feedback. Placement and replication could also be optimized jointly with query-time selection, allowing the system to decide both where content should live and which copy should serve each request. Finally, the same formulation could be extended beyond independent passage retrieval to account for multi-hop or graph-based reasoning, where the location and cost of one retrieval may influence which document should be fetched next. Our evaluation answers four questions. Does latency-aware selection reduce tail latency and cost, and what retrieval-quality trade-off does it introduce? How does this trade-off depend on data placement? Can replication recover quality lost to latency awareness? Large language models excel at generating text, but they can hallucinate, and their knowledge can become outdated. Retrieval-Augmented Generation helps by retrieving relevant passages at inference time and giving them to the model as additional context~. RAG is now widely used in enterprise assistants, developer copilots, and analytics systems. In practice, the data used by these applications is often spread across multiple cloud regions and edge locations. Organizations do this to reduce client latency, control costs, improve availability, and meet data-residency requirements. This creates a new challenge for RAG: the most relevant passage is not always the best one to fetch. A highly relevant passage may be stored in a distant region, making it slower and more expensive to retrieve. Regional policies may also limit how much content can be fetched from a particular location. As a result, relevance-only top-$k$ selection can increase tail latency, raise egress cost, and behave poorly under regional constraints. Consider a SaaS assistant whose knowledge base is distributed across , , and . A user in Paris is served from , but several of the most relevant passages for a query are stored in , roughly 115\,ms away. A relevance-only policy will select passages without considering their storage locations, increasing both fetch latency and cross-region egress costs. If similar passages are available in Europe, choosing them instead can keep most of the retrieval quality while reducing latency. But if the useful content exists mainly in , then avoiding the remote fetch will also reduce relevance. In other words, the benefit of location-aware retrieval depends on . We formalize this problem as the . GRaSP runs after retrieval or re-ranking and before the selected passages are fetched. It re-scores candidates using semantic relevance, network latency, and optional retrieval cost, while also enforcing global and per-region selection limits. Because GRaSP operates only on the candidate pool produced by the existing retriever, it can be added to dense or hybrid RAG pipelines without retraining or re-indexing. Although the objective includes latency decay, cost, and regional constraints, the base optimization problem is simple. For a given query, each candidate has a fixed utility. This reduces the problem to a linear selection problem that can be solved exactly using an $O $ greedy algorithm. The problem becomes NP-hard only when we add constraints such as a hard egress budget. Our evaluation shows that GRaSP's benefit depends strongly on data placement. When relevant content is spread across regions or well replicated, GRaSP can reduce latency and cost with little loss in retrieval quality. RAG research has emphasized retrieval quality and fusion strategies~, along with graph-structured retrieval~ and, more recently, distributed RAG architectures~. Adaptive and cascaded retrieval methods decide to retrieve or to stop~, but they optimize over a centralized index and do not treat network location as a selection signal. GRaSP is orthogonal and composable: it re-scores whatever candidate pool an upstream retriever or re-ranker produces. A large systems literature optimizes data and task placement across wide-area networks for latency and cost. Iridium~ jointly optimizes data and task placement to reduce wide-area query latency; Uluyol et al.~ quantify latency versus cost trade-offs in geo-distributed storage, and transaction chains~ achieve low-latency serializability. SPANStore~ minimizes the cost of geo-replicated storage placement across multiple clouds. These works decide fixed objects; GRaSP instead decides objects to fetch at query time given a fixed placement and uncertain relevance, and is complementary to placement optimizers. Content caching in CDNs and edge networks decides content to keep close to users to reduce latency; recent work makes this decision , accounting for the external bandwidth and retrieval cost of cache misses rather than hit-ratio alone~. In the LLM setting, semantic caches reuse prior responses or retrieved contexts for similar queries to cut inference cost and latency~, and in multi-tenant RAG, frequency-aware global caches outperform per-tenant partitioning under skewed workloads~. These mechanisms operate a query, provisioning content by predicted popularity; GRaSP instead operates query time, selecting among already-placed candidates under per-query relevance, latency, and cost. The two are complementary---our replication result shows that caching hot content into nearby regions widens the regime in which GRaSP's selection is near-free. Systems such as DONAR~ and C3~ choose of a known object to contact to minimize latency under load. The key distinction is that replica selection assumes the target object is fixed and replicated, whereas GRaSP must jointly decide documents to include---trading semantic relevance against location---before any replica choice is made. When documents are replicated, GRaSP naturally subsumes best-replica selection as the inner fetch decision . Algorithmically, GRaSP's cap-constrained selection is a linear objective over a partition matroid; its budgeted variant relates to the 0--1 knapsack~. We make this structure explicit in Section~, which clarifies when exact selection is cheap and when approximation is warranted.
