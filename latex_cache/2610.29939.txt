We present ERCLIP, a small-data adaptation of CLIP for long-caption image--text retrieval. During training, a long caption is split into sentences that the CLIP text encoder encodes independently; the sentence embeddings are fused by mean pooling or a gated residual Transformer scorer and receive their own contrastive loss. At inference ERCLIP is an unmodified CLIP dual encoder: test captions are still read through the 77-token window, and only training sees the full caption. We find that this fine-tuning erodes CLIP's zero-shot classification, most when the fusion output is not a combination of CLIP embeddings, and add a frozen-CLIP preservation loss on the image and short-caption embeddings. With 20k pairs and one epoch, ERCLIP improves long-caption R@1 over CLIP by 6.5--11.2 points, closing 62--77\ CLIP dual encoders align images and text in a shared embedding space and support both retrieval and zero-shot recognition . Their text encoders, however, read at most 77 tokens, so detailed descriptions are truncated and the discarded facts never reach the embedding. Long-context methods extend the positional embeddings, match features across caption lengths, or learn token selection . They rely on large long-caption corpora and rarely report what the adaptation does to the tasks CLIP already solves, a cost documented for classification fine-tuning but not for long-caption retrieval. We study the opposite regime: 20k image--caption pairs, 2\ Our contributions are a sentence-level scheme that adapts CLIP to long captions from 20k pairs without changing its inference; evidence that the fusion module sets the cost to zero-shot ability, which motivates gated residual fusion and a label-free preservation loss; and a joint evaluation of long-caption retrieval, short-caption retrieval, and zero-shot classification at one fixed budget. The ingredients are not new ; our focus is their behaviour under scarce data. CLIP learns image and text encoders contrastively ; follow-up work changes the data, objective, or supervision , all at a scale far beyond our budget. LongCLIP stretches the positional embeddings to 248 tokens and aligns the principal components of short-caption features . SmartCLIP and TULIP learn structured selection or relative positional encodings for long text , and HiMo-CLIP models semantic hierarchy and monotonicity . All are trained on large corpora, e.g., 1M ShareGPT4V captions for LongCLIP. LongCLIP's ablations include aligning an image with both its long and short caption, and anchoring short-text features to the frozen CLIP encoder, and find both inferior to its own strategy . ERCLIP's objective is close to these variants, and we do not claim the losses as new. We study them in a different setting: a 77-token encoder that reaches content beyond its window only through sentence-level fusion during training, an anchor on the image as well as the short caption, and 20k pairs for one epoch. We ask how much of the full-scale gain this recovers, and at what cost, without large data or compute. Fine-tuning CLIP degrades its zero-shot ability. WiSE-FT recovers much of it by weight interpolation , LP-FT shows that full fine-tuning distorts pretrained features , and FLYP retains more by fine-tuning with the contrastive pretraining loss ; continual learning earlier used distillation to a frozen copy or parameter regularisation . Only the image and short-caption embeddings, the interface of zero-shot classification, are anchored. The long-caption embedding stays free, since pretrained CLIP has no good long-caption representation to anchor it to . Unlike weight interpolation after training , $_{}$ anchors features during training and needs no labels or extra data. Fusion and teacher are used only in training. At test time ERCLIP is the adapted dual encoder with CLIP's 77-token context, so longer captions are truncated exactly as in CLIP. ``Free-length'' thus refers to training, where every sentence reaches the encoder; the gains in Tab.~ come from better alignment of the part of the caption the encoder reads, not from reading all of it. Sentence-wise encoding at test time is left to future work. ERCLIP keeps the parameters and latency of its backbone. We train on the first 20k pairs, in COCO image-id order, of the 50k-image pool shared by COCO and ShareGPT4V , with the first COCO caption as the short caption and the ShareGPT4V description as the long one. We report Recall@1 for image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval on Urban1K, ShareGPT4V-1k, DOCCI, and Long-DCI and on the 5k images of COCO val2017, and zero-shot accuracy on CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet with the CLIP prompt ensemble. Only Urban1K overlaps with our training data: 61 of its images are in our training set and 170 in our pool. For comparability with published results we report the full Urban1K set; on the 830 clean images every run's gain over CLIP changes by at most 1.2 points and the ranking of the variants is unchanged. ERCLIP fits when only thousands of long captions and one GPU are available, e.g., in a specialised domain, and the result must remain a standard CLIP checkpoint that keeps short-caption retrieval and zero-shot classification usable. Its inference cost equals CLIP's, a small advantage over LongCLIP, whose extra cost is confined to a 248-token text pass; we did not benchmark it.
