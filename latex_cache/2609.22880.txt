Graph-based retrieval enriches an underlying dense passage index with structural signals . ~, the successor of HippoRAG~, couples a passage-graph personalized-PageRank walk with dense ANN retrieval over an ~ index: PPR mass concentrates on passages that are graph-neighbours of dense-similar seeds, capturing the multi-hop structure of questions that single-vector ANN misses. ~ replaces PPR with beam search over proposition paths, trading per-query latency for explicit-chain interpretability. Our earlier paper~ introduces a PIT-Boltzmann score calibration that lets graph and vector scores be linearly mixed at a per-dataset thermo $ $. All three families are : every query pays the graph traversal cost, regardless of whether the question is multi-hop enough to benefit. Our work treats one such pipeline as the free retrieval substrate and asks the orthogonal question of when an even more expensive layer should be invoked. A long line of work has explored how to combine multiple retrievers without re-training. Reciprocal Rank Fusion ~ averages the reciprocal ranks of items across multiple lists and has remained surprisingly competitive because it is parameter-free and rank-invariant under score scaling. Linear interpolation of $$ and dense scores requires per-corpus score calibration~ but is the dominant approach in modern hybrid retrieval; 's contribution is to extend this calibration to graph-vector mixtures via per-source temperature scaling followed by Boltzmann normalization. Across all these methods, ensembling is a coverage-preserving operation: multiple retrievers' candidates union into a richer pool but no information beyond rank is introduced. LLM reranking changes that: it reorders the merged list using semantic reasoning that the score-level fusions cannot replicate, and our Phase-2 ablations show 8 score-only fusion variants all reaching K=5 coverage $ 0.623$ on 2Wiki vs.\ LLM rerank's $0.800$ . The LLM rerank is therefore a fundamentally different operation than fusion, and per-query gating is the right level at which to manage its cost. A growing body of work appends a large generative LM to rescore a small candidate set returned by a cheap first-stage retriever. ~ demonstrated that frontier LLMs can be prompted listwise and approach or beat supervised cross-encoders. ~ distills these listwise rankings into a smaller open model for cost-efficient deployment. A parallel line of work uses supervised rerankers trained on relevance pairs: cross-encoders such as ~ and ~, and the late-interaction model ~. Our Phase-2 baseline shows that , the strongest open cross-encoder we tested, reaches only $0.595$ at $K\!=\!5$ on 2Wiki vs.\ Llama-3.3-70B's $0.800$ at roughly $50 $ lower inference cost. This is the cost-quality tradeoff that motivates per-query gating: the LLM is more accurate an order of magnitude more expensive than its cheaper alternatives, so the policy question is when to spend the cost. We treat the LLM reranker as a fixed ceiling module and ask only to invoke it. Several recent systems route between retrieval modes. Self-RAG~ trains a critic to decide whether retrieval is useful per-token; Adaptive-RAG~ classifies questions into single-hop / multi-hop complexity classes and routes to different retrievers per class; Self-Route~ chooses between short-context retrieval and long-context direct prompting. The shared intuition is that the cost of retrieval (or the next pipeline stage) is non-uniform across queries, so a learned router can save inference without losing accuracy. None of these papers routes the LLM reranker specifically; the gate-then-rerank decision sits at a coarser cost-quality knee. In our own family of work, ~ routes between query-only retrieval and graph-fusion via a regime-conditional classifier and matches always-fusion coverage at $5290\ compute; we adapt the same per-query routing intuition to the more expensive LLM-rerank choice, selecting the gate under a coverage constraint against always-rerank rather than against the cheap baseline, and reporting where that constraint fails to hold out of sample. ~ uses an LLM judge $s $ at query time to score bridge passages, which is the cost profile to ours: adds query-time LLM cost on top of dense retrieval, while amortizes it away on the cells where it would not have helped. Several papers that appeared while this work was in preparation route on cost. Retrieval-as-a-Decision~ gates without training; RAGRouter-Bench benchmarks lightweight routing across retrieval strategies of different token cost; CA-RAG~ picks a retrieval per query by maximizing a utility over quality, latency and token cost; Adaptive Re-Ranking~ adapts the re-ranking stage itself; and BalanceRAG~ certifies threshold pairs for an LLM-only$ $RAG cascade at a prescribed risk level using sequential graphical testing. Two works are closer still. ARDS~ trains a lightweight classifier on retrieval signals to decide per query whether a cross-encoder reranker should run at all, the same rerank/no-rerank framing as ours with a cheaper reranker; gate a few-shot reranker with an uncertainty estimate at a calibrated operating point and document the cases in which reranking degrades results. These share our premise that stage cost is non-uniform across queries. They differ in the routed object: none gates a listwise LLM reranker over a merged graph-vector candidate set with an executable distilled fallback, and none reports per-query harm alongside the aggregate coverage constraint. We do not claim the framing itself as new. BalanceRAG's risk control is complementary rather than competing: our per-cell threshold $T$ is selected by out-of-fold sweep, and the $3$--$7.5$\,pp held-out regressions we report in Section~ are exactly the kind of gap a certified threshold procedure is designed to close. Conformal prediction~ turns a scoring rule into prediction sets with a distribution-free coverage guarantee, given a calibration sample that is disjoint from the test sample and a fixed, nested family of sets. An earlier draft of this paper reported ``conformal prediction-set sizes'' for the gated pipeline; those numbers were computed on the same queries used to choose the threshold and with missing cutoffs filled from larger ones, so they were neither conformal nor exact. Section~ now reports plain empirical coverage-versus-$K$ curves on held-out queries for the always-rerank pipeline and for the gated pipeline, with no guarantee claimed. LLM relevance scores can be distilled into cheaper scorers. ~ distills frontier-LLM listwise rankings into a 7B model; classical cross-encoder distillation underlies most of the dense-retrieval pipeline. Our distillation is a per- GBM regression on the cached Llama-3.3-70B relevance scores of the merged candidates, with features derived from embeddings and rank positions in the upstream retrievers. We do not use the distilled scorer as a standalone retriever; it is one of the two executable the gate can choose (the other is 's own top-$K$), and it is trained inside every evaluation fold . When the gate skips the LLM, the query is served from a fallback ranking that must be computable without the gold label. We use two. $H $ is 's own top-$K$. $D $ is the top-$K$ of a ranking: a per- gradient-boosted regressor predicts the cached Llama-3.3-70B relevance score of each candidate in the merged list $M $ from 33 cheap features, exactly those the production code computes: the cosine between the query and passage embeddings; the passage's 0-based rank in $H $ and in $V $ and a constant placeholder for the rank; its position in $M $; the first 8 dimensions of the elementwise product and of the difference of the two embeddings; and the first 12 dimensions of the query embedding. The regressor is trained inside every evaluation fold on the training queries' cached scores ; the candidates are then sorted by predicted score. An earlier draft of this paper scored a third fallback, the per-query maximum of $H$'s and $D$'s hit indicators. That quantity needs the gold label and is therefore an oracle, not a policy; the served implementation, which placed $H$'s top-$K$ before $D$'s in a de-duplicating merge, returned exactly $H $ on every evaluated query. The three cells whose savings rested on that fallback were the ones that carried the earlier headline, and they are gone from this version. A cell's policy is the tuple selected inside the training fold; at inference it is one classifier, one threshold and an optional projection matrix, dispatched by a dictionary lookup on . The Platt-calibrated harm probabilities of Section~ have small expected calibration error on held-out rows, yet the skip sets chosen by their own expected harm realise $0.6$\,pp more harm than promised on average. Choosing the most aggressive of $24$ candidates by a quantity estimated on the same inner folds is a winner's curse, and a budget stated to a user should be discounted for it; a split of the training fold between calibration and selection, or a conformal bound on the harm count, would trade savings for a promise that holds, and we have run neither. The probabilities also rank harm poorly , so the achievable savings at any budget are set by each cell's harm base rate rather than by the classifier. The gate treats all queries in a cell uniformly. Routing work such as Adaptive-RAG~ and Self-RAG~ benefits from explicit query-type classification; an upstream query-type classifier choosing the fallback or the threshold per query is a natural extension, and the oracle column of Table~ bounds what any such refinement could add. The distilled fallback regresses on the cached Llama-3.3-70B scores, so it inherits the reranker's errors and cannot exceed it; where the distilled top-$K$ covers more than 's the gate chose it, where it does not the gate fell back on . A different teacher would move both the fallback and the reranker, and with them the whole trade-off; we have not tried one. LLM rerankers are a real accuracy boost for graph-augmented multi-hop retrieval and a real cost on every query. We asked whether a small gate over pre-LLM features can tell, per query, when the reranker is not needed, and we answered it under a protocol in which nothing the gate needs is chosen on the queries it is scored on. The answer is a qualified yes: the gate skips half of the reranker calls on average, four of nine cells are non-inferior at $1$\,pp, the decisions are far better than random skipping at the same cost on every high-lift cell, and the cost saving tracks the skip rate. It is also a plain no to the claim the earlier version made: the gate is not lossless, harmful skips occur in eight of nine cells, and the five cells with the largest reranker lift pay $1.2$ to $3.3$\,pp for $21$ to $54\ Whether that trade is worth taking is a deployment decision that Table~ now gives the numbers for. Setting the threshold from a stated expected-harm budget over calibrated probabilities makes the trade explicit: $42\ $1$\,pp, and a realised harm about half again what was promised, which a user should budget for. The natural follow-ups are more training queries per cell, since the in-fold selection is choosing among policies it cannot distinguish; a selection-aware or conformal bound on the harm count; a gate that defends several cutoffs at once; passage-level rather than title-level targets; and a test of the gate under an encoder replacement, which this paper does not contain.
