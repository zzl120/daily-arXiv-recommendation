Existing approaches face a fundamental trade-off: click tables perform well for queries seen before but cannot generalise to new query formulations, while embedding-based retrievers can generalise across content but often struggle to rank navigational targets accurately. To address this challenge, we introduce , a generative retrieval framework that stores web page representations directly in the parameters of a language model. Given a query, the model generates a compact two-token semantic identifier that is constrained to a table of real URLs, enabling direct retrieval of a specific web page. We develop , which indexes 201 million web pages, and evaluate it on real-world navigational search traffic from the month following training. Our results show that generative retrieval is particularly effective at handling new ways of expressing requests for familiar pages. A user who types ``nyt connections'' wants one particular page, the puzzle at , and the search engine serves the query well only if it puts that page first. called such queries ; they were a fifth to a quarter of the queries he analysed. He set them apart from queries, which ask for content on a topic, and from queries, which seek a service such as a purchase or a download. Since a service is reached through a page, we count a transactional query as navigational whenever its clicks concentrate on one page . Navigational search is thus the task of finding one page among the hundreds of millions that a search engine can return, and of ranking it first. Search engines answer such queries with a click table or a dense retriever, and each covers only part of the traffic. A click table returns the page that past users clicked for the same query, so it has no answer for a query it has never seen; in the traffic we study, a third of navigational clicks come from such queries . A dense retriever answers any query by comparing its embedding with those of the pages , but similarity is not what navigation asks for. For almost a third of navigational traffic, dense retrieval has the target among its first ten results but ranks another URL above it, often another page of the same site . Large language models asked for the URL from memory are neither accurate nor cheap enough , and they can name URLs that do not exist~. Generative retrieval takes a different route~: a language model is trained to write the identifier of the page a query leads to, so that the model itself is the index. Navigation suits this design. Each navigational query has one right answer, the answers come from a fixed list of URLs, and past clicks show which page each query led to. From these examples a model can learn which of many similar pages users want, and its output can be restricted to URLs that exist. GR has not yet been shown to work at web scale, however. It has reached the 8.8M passages of MS MARCO, and going beyond was called ``an important and unsolved challenge''~. Larger corpora have remained difficult~, and recent studies of how GR scales and where it fails still work at that scale or below~. Generative systems with 100 million to a billion targets exist, but they index videos, products, advertising keywords or whole sites~, not individual web pages. Web pages raise three problems at once. Every one of hundreds of millions of pages needs a short identifier that names it and no other page . The model must learn about a billion query--URL pairs of very uneven popularity over tens of thousands of training steps, during which pairs learned early can be forgotten . And many pages have been reached by only a few queries, so the model has little to learn them from . This section explains how dense and generative retrievers work and introduces the terms used throughout this report. We call the items returned by a retriever documents; in our setting, these documents are web pages identified by their URLs. A dense retriever, or embedding retriever, uses a neural encoder to map queries and documents to vectors. The encoder is trained so that a query lands close to the documents relevant to it~, and the retriever answers a query with the documents whose vectors are most similar to the query's, usually by cosine similarity. Because it compares meanings rather than words, it generalises to new queries, and it is the standard tool for informational search~. It needs an index that stores a vector for every document. Searching that index exactly costs memory and time in proportion to the number of documents, and compressed approximate indexes trade some accuracy for size~. A larger index also holds more documents that can come close to a query by chance. found that, as the index grows, the accuracy of dense retrievers falls faster than that of sparse retrievers, which match words, and the more so the fewer dimensions the vectors have. measures this fall for our dense baseline and for GR. A generative retriever produces the identifier of a document one token at a time, as a language model produces text~. It learns the corpus from training samples that map queries to the identifiers of their relevant documents, often together with samples that map a document's own text to its identifier~. Because the corpus lives in the weights, learning new documents can erode what the model knew about old ones~. measures what governs this forgetting. An identifier can be an arbitrary number, a string such as a title or a URL~, or a , a short sequence of codes in which similar documents share a prefix~. SIDs are usually built by residual quantisation~. Each document is embedded as a vector, and $k$-means clustering assigns the vector to its nearest centroid, whose index is the first code. A second $k$-means clusters the , the vectors minus their centroids, and gives the second code. Documents that are close in the embedding space thus share their first code and are told apart by the second. Nothing in this procedure keeps two documents from receiving the same codes, however, whereas a retriever needs every identifier to name exactly one document; describes how ensures this. GR produces its results by beam search, which keeps the $k$ most probable partial identifiers at each step and returns $k$ complete ones, ranked by probability. Decoding is constrained by a prefix tree, or , of all valid identifiers: at each step, only tokens that continue some valid identifier are allowed~. Every output therefore names an existing document, and deleting an identifier from the trie removes its document from every possible output. Every URL in a universe receives a two-token semantic identifier $ $. We call $s_0$ the first code and $s_1$ the second code. Each is a number from 0 to 65,535, that is, an unsigned 16-bit integer. The URL of , , has the SID $ $. Two tokens keep decoding to two steps, and two codes of 65,536 values leave room for far more URLs than holds . The codes come from quantising an embedding of the URL, so that URLs with similar embeddings share their first code . The text embedded is the URL's : its normalised form followed by up to five of its most-clicked queries, for example ``''. A URL text contains no page content. BM25 and the dense retriever index the same texts . The encoder is that of the dense retriever: a checkpoint of Octen-Embedding-4B~, whose embedding is truncated to its first 1,024 dimensions and passed through a linear map trained on navigational query--URL pairs from our click data. These 1,024-dimensional vectors, one per URL of , are the data points of two rounds of $k$-means clustering with 65,536 centroids each~. The first code $s_0$ is the index of the nearest centroid of the first round. The second round clusters the residuals, and the second code $s_1$ is the index of the nearest residual centroid, unless the uniqueness rule below assigns another. Plain residual quantisation would give 51.5\ another URL. We instead make second-level codes unique within each first-level group by a capacity-constrained assignment, in the manner of collision-free identifier schemes~. The assignment runs in proposal rounds: every unassigned URL proposes its next-nearest residual centroid, and when several URLs of one first-level group propose the same code, the closest keeps it. Every URL ends with a unique pair; 63.5\ nearest. Each SID therefore names exactly one URL, and all metrics are computed over URLs. The model writes an SID as two new tokens, one for each code. The same number means different things in the two positions: as $s_0$ it names a group of similar URLs, as $s_1$ one URL within a group. We therefore add two sets of 65,536 tokens to the vocabulary of Qwen3, one set for $s_0$ and one for $s_1$. They are numbered after the 151,936 tokens of Qwen3, first the set for $s_0$, then the set for $s_1$, so that in the example $s_0 = 1424$ is token $151{,}936 + 1{,}424 = 153{,}360$ and $s_1 = 56829$ is token $151{,}936 + 65{,}536 + 56{,}829 = 274{,}301$. Each new token needs an embedding, the vector that represents it inside the model. We start it at the average of the final-layer hidden states that the model computes for the prompts of queries leading to URLs with that code, so that from the start the model favours the code after similar queries~. This section turns them into a training recipe and estimates what a universe of a billion URLs would take. The measurements of suggest five rules for further training rounds. Expose key query--URL pairs late, while the learning rate is still at least about a quarter of its peak, since exposures at lower rates teach little . Use the largest batch the hardware allows, with the learning rate scaled in proportion, so that the decay window holds more exposures without growing in steps . Avoid re-warming . Give new pages their queries, not only an identifier . Evaluate decayed checkpoints only . A model of about 13B parameters could index a billion URLs as well as ours indexes 201M, if training delivers the exposures. Three quantities bound a universe of a billion URLs. We extrapolate from the round-3 end point, whose decay window we measured, and assume that a larger universe has as many training pairs per URL as . The grid's iso-accuracy rule , which holds the exposures per pair fixed, asks for about 13B parameters and 15 times the training compute of . A decay window can be about 12,000 steps long before its own start is half forgotten. At our batch size it holds on the order of 200 million single exposures, about as many pairs as the round-3 end point recalls, and at four times the batch size enough for a billion URLs at the same recall rate. Two tokens of 65,536 codes provide 4.3 billion identifiers, of which a billion URLs would fill 23\ extrapolated from a single point, and scaling trends often fail to extrapolate~. The exposure budget is moreover an upper bound, since it is measured at hit@10 and exposures late in a window teach little .
