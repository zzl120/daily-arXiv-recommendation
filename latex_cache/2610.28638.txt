However, the three tasks rely on historical evidence with substantially different structures and granularities. Meanwhile, their prediction targets differ in output form, making it difficult to integrate retrieved evidence through a single fusion mechanism without causing mismatch or negative transfer. These issues can increase prediction errors and subsequently lead to imbalanced resource allocation, where some regions experience unmet demand. In contrast, others suffer from underutilization, ultimately degrading overall efficiency and service quality. To address these challenges, we propose TAGR, a Task-Adaptive Geo-Semantic Retrieval framework across heterogeneous urban delivery tasks. TAGR constructs an LLM-based geo-semantic shared prior and task-specific retrieval banks to share regional knowledge while preserving heterogeneous task-specific evidence. The LLM-derived regional representations encode geographic context and semantic relations among regions, providing transferable knowledge for all three tasks without requiring online LLM inference. It further introduces a task-adaptive retrieval augmentation module to accommodate heterogeneous output structures, thereby mitigating information mismatch and negative transfer caused by a uniform retrieval-fusion strategy. Experiments on three city datasets demonstrate that TAGR consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across multiple tasks. In urban instant delivery scenarios, demand forecasting, estimated time of arrival prediction, and next-stop prediction constitute three core spatio-temporal tasks that underpin the operational optimization of delivery systems~. They correspond to different stages of the delivery workflow: regional demand affects workload allocation, delivery conditions influence order completion times, and the current delivery state guides subsequent routing decisions. Although these tasks share geographic and operational context, the historical evidence they rely on differs substantially in structure and granularity. In addition, their prediction targets also vary in output form. This heterogeneity makes it challenging to exploit their shared geographic and operational context without compromising task-specific modeling. Existing approaches generally follow two paradigms: independent task-specific modeling and joint shared-representation modeling. The former trains a dedicated model for each task and therefore captures task-specific patterns independently, whereas the latter employs a shared encoder or unified representation to extract common knowledge across tasks. However, when the tasks differ substantially in data granularity and output form, excessive representation sharing may obscure task-specific information and introduce cross-task interference~. Therefore, the central distinction between the two paradigms lies in how they balance task-specific modeling against cross-task knowledge sharing. {sections/figures/fig1-two.pdf} Task-independent modeling employs a separate model architecture for each task according to its data structure, feature composition, and prediction target. This preserves the individual characteristics of each task. As illustrated in Figure~ , demand forecasting models temporal demand sequences, ETA prediction relies on order attributes and geographic context, and next-stop prediction considers route prefixes and dynamic candidate sets. Such task-specific architectures can preserve the distinctive characteristics of each task and avoid forcing heterogeneous information into a unified representation. However, the isolated models provide no effective channel for cross-task knowledge exchange. Consequently, shared regional semantics, delivery patterns, and complementary historical evidence cannot be effectively transferred or reused, limiting the overall representation capacity of multi-task delivery prediction{~.} Joint multi-task modeling instead introduces a shared encoder or representation space to facilitate information exchange and improve parameter efficiency. However, as illustrated in Figure~ , the three tasks rely on historical evidence with substantially different structures and granularities. Their outputs also take distinct forms, including multi-step demand sequences, scalar arrival times, and rankings over dynamic candidate sets. Hard parameter-sharing methods compress these heterogeneous signals into a largely unified representation, despite their different information requirements and optimization objectives. Consequently, the learned representation may favor certain tasks but deviate from others, causing optimization conflicts, information mismatch, and negative transfer that undermine task-specific prediction~. To address these challenges, we propose TAGR, a unified framework for heterogeneous urban delivery tasks. TAGR first constructs a geo-semantic shared prior to capture reusable regional knowledge across tasks. Specifically, a shared frozen LLM encoder maps task-oriented regional descriptions into a common semantic space, thereby establishing a unified geographic knowledge interface. However, shared semantics alone cannot preserve the distinct historical evidence required by different tasks. TAGR therefore introduces task-specific adapters and retrieval banks. The adapters retain information relevant to each prediction objective, and the retrieval banks store historical evidence in the structures and granularities required by demand forecasting, ETA prediction, and next-stop prediction. Together, these components enable cross-task knowledge reuse without obscuring task-specific patterns, providing the basis for subsequent task-adaptive retrieval and output-aligned fusion. We propose TAGR, a retrieval-augmented framework that jointly models ETA and next-stop prediction while extending a unified geo-semantic prior and task-adaptive retrieval mechanism to demand forecasting, thereby supporting all three core tasks in urban instant delivery. Two synergistic modules are designed: an LLM-based geo-semantic prior with task-specific retrieval banks to share regional knowledge and preserve heterogeneous evidence, and a task-adaptive retrieval augmentation module to match each task’s granularity and output form, reducing noise and negative transfer. We conduct comprehensive multi-task delivery prediction experiments on three real-world city datasets spanning the scale range of the benchmark. The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of TAGR across multiple cities and tasks, and ablation studies further verify the contribution of each module. Demand forecasting, ETA prediction, and route/next-stop prediction constitute the core spatio-temporal tasks in last-mile delivery~. General spatio-temporal graph networks---including DCRNN, Graph WaveNet, MTGNN, STGCN, and AGCRN---provide strong region-level forecasting backbones~, while classical heuristics such as nearest-neighbor regression, mobility Markov chains, and constraint-programming routing solvers remain competitive references for ETA and next-stop prediction~. Delivery-specific models such as DeepRoute and Graph2Route instead predict the courier's visiting order directly~. Beyond task-specific spatio-temporal predictors, recent studies have explored general-purpose time-series and urban spatio-temporal foundation models, including MOMENT and Timer-XL ~. Recent surveys have further systematized the emerging landscape of spatio-temporal foundation models ~. However, existing studies typically address these tasks in isolation and do not jointly cover demand, ETA, and next-stop prediction. Retrieval augmentation combines parametric models with non-parametric external knowledge~, and has recently been applied to spatio-temporal prediction, e.g., RAST for traffic forecasting~ and retrieval-augmented time-series forecasters~. These two lines of work remain largely separate: delivery-oriented multi-task models such as M2G4RTP~ jointly predict route order and arrival time but do not retrieve external evidence, whereas retrieval-augmented spatio-temporal models target homogeneous continuous-valued targets and do not handle order-level regression together with ranking over a dynamic candidate set. TAGR is, to our knowledge, the first framework to build task-adaptive geo-semantic retrieval for heterogeneous delivery tasks whose input structures, prediction granularities, and output spaces all differ, injecting evidence at output-compatible fusion stages. Compared with conventional spatio-temporal models , TAGR achieves stronger demand forecasting: these models capture temporal patterns and fixed spatial dependencies purely through graph convolution or recurrent structures, and cannot explicitly represent regional functions, road accessibility, or delivery-related semantics that vary across regions, whereas TAGR complements them with an LLM-derived geo-semantic prior that supplies exactly this missing information, confirming the value of combining geographic semantics with spatio-temporal modeling for urban delivery prediction. Compared with heuristic and non-parametric baselines , TAGR is substantially more robust across tasks: these methods rely on static statistics, local similarity, or hand-crafted rules, so a fixed rule or single similarity measure cannot adapt to complex order attributes, dynamic candidate sets, and the differing output forms of the three tasks, whereas TAGR learns task-specific representations and dynamically fuses retrieved evidence, showing that adaptive, learnable retrieval is necessary where static heuristics fall short. Compared with RAST and IMPEL, which already combine learning with retrieval or semantic representations and are thus the closest comparisons to TAGR, TAGR still outperforms both on most metrics: RAST applies a relatively uniform retrieval-augmentation strategy that cannot simultaneously adapt to demand regression, ETA regression, and next-stop ranking, and IMPEL uses semantic representations only as static input features without task-specific historical retrieval, whereas TAGR injects retrieved evidence into the representation, prediction, and candidate spaces according to each task's output form, underscoring the importance of output-aligned retrieval fusion under heterogeneous task targets. The results further reveal two finer-grained patterns. For ETA prediction, TAGR obtains the best RMSE in all three cities, though its MAE is not always the lowest. Since RMSE is more sensitive to large errors and long-tail orders, this indicates that the Huber loss and retrieval-reliability gate mainly suppress large deviations on abnormal completion times rather than uniformly lowering average error, showing that ETA prediction benefits as much from robustness to outliers as from average accuracy. For next-stop prediction, TAGR achieves the most significant gains of the three tasks. Existing route models capture transition patterns or sequential dependencies but lack explicit retrieval of similar historical routes to form a candidate-level prior, whereas TAGR converts retrieved future stops into a route prior that directly enhances candidate scoring, confirming the particular value of historical-route retrieval for next-stop ranking. We further compare task-independent modeling, shared--private modeling without gating, and the full gated TAGR on Hangzhou . Ungated shared-private fusion outperforms task-independent modeling, showing that a shared component enables knowledge exchange between the two tasks; yet it still falls short of gated TAGR. This is because task-independent modeling fully isolates the regional knowledge accumulated by each task, while ungated sharing, although it introduces a shared component, combines the shared and private information through a fixed operator that cannot adapt when the two tasks require different sharing ratios on a given sample. This shows that the relationship between ETA and next-stop cannot be reduced to whether to share representations, but also depends on how much to share on a per-sample basis, confirming the necessity of the gated design over both ungated sharing and independent modeling. The results are shown in Figure~. {sections/figures/fig3.pdf} Increasing the retrieval size does not consistently improve performance. The benefit of retrieval comes from relevant historical evidence rather than the number of neighbors; overly large $K$ introduces weakly related samples and noise. Thus, retrieval size should be selected according to task-specific evidence structures. Demand and ETA generally prefer small or moderate $K$. Both tasks depend on locally similar demand patterns or order contexts, while excessive neighbors may include inconsistent trends or completion-time distributions. This indicates that regression tasks require precise retrieval rather than broad retrieval. Next-stop prediction performs best around $K$=5. A moderate number of similar routes provides a clear route prior, whereas too many routes make the candidate prior more dispersed and less discriminative. This shows that next-stop ranking is especially sensitive to the relevance of retrieved route evidence. In this paper, we propose TAGR, a geo-semantic and task-adaptive retrieval-augmented framework for urban delivery prediction. Experiments on three real-world city datasets demonstrate consistent improvements in demand forecasting, ETA estimation, and next-stop prediction. In future work, we will explore adaptive retrieval sizes, online knowledge-base updates, and evaluations in more cities and evolving delivery environments.
