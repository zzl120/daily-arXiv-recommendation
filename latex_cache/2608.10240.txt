Within recommendation, the problem is treated as a collaborative-filtering one: FeatProp~ propagates features along an item-to-item co-interaction graph, MMGACL~ completes features by diffusion with bimodal attention, SiBraR~ trains a single-branch encoder on random modality subsets, and LRMM~ applies modality dropout to review-based rating prediction, none of which model the temporal item ordering that sequential recommenders exploit. Outside recommendation, Bernoulli modality masking is a mature training-time tool, used for audiovisual gesture recognition~, talking-face synthesis~, medical-image segmentation~, and masked cross-modal projection~, but always on a single sample, where no user sequence exists. Neither line covers multi-modal recommendation, a gap that a recent survey of 354 missing-modality papers~ names explicitly as an under-explored temporal setting. We close it by bringing modality masking into sequential recommendation with a design built for the temporal structure of user histories. We propose , a per-sample Bernoulli modality mask injected at the fusion point of any multi-modal sequential recommender. For each training sample, each modality stream is independently zeroed with probability $p$, and the same mask applies to every item in the user's chronological sequence. This design preserves the temporal signal the sequential Transformer relies on, and matches real catalog missingness, which tends to cluster within a session or a product category rather than varying independently across items. is also a : the same module integrates into MM-SASRec, IISAN~, MISSRec~, and fMRLRec~ without per-architecture tuning and leaves the host optimizer and hyperparameters untouched. During training, we draw an independent Bernoulli mask for each sample $b$ in the minibatch and each modality $m \{v,t\}$, m_b^{ } , _m }) = m_b^{ } f_m }), and substitute $ f_m$ for $f_m$ in . The mask is per-sample, not per-item: all items in user $b$'s sequence share the same modality mask. This matches how modalities go missing in real catalogs, where an entire product category or data source tends to lack a modality at once, rather than an artificial pattern where missing items are scattered evenly through the sequence . At test time the mask is not applied, except for the deterministic masks used in our robustness evaluation . Unlike standard dropout, which at test time rescales activations by $1/ $ to preserve their expected magnitude as a regularizer~, zeroes a whole modality with no rescaling, following the established modality-dropout convention~. Its goal is invariance to a genuinely missing modality rather than variance reduction, so the full-modality input seen at test is simply the $p\!=\!0$ case the model already encountered during training, and no compensating scale is required. A natural alternative is a mask that decides separately for each item whether to drop a modality, so some items in a sequence keep both modalities while others lose one. We use the per-sample mask instead because real missingness is whole-modality and structured by category or source rather than independent across items: a modality tends to go absent in a block, the same pattern that produces the cold-start problem~, and in our data the text-missing rate varies sharply across categories (34.3\ A per-item mask would instead train the model for an independent, missing-at-random pattern that is rarely seen in deployment~.
