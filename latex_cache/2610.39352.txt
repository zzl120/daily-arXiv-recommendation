 Recommenders on Web platforms such as e-commerce and music streaming need their training recipes re-tuned for each dataset. Large language model agents can now rewrite these recipes, enabling recursive self-improvement of recommender systems. Existing agents propose one edit per round, fully retrain the recommender, and keep the edit only if its ranking quality improves. Unlike programs or patches that can be scored by execution, a recommender edit changes how the model is trained, which raises two challenges. First, ranking feedback is delayed: an edit's ranking quality is known only after full retraining, so much of the retraining budget goes to edits that are later discarded. Second, the signals available before retraining are unreliable: sampled training losses can look solved while full-ranking performance remains poor, and they are not comparable across edits that change the loss or sampler. We propose RecRSI, which generates diverse candidate edits from a diagnosis of the current model and selects one for full retraining using early ranking feedback: each candidate receives a short trial, the first steps of its full training run, scored by full-ranking NDCG@10 on held-out probe users. With two sequential recommenders on three datasets and the same number of full retrainings, RecRSI significantly outperforms existing self-evolving agents in every setting, and on both Amazon datasets reaches in its first round a ranking quality that none of them reaches in five. {figure/teaser.pdf} Ranking feedback arrives only after a full retraining, and across three agents , two backbones and three datasets , 37 of 90 retrainings (41\ The training recipe of a recommender, including but not limited to its architecture , training loss , negative sampler and optimization schedule , largely determines how well it performs, and the best recipe varies across domains and datasets . Consequently, identifying an effective recipe still relies on manual and iterative tuning, in which practitioners repeatedly modify the training code and retrain the model. Large language model agents have begun to take over part of this work. Building on coding agents that evolve programs from execution feedback~, recommender agents rewrite the training code of a recommender, retrain the edited model, and keep the edit when the retrained model ranks better on validation users~, bringing recursive self-improvement from general agents~ into recommender development. This loop is inherited from domains in which candidates can be evaluated directly by execution, yet recommender edits depart from this setting in an essential respect. Programs for algorithmic problems~ and patches for coding benchmarks~ can be assessed by executing them on test instances, whereas a recommender edit modifies the procedure through which the model is learned, so that its ~, the validation gain of the edited recommender, can be observed only after the model has been retrained on the interaction data. Consequently, proposing an edit requires a single LLM call, whereas evaluating it requires a complete retraining. Under this asymmetry, an agent is able to propose many candidate edits but to retrain few of them, and the improvement it attains depends largely on which candidates are selected for retraining. Existing agents propose one edit per round and retrain it without prior assessment, so that each retraining is spent on the first proposal of the LLM, and fewer than half of these retrainings yield a kept edit ). A natural alternative is to propose several edits per round and to retrain the most promising among them, provided that their relative promise can be estimated at a small fraction of the cost of a full retraining. Such an estimate is, however, non-trivial to obtain for recommender edits, because they undermine the signals on which early evaluation conventionally relies. In hyperparameter search, candidate configurations share a training objective and an evaluation protocol, so that their early losses or learning curves can be compared and extrapolated~. Recommender code edits violate this premise. Recommenders are commonly trained with sampled objectives, and studies of sampled evaluation have shown that outscoring a small set of sampled items reveals little about the rank of an item under the full-ranking protocol~, so the training loss may approach its floor while full-ranking performance remains poor. The difficulty is compounded when an edit targets the objective itself, as many effective edits do, since modifying the loss or the negative sampler also alters the scale on which training progress is measured and thereby deprives competing edits of a common basis for comparison ). Signals that assess an edit without training it, such as parent diagnosis or LLM patch judgments, avoid this difficulty but ignore how the edited model learns. These observations suggest that an early signal should be obtained through the same procedure as the outcome reward, with a shorter training run, rather than through a different quantity. The outcome reward is informative because it runs the edited training procedure and judges the resulting model by full-ranking performance, and its shortcoming lies in when it arrives rather than in what it measures. An early signal that runs the same training procedure for fewer steps and applies the same full-ranking measurement, two properties we call and , preserves what makes the outcome reward reliable at a fraction of its cost. Such brief runs resemble the low-fidelity evaluations of hyperparameter search~, but for recommender code edits their usefulness depends on keeping both properties, which signals read from the training loss give up. Building on this principle, we propose , a framework that compares candidate edits through short trials and spends the single full retraining of each round on the most promising one. In each round, the LLM proposes several diverse edits grounded in a diagnosis of the current model and a record of earlier rounds, and each executable edit receives a , which trains the edited recommender from the same initialization as the full retraining for a small fraction of its training steps and scores it by full-ranking NDCG@10 on held-out validation users. The edit with the highest trial score then receives the full retraining and is kept if it improves validation performance under the same rule as in existing agents. Because the short trial reuses the training and evaluation code of the full retraining and requires no learned reward model, this selection step can be incorporated into existing recommender agents. We evaluate RecRSI with SASRec and HSTU on two Amazon datasets and a music listening log, granting every method the same number of full retrainings and reporting the additional cost of the trials separately. A short trial trains an edited recipe from $ ^{ }$ for the first $K_s$ steps of its full training run and scores it by full-ranking NDCG@10 on probe users, and this score serves as the signal $S $ that decides which of the proposed edits receives the round's full retraining. Each round still spends one full retraining, which now goes to the edit with the highest trial score among several tried edits rather than to a single untried one, at the additional cost of the trials . We first describe how RecRSI proposes a batch of edits , then specify the short trial , and finally describe how the round spends its retraining and records what it has learned . The benefit of selecting among edits depends on the batch from which the selection is made, which should consist of edits that address the failures of the parent and that differ from one another, since near-identical edits are likely to yield similar outcomes. To this end, RecRSI grounds the proposal in a diagnosis of the parent and asks the LLM to distribute its edits over different components of the recipe. The diagnosis is computed by a forward pass of $ _p$ over probe users and summarizes where the ranking of the parent fails. It includes the rate from Section~ at which a positive scores below the strongest of 32 random items, together with the average score margin to that item, and the share of score variance explained by item identity alone, which indicates how close the parent is to ranking items in the same order for every user. It also reports how the positives of probe users are distributed over ranks 1--10, 11--100 and beyond 100 under the parent, as well as NDCG@10 on head and tail items, so that each hypothesis can name the group it aims to improve. The diagnosis describes the parent rather than any edit, and RecRSI therefore uses it to inform which edits are proposed while leaving the choice among them to the short trial. The prompt combines the diagnosis with the source code of the parent, the interface between a recipe and the training harness, and the memory $$ of earlier rounds . To keep the edits distinct, the LLM is asked to place them on different components of the recipe, such as the loss, the negative sampler, the optimizer, the prediction and the architecture, so that the batch is more likely to cover several hypotheses than variants of one. Each edit is returned as search-and-replace patches together with the component it changes, a hypothesis that cites the diagnosis, and the rank group it expects to improve first. Since edits are free-form code, RecRSI checks each of them before it touches real data, both to avoid spending a trial on code that cannot run and to reduce the risk that an edit alters how models are evaluated. The evaluation code is kept fixed and outside the editable recipe, and an edit is accepted when its patches apply cleanly, a static check finds no disallowed imports or attribute access, and a smoke test on a synthetic batch shows that the edited recipe completes a training step and still exposes the scoring interface that the evaluator calls. An edit that fails these checks receives one repair request containing the error message and is dropped if it fails again, and an edit whose code is identical to a recipe trained earlier in the run is removed as a duplicate. The trial runs the edited recipe itself, with its own architecture, loss, sampler, optimizer and schedule, from the initialization $ ^{ }$ and seed $ $ of the full retraining, and it follows that schedule as in the full run rather than compressing it to $K_s$ steps. Under deterministic execution, the trial model therefore coincides with the state that the full retraining of $T_a$ reaches after $K_s$ steps, so the trial and the outcome reward observe the same training process at two points in time. Starting from $ ^{ }$ also removes the need to transfer weights from the parent, so edits that add parameters, such as a new architectural module or a learned prediction head, are evaluated alongside loss and sampler edits. The trial score is computed by the evaluator of the outcome reward, with the same item set, masking of each user's history and tie handling, so $m_a$ is the full-ranking NDCG@10 that defines $Y$, computed on probe users instead of select users, and it is comparable across edits regardless of the loss they train with. Automated recommender design first searched predefined spaces, as in NASRec with its weight-sharing supernet~. LLM agents remove this restriction by editing code: general systems such as FunSearch, AlphaEvolve and DeepEvolve evolve programs from execution scores~, and recommender agents combine offline experiments with online validation~, accumulate experiment memory and skills~, route modification directions with a bandit~, or coordinate long-horizon research in industrial systems~. Self-EvolveRec is closest to our setting: it diagnoses the parent numerically, simulates user critiques~, and evolves its diagnostic code together with the model~. Beyond accuracy, study what successive recommenders should retain during RSI. These works choose edits to propose, whereas RecRSI selects edits for retraining and can use any of their proposers. Self-improving coding agents reduce the cost of judging a modification by evaluating it on task subsets~, by allocating evaluations across lineages~, or by ranking patches with an LLM judge before any benchmark run~; research preference models rank solutions or run pilot experiments first~, and AlphaEvolve passes programs through a cascade of increasingly expensive tests~. In hyperparameter and architecture search, multi-fidelity methods train many configurations briefly and continue only the promising ones~, learning-curve extrapolation predicts final accuracy from a prefix~, and zero-cost proxies score architectures at initialization~, a direction that Self-EvolveRec also offers as an efficient variant~. RecRSI borrows the low-fidelity trial of multi-fidelity search and can be read as a two-stage cascade, but its setting differs from both: multi-fidelity methods compare configurations that share one training objective, and cascade stages are tests designed by the user, whereas recommender code evolution compares open-ended LLM edits that often rewrite the objective itself, under a budget counted in full retrainings. RecRSI designs this first stage for training code: it starts from the shared initialization and compares edits by the outcome's full-ranking evaluation. Recommenders are commonly trained with sampled negatives or sampled softmax~, and both sampled metrics~ and sampled training objectives~ are known to distort full-ranking evaluation. Our measurements show that this distortion also hides from a self-improving agent which of its edits help, and RecRSI turns it into a design requirement for the selection signal. Recursive self-improvement of recommender systems is held back because an edit's ranking quality is known only after a full retraining, which forces each retraining onto a single untested edit, while the signals available earlier, such as the sampled training loss, do not reflect full-ranking performance. To this end, this work presented RecRSI, in which the LLM proposes a batch of diverse edits and short trials that follow the full training run and measure full-ranking performance choose the edit that receives the retraining. Experiments with two backbones on three datasets showed that RecRSI significantly outperformed AlphaEvolve, DeepEvolve and Self-EvolveRec in all six settings at the same number of retrainings and avoided the regressions of the other selection signals. The equal GPU-budget comparison further shows that selecting edits with ranking feedback improves the use of computation: RecRSI achieves higher Test NDCG@10 even when competing agents complete more rounds. Short trials therefore make ranking feedback actionable when choosing what to retrain. This work still has a few limitations. On the one hand, the main comparison fixes five rounds per trajectory. This horizon is close to where such searches already peak: Self-EvolveRec reaches its best model within 6--11 iterations in its own experiments~, and under one GPU hour , the baselines run 11--20 rounds and RecRSI still leads; longer searches on larger datasets remain to be evaluated. On the other hand, the large language model employed in this study is limited to a single open-weights model , and the effects of using different models have not yet been investigated.
