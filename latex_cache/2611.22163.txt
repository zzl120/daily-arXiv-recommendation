In this paper, we present Cluster-Guided Generative Reconstruction , which learns a hierarchy of semantic clusters and uses a cluster-level prior to steer item ranking for personalized recommendations. Unlike existing systems that only use clusters to pre-select candidates, CG2R adds the cluster prior directly to the item scoring rule, so that the learned cluster structure shapes the ranking itself. Classical collaborative filtering, such as Matrix Factorization ~ and Probabilistic Matrix Factorization ~, retrieves quickly over large catalogs but relies primarily on user–item interactions. Without side information or specialized extensions, they typically handle cold-start users and items poorly. More expressive neural and sequential models, including NeuMF~, SASRec~, BERT4Rec~, and LightGCN~, improve representation learning and temporal modeling, but they still score each item on its own and carry no explicit notion of the semantic region a recommendation belongs to. Large language model -based recommenders~ can model user preferences flexibly, but their inference cost and latency block them from serving as first-stage retrieval over a large catalog. Retrieval-augmented generation ~ and semantic clustering~ improve grounding and coherence by treating retrieval as a preprocessing step that narrows the candidate pool, but leaves the ranking function itself blind to semantic structures. Overall, existing recommendation approaches tend to sit at one of two extremes: they scale well but give no control over which semantic region of the catalog recommendations come from, or they reason expressively but too slowly for online inference under a tight latency budget. We argue that such limitations could be overcome by making semantic cluster structure part of the recommender itself. Specifically, instead of scoring $N$ catalog items independently, we can learn a hierarchy of semantic clusters and a cluster-level prior to steer item ranking, where the clusters specify an explicit semantic region of the catalog to stay within, and the prior is trained with $K$ cluster centroids . We propose , which consists of a three-phase framework: 1) the cluster retrieval phase learns a hierarchy of semantic item clusters and the user-cluster affinities from users' interaction histories; 2) the contextual expansion phase refines the cluster-level prior using the clusters each user has previously interacted with; 3) the constrained decoding phase applies the learned user-specific prior as a trust region over the catalog, similar to the idea of trust-region policy optimization~. The key difference between CG2R and prior cluster-based systems lies in where the cluster guidance acts: those systems only use clusters to preselect a candidate pool, or to regularize training, while CG2R adds the learned cluster prior directly to each item's semantic-relevance score. The cluster structure therefore influences the final ranking itself. We evaluate CG2R on three public benchmarks that vary widely in interaction density and catalog size, against classical, neural, and LLM-based baselines. Specifically, for MovieLens, the densest of the three, CG2R improves R@10 by 82\ The baselines themselves separate along the density axis. NeuMF is competitive on MovieLens but collapses on Google Local Washington , which is consistent with its reliance on the dense co-occurrence signal that a sparse local-business catalog does not provide. LLMRec is the strongest baseline on MovieLens, yet it falls behind PMF on both Steam and Google Local Washington, which suggests that a pretrained language prior does not make up for missing collaborative signal in every domain. In contrast, CG2R is the only method that holds up across all three regimes. Beyond improving ranking quality, CG2R is fast enough to serve as first-stage retrieval. In our offline benchmark experiments using a single GPU, CG2R's average end-to-end latency is 1.6--2.5\,ms per request, compared with 0.14--2.55\,ms for the reported classical and neural baselines across the three datasets. LLMRec requires 53.3--55.8\,s per request, making it less suitable for latency-sensitive applications without significant optimization. CG2R is also robust for different user–catalog scale and sparsity regimes. Steam has a large user pool with a small catalog , whereas the user pool and the catalog for Google Local Washington are both large , but its interaction data are much sparser. CG2R leads the metrics on both datasets, so its performance margin does not appear to depend on a particular user/catalog scale or data density. Two further properties of CG2R are expected to be helpful in production, though we have not evaluated them here. First, alongside the soft trust region of Section~ applied in this paper, the prior can also support a hard variant that restricts item scoring to the items in the top-$m$ clusters, which is an explicit $O $ shortlist that avoids scoring all $N$ items. Because the prior is assembled from $K$ cluster centroids rather than $N$ item-level statistics, the guidance stays compact as the catalog grows. Second, Equation~ handles a cold-start user directly: the history term vanishes and the prior falls back to the population-level affinity. We presented CG2R, a three-phase recommendation framework in which a learned hierarchy of semantic clusters supplies a contextual prior that is added directly to the item scoring rule. This allows the cluster structure learned during training to influence ranking at inference. Across the three benchmark datasets evaluated, CG2R outperforms classical, neural, and LLM-based baselines on every metric we report, and its performance margin is largest on the sparsest and largest catalog. A natural future direction is to replace the global cluster-guidance strength with a per-user or confidence-gated value, allowing the model to apply guidance more selectively. This study uses existing public benchmark datasets and involves no direct user intervention or new collection of personal data. As with other personalized recommenders, CG2R may reproduce biases in historical interactions, over-concentrate exposure within popular or semantically narrow clusters, and infer sensitive preferences. Deployments should therefore apply appropriate data-governance and privacy controls, audit ranking quality and exposure across relevant user and item groups, and provide mechanisms for diversity, user control, and monitoring of harmful or inappropriate recommendations.
