We address local citation recommendation , where the goal is to retrieve appropriate references for a given sentence. Web tools generally depend on the existing citation graph, which may bias results toward citation count or more recent publications. LCR research using full paper texts has been limited by the lack of publicly available data. We present a new dataset, the Local Citation Recommendation Corpus , containing 14{,}735 scientific statements derived from astrophysics review papers, each with one or more citation targets whose full text is accessible via API. Using this dataset we systematically compare retrieval methods and develop a citation recommendation system that avoids citation graph bias while achieving 8.0\ Scientific integrity has long required accurately citing prior research. Comprehensive citation, besides being expected for publication in most settings, connects research efforts across disparate teams and distant times. In recent years, scientific research in general has been subject to increased public scrutiny . This heightens the need for thorough citations that encourage verifiability, reproducibility, and transparency in research. However, identifying and verifying appropriate citations remains a time-consuming and error-prone process, leading many writers to rely on digital tools. Some search engines such as Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar specialize in ranking research publications, but prior work has observed that these tools favor citation counts and author metadata . These biases likely cause or exacerbate Matthew effects, where papers and authors become `popular for being popular' . The scientific community would benefit from improved and more transparent citation recommendation tools. This work focuses on local citation recommendation , the task of predicting appropriate citations for a given sentence from a research paper. LCR research has faced challenges making results difficult to compare, reproduce, or deploy. Open datasets for this task have lacked papers' full text, likely due to copyright constraints. Lacking this data, many systems rely on metadata and the citation graph rather than a paper's true content. LCR models trained on this metadata can quickly become obsolete as new authors and papers enter the body of scientific research. Vector stores present a logical solution to support a constantly expanding corpus and text retrieval . But while a query's embedding and its citation targets may likely have high similarity, so will embeddings of different sentences citing the same source. Therefore, vector similarity alone is insufficient to discriminate between true and false positives. LCR often resembles natural language inference , where a model attempts to predict if one text implies another. Many citing sentences $s$ cite a reference $r$ because $r$ supports the factuality of $s$, such as this one . But in practice, researchers cite papers for many reasons besides `$r$ implies $s$', including to clarify, contradict, or express a unique interpretation of earlier results . As a result, sentences can have many different relationships to the documents they cite, relationships that are not easily resolved by vector similarity, NLI scoring, or any one single method. LCR systems have typically contained the following components: Document representation Query representation Document ranking against a given query Optional filtering or reranking of top-ranked documents with much of the research effort focused on representation strategies. Early LCR research used various NLP techniques to construct representations. One approach computed TF-IDF vectors from passages citing a document as that document's representation . Context-based Collaborative Filtering represented queries as binary vectors computed by citation co-occurrence with other papers in the corpus. Probabilistic approaches include which adapted a translation model to produce citation likelihoods, and which contextualized queries with metadata including past citations and author information. In recent years, researchers have explored neural embeddings combined with different context augmentation strategies. Early neural approaches used LSTMs to create embeddings ; subsequent work augmented embeddings with title and abstract or modeled writer intent . Transformer-based approaches include , which uses as its embedding model, and also augments inputs with their titles and abstracts. CiteBART adapts BART to generate `` '' recommendations as a sequence-to-sequence task. Another branch of research focuses on graph-based methods. Some work models citation dynamics using temporal graph neural networks or estimates citation impact . fine-tunes BERT to encode queries and a graph neural network to encode the corpus. Multi-granularity preference alignment combines signals derived from the citation graph with intent modeling. These approaches leverage structural and temporal information in citation networks, but are fundamentally oriented toward modeling existing citation graph dynamics, rather than identifying semantically relevant papers for a specific sentence. A further limitation of prior LCR work lies in the datasets used for training and evaluation. Many studies rely on unreleased datasets or citation graphs derived from regular research papers, which may bias towards popularity, social networks, or other factors . Using such citation graphs as ground truth risks perpetuating these biases: when systems are evaluated against such data, it remains unclear whether they are identifying scientifically appropriate citations or simply learning to replicate existing popularity and network effects. This motivates alternative data sources, such as review papers, which reflect expert-curated citations. In the context of research writing, we define the local citation recommendation task as retrieving the most scientifically appropriate set of documents from a corpus that should be cited for an input query. We define the query $q$ as a sentence from a scientific manuscript, and possibly its context. Context can include additional data that helps clarify the sentence's meaning, such as preceding sentences, the paper's title, or abstract. A model performing this task aims to return the documents $D = \{d_1, , d_n\}$ which the input sentence from $q$ should cite. For the current task we assume $D$ is non-empty and consider only nontrivial sentences, which we define as sentences that require one or more scholarly citations to support or contextualize their claims. We leave distinguishing trivial and nontrivial statements as future work. Since the model maps queries to sets of documents, this task is a special case of document retrieval. As the task also primarily deals with natural language, it can also be thought of as a type of semantic similarity estimation. However, it is important to note that the relationship between a research statement and the set of documents it should cite is more constrained than semantic similarity alone. If paper $c$ establishes some scientific fact which is cited by both statements $s_1$ and $s_2$ in other papers, the semantic similarity between $s_1$ and $s_2$ is likely to be high and quite possibly higher than their similarity to $c$. Consider the case $s_1 =$ ``A superwind does not blow in low metallicity environments'' in one paper and $s_2 =$ ``Superwinds do not blow in lower metallicity environments'' in another paper, both of which should cite $c =$ Bowen primarily for the following passage: ``We estimate that for $Z< 0.1Z_{ }$ there will usually be too little dust to produce significant dynamical effects.'' Although both $s_1$ and $s_2$ should cite $c$, their near-identical wording makes it likely that $s_1$ and $s_2$ will exhibit higher cosine similarity to one another than either does to $c$. As a result, a retrieval system based purely on semantic similarity is likely to rank $s_2$ as more relevant to $s_1$ than the correct citation $c$, despite $c$ containing the supporting evidence. This illustrates why citation prediction cannot be reduced to sentence-level semantic similarity alone. A model performing the citation prediction task produces a ranked set of predicted documents $ = \{_1, , _k\}$, often referred to as the top-$k$ documents. To evaluate the model's success, we define metrics comparing the predicted $$ to the target set $D$, parameterized by $k$: : 1 if of the target documents appear in the top $k$ retrieved, 0 otherwise. : The proportion of target documents retrieved in the top $k$. For example, if a query has 4 target documents and at $k = 20$ two of those documents appear, then Recall@20 is 0.5. : Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank $k$ . This metric accounts for the position of relevant documents in the ranked list. Since it is common in vector-based retrieval for the same document to appear multiple times in a ranked search result due to chunking, we consider only the first occurrence of each document when computing NDCG. This is more aligned with the end goal of presenting the most likely citation candidates to the user. Then for a dataset of $N$ queries, the mean Hitrate@$k$, Recall@$k$, and NDCG@$k$ are the sum of the individual per-query metric values divided by $N$. In Section~ we refer to these means simply as Hitrate@$k$, Recall@$k$, or NDCG@$k$ when discussing results over an entire dataset. To build a citation prediction model, we constructed a dataset of queries containing sentences requiring citations, associated metadata, and their reference citations. We then formed a search corpus against which candidate citations are retrieved, enabling evaluation of each model's Hitrate@$k$ and Recall@$k$. We built a labeled query dataset from review papers and a retrieval corpus from general research papers, drawn from multiple scientific literature sources. In the experiments presented in this work, these datasets are derived from the Astrophysics Data System . We then segmented the corpus papers into chunks and embedded them in a vector database to support dense document retrieval. We describe our experimental setup and results in Section~. To build a labeled dataset, we curated 354 digitized review papers from a scientific literature corpus spanning astrophysics, earth science, and planetary science, provided by the Astrophysics Data System. Review papers synthesize prior research and are typically written with expert-curated citation practices, making them a suitable ground truth for LCR. From these review papers we extracted individual sentences containing inline citations and, where possible, resolved the inline citations to uniquely identifying document ID's . We implemented an automated extraction pipeline to scale this process. First it splits the paper's body on sentence boundaries. Each sentence is then passed to an LLM agent which rejects unusable sentences such as captions, reference section entries, or sentences containing severe OCR errors. For sentences passing this filter, the agent identifies and normalizes inline citations to an array of tuples. The extracted tuples are then passed to deterministic code that maps each citation to a uniquely identifying DOI using the paper's bibliography. Sentences with any inline citations that did not fully resolve to a DOI were excluded. For example, given the sentence: ``Kaiser et al compute the second moment within a variable aperture...'' the extraction stage would yield tuples . These tuples are resolved to their corresponding DOIs by searching through the review's references. The resolved papers form the target set $D$ for the query, and the inline citations are masked to form the query $q$. Applying this pipeline produced 14{,}735 samples such that: Sentences of captions, reference entries, and garbled OCR are excluded. Each sample sentence contains one or more inline citations. All inline citations are matched to their DOI. The dataset is provided as a JSONL file. Each line is a JSON object containing keys for the original sentence, citation target DOIs, the sentence with inline citations masked, the sentence position within the review paper, its source DOI, title, abstract, and additional fields.} All dataset queries constructed cite a combined 9{,}980 distinct research papers. In our experiments we expanded the corpus to 52{,}618 distinct research papers to make the retrieval task more challenging. For the experiments presented in this work, we obtained these papers' full-text contents from ADS to form our corpus. From these records, we reconstructed each paper by concatenating the title, abstract, and body fields, intentionally omitting all other fields such as authors, affiliations, and references, in order to avoid unnecessary bias. We implemented two forms of document representation: chunk-based and contribution-based representations. In the chunk-based representation, we used standard text-splitting techniques to divide the papers into a corpus of 460{,}801 chunks. For the contribution-based representation, we used a large language model to extract a paper's original findings, observations, and methodologies . These include prepending the title or abstract from the query's original paper, or the previous $n$ sentences for $n \{1, 2, 3, 5, 7\}$. We initially found that representing the input query sentence by prepending its previous three sentences, , was the best-performing strategy at many values of $k$, suggesting that this additional context helped the embedder better encode the underlying meaning of the query sentence. After some informal error analysis of the strategy, we found that some queries benefited from the additional context but others did not. This led to the development of a `mixed query representation' strategy. In mixed query representation, we represent the input query with multiple distinct representations. Specifically, we take the original sentence and the sentence appended to its previous two sentences, then embed them in parallel. Each vector is used to perform cosine similarity search against the database, retrieving the top $k // 2$ records. In other words, to retrieve a total of $k$ results, we retrieve half from the query's original text and the other half from the augmented query. We merge the two resulting sets by interleaving by rank: for chunks $ $ returned by the unmodified query and $ $ from the augmented query, the final ranking is $ $. This interleaving outperformed sorting by cosine similarity alone. We call this query representation strategy since it combines the query sentence as-is with the query sentence plus its previous two sentences. This strategy significantly outperformed the baseline and all other query representations at all values of $k$. One possible explanation is that, while additional context can help disambiguate certain citation intents, it may also introduce topic drift. In review papers, preceding sentences often summarize broader background or adjacent results that are only loosely related to the specific claim being cited. As a result, uniformly expanding all queries with surrounding context may dilute the semantic signal most relevant for citation selection. The mixed query representation may mitigate this trade-off by retaining both a high-precision signal and a higher-recall signal . We hope this encourages more work that focuses on the content of scientific statements rather than authorship or citation counts. We also introduce an effective context management technique, , which performs citation prediction significantly better than baseline vector similarity. This is consistent with classic IR research showing multiple query representatives can improve retrieval . Our strategy can be viewed as an instance of this principle in a dense retrieval setting, using natural context rather than generated query variants. Furthermore, we identify as a top-performing embedding model for the citation prediction task, and we identify a query representation and rank fusion strategy that collectively improve Hitrate@25 by 7.5\ above the baseline approach. Our experiments suggest that LLMs specifically trained for embedding tended to outperform LLMs fine-tuned on astrophysics text. To represent documents in the corpus, simple chunking outperformed the summarization methods attempted. We also show that expanding the query with additional context produced limited results when used as the only input. However, when used in parallel with the original query, merged results significantly improved. Finally, our reranking experiments found that rank fusion between BM25 and the raw semantic similarity ranking achieved better results than reranking by BM25 or other methods. We find that embedding models outperformed all other models fine-tuned on astrophysics data. This may be due to 's contrastive loss function, which enables it to learn high-quality document embeddings. Fine-tuning astrophysics-specific models with a contrastive loss, or further fine-tuning on domain-specific data, may improve performance by combining the strengths of both approaches. The majority of our model's improvement comes from the mixed query representation strategy. Future work may explore improved combinations of query representations and evaluate whether these techniques generalize to other scientific domains. *{Limitations} Our dataset excludes queries with significant amounts of math notation since the digitized text often fails to accurately represent the original expressions. Short of re-scanning the corpus with more powerful OCR, additional work is required to obtain accurate representations or address inaccurately digitized text.
