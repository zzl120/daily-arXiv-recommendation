DCN and DCNv2 efficiently automate polynomial feature crossing. However, their cross layers construct interactions using only non-negative integer powers of the input features, with the maximum degree controlled by network depth. Consequently, they cannot directly represent the reciprocal terms required for ratios, rates, and normalized counts unless such features are explicitly provided as inputs. We introduce DCDN, a Deep Cross-Division Network that augments a DCNv2-style cross layer with a division branch. DCDN jointly learns product, ratio, and mixed product–ratio interactions. We characterize the representable interactions as multivariate Laurent polynomials and show that a $K$-layer DCDN generates Laurent monomials of $ _1$-order up to $K+1$, with signed degree between $-K$ and $K+1$. We evaluate DCDN against a production-optimized neural-ranking baseline built around DCNv2 and supplied with manually engineered ratio features. Large-scale ranking systems rarely optimize predictive quality in isolation. A ranker scores many candidates under a strict latency budget and often at very high request volume. A model that improves an offline metric can therefore be unattractive in production if it reduces throughput, increases tail latency, or requires substantially more resources. In our production stack, model application accounts for approximately $20\ Part of this trade-off comes from the feature set itself. Production rankers commonly augment user, item, and context signals with derived statistics that encode domain knowledge. Ratio features are a familiar example: click-through rate normalizes clicks by impressions, and analogous features normalize other engagement counts by exposure or opportunity. Such features can be highly predictive because they expose a useful relation explicitly, but they also widen the input and increase the work done by downstream interaction layers. We distinguish a base feature set $B$ from a set $R$ of engineered ratio features whose numerator and denominator operands remain available in $B$. Removing $R$ makes the representation cheaper, but can discard useful signal. We study whether some of that signal can instead be recovered through the interaction operator. DCDN extends the matrix CrossNet layer of DCNv2~ with a reciprocal crossing anchored at the original input. The additional branch makes relations between retained operands easy to express while preserving the surrounding ranking architecture. The design is intentionally specialized: it targets feature regimes in which ratio-like interactions matter. AFN~ and EulerNet~ already support negative or non-integer orders through more general parameterizations. The practical question is whether a direct reciprocal bias can provide a better cost--quality trade-off for ratio-like interactions in ranking data. We examine that question from four angles. A controlled quotient task first tests what the reciprocal parameterization actually learns. We then evaluate DCDN on Yambda, KuaiRec, KuaiRand, and VK-LSVD against a broad set of feature-interaction models using task-appropriate ranking metrics. Separate latency and capacity-matched comparisons distinguish accuracy, model size, and measured inference cost. Finally, we replace the DCNv2 block in a production multi-target short-video ranker and evaluate both online user metrics and serving performance. Our contributions are: We introduce a reciprocal CrossNet interaction and characterize the exact Laurent-monomial support of a $K$-layer DCDN stack, including tight signed-degree bounds from $-K$ to $K+1$. We evaluate DCDN on a controlled quotient task and four public recommendation tasks, comparing it with DCNv2 and representative interaction models while separating full-feature-view, capacity-matched, and measured-latency comparisons. We deploy DCDN in a large-scale short-video ranker, substantially reducing engineered ratio features while improving both user-facing and serving metrics. Feature interaction modeling is central to CTR prediction and ranking. Factorization Machines model pairwise interactions with shared latent factors~; DeepFM combines factorization with a deep component~; xDeepFM constructs bounded high-order interactions with a Compressed Interaction Network~; and AutoInt learns interactions with self-attention~. Wukong scales explicit interaction capacity through stacked factorization-style blocks~. These models span a wide range of interaction families and serving costs, which makes them useful references for evaluating whether a more specialized operator can occupy a competitive quality--efficiency regime. DCN~ and DCNv2~ are the direct architectural predecessors of DCDN. Their cross layers repeatedly combine a learned projection of the current representation with the original input, yielding bounded-order multiplicative interactions in a form that has proved practical at web scale. Later work has modified this family in several directions: FCN increases the growth rate of explicit high-order interactions~, DCN$^2$ studies explicit cross layers together with implicit collision effects~, and recent efficiency-oriented variants reduce or restructure the cross computation~. DCDN takes a different route: it keeps the CrossNet recursion but adds a reciprocal anchor, so normalized relations can be formed directly from retained operands. AFN learns adaptive feature orders after a logarithmic transformation, allowing negative and fractional exponents~; AFN+ combines this component with a deep predictor. EulerNet uses Euler's formula to support real-valued interaction orders without the same non-negativity restriction on the original representation~. These methods establish that negative powers and division-like dependencies are already within the scope of neural feature-interaction models. They also provide an important contrast to DCDN: AFN and EulerNet are substantially more general, whereas DCDN deliberately hard-codes only the reciprocal direction. This distinction motivates our controlled quotient experiment: beyond asking whether a ratio is representable, we test whether the corresponding dependency is easy to learn from its operands. A complementary line of work reduces serving cost by changing which features are retained or how they are processed. AdaFS adaptively emphasizes predictive features~, while OptFS jointly considers feature selection and interaction modeling to remove unnecessary computation~. Rank-aware cross-network decomposition targets the cost of the interaction operator itself~. Our setting connects these two concerns: removing engineered ratios reduces input and interaction cost, but may lower ranking quality; reciprocal crossing attempts to recover part of that signal from the operands that remain, shifting the burden from the feature pipeline to the model's inductive bias. The production system follows a candidate-generation--ranking architecture. The final ranker consumes heterogeneous inputs: counter-like user, item, and context statistics; predictions produced by upstream models; and sparse categorical features. The control feature pipeline also materializes a sizeable family of manually engineered ratio features derived from these signals, including normalized counters and ratios involving upstream-model scores. These features are useful to the existing ranker but increase both feature-pipeline complexity and the width of the interaction stage. Continuous and count-like inputs are encoded with piecewise-linear encoding~, while sparse categorical features are represented through embedding lookup. The deployed treatment keeps the underlying operands and upstream-model predictions but removes the targeted engineered ratios. The encoded representation is then passed through a single DCDN layer, followed by ~. The two interaction stages play different roles: DCDN provides a lightweight structured bias toward product/ratio relations, whereas 's heterogeneous self-attention captures more general cross-feature dependencies. The resulting representation feeds task-specific residual towers for the production objectives, including watch-time distribution modeling~, likes, shares, and clicks to open fullscreen. A sequential user transformer supplies a user-history embedding to the task predictors. The downstream calibration and utility-fusion logic is identical in control and treatment; these components are therefore not part of the DCDN intervention. RQ1 helps explain why the specialized operator can work. Under a log-uniform training distribution spanning the multi-scale range of counter features, DCDN learns the target quotient to near-zero error across reference, rescaling, and denominator-shift regimes and is dramatically more accurate than DCNv2. This complements the Laurent-support theorem: the theory establishes representability, while the controlled experiment shows learnability in the numerical range of interest. The experiment does not establish arbitrary out-of-distribution arithmetic, since the reported regimes lie within the training support. Likewise, the clipped reciprocal used in practice differs from the exact Laurent operator outside its unclipped range. Several limitations qualify these results. First, the controlled quotient experiment does not make DCDN uniformly best even in the setting it targets: under common rescaling, AutoInt+ and EulerNet attain lower NMSE, while DCDN is third-best. This suggests that the reciprocal bias makes quotient-like dependencies easy to learn, but does not guarantee the best optimization behavior for every transformation or architecture. Second, latency depends on kernels, hardware, and batching.
