Bheri Technology Foundation, India} {Dh\={a}r\={a}} {Dhara Bench: An Evidence-Oriented Retrieval Benchmark for Indian Knowledge Systems} {Dhara: Evidence-Oriented Retrieval for Indian Knowledge Systems} Retrieving evidence from Indian Knowledge Systems is complicated by transliteration variants, ambiguous names, and relations distributed across passages. We present an ontology-guided knowledge graph pipeline that links typed entities and relations extracted with Qwen3-32B to original textual evidence. Retrieval combines sense-aware grounding, bounded graph traversal, lexical anchors, relationship-vector search, and passage reranking. We evaluate KG alone, single-vector retrieval , and KG unions with SV and Entity Notes on 320 questions from Bench, matching the per-question candidate count across internal systems under BGE and Qwen rerankers. Standalone KG has the highest Precision@5 under both rerankers and the highest MRR@20 in Counting, Composite, and Multi-hop among the evaluated internal configurations. With Qwen, KG achieves Multi-hop MRR@20 of 0.8722, compared with 0.7622 for the fully reranked KG + SV union. The KG + SV + Entity Notes union instead provides the highest overall Recall@20 , compared with 0.4347 for KG. On the 15 Multi-hop questions, Qwen KG achieves complete-evidence MRR@20 of 0.4651 versus 0.3372 for SV, while both full unions complete ten questions within twenty results compared with nine for KG. Gemini 3.7 Flash, evaluated through manually reviewed source mappings, remains stronger on overall MRR and nDCG, while internal systems have category-specific advantages. All comparisons are descriptive. A separate deployment timing study measures mean retrieval times of 20.374 s for KG and 0.646 s for SV. The findings support selecting KG or its unions according to the required balance of early relevance, evidence completeness, and retrieval cost. } Indian Knowledge Systems and heritage encompass a continuing civilizational tradition of the Indian subcontinent, developed over millennia and connecting philosophy, scientific inquiry, ethics, and everyday life . This integrated perspective provides a conceptual foundation for understanding diverse knowledge practices rather than treating them as isolated disciplines. Their textual sources include narrative literature, philosophy, medicine, architecture, performance theory, and normative texts. Evidence retrieval across these traditions involves several distinct difficulties. A name can appear in multiple transliterations or through an epithet; the same surface form can refer to different entities; and a question can require connecting an entity, an action, and a contextual qualification rather than locating a passage with similar wording. Domain-specific terms also resist a uniform interpretation across genres. A useful retrieval system must identify relevant passages while retaining enough structure to distinguish these cases. Dense retrieval provides a strong mechanism for discovering semantic similarity. Multi-vector representations can preserve more local matching information than a single pooled vector, as exemplified by late-interaction retrieval . However, similarity alone does not explicitly encode whether an entity performs an action, is the object of that action, belongs to a particular lineage, or participates in a relation in a specific textual context. A passage mentioning two entities is not necessarily evidence for a relation between them. Likewise, a highly similar passage may concern a different sense of a shared name. These are representational differences, not claims that dense retrieval is incapable of answering relational questions. We investigate a complementary approach: construct a typed, source-linked graph and use it to discover evidence candidates before reranking the original text. The graph is an intermediate retrieval representation, not a substitute for textual evidence or an authoritative store of automatically verified facts. Its value lies in making entities, relations, and provenance available as explicit retrieval signals. Its weaknesses, including extraction omissions and incorrect merges, remain relevant to the final ranking. This work is the third component of our research programme. The first, ~, establishes an expert-curated ground-truth dataset. The second, ~, studies evidence-retrieval methodologies including single-vector, multi-vector, and Entity Notes retrieval. The present paper focuses on KG construction and KG-based retrieval within that unified system. Multi-vector retrieval, Entity Notes retrieval and remapping, KG retrieval, and their combinations are all components of our system; Gemini is used only as an external comparison system. Our contributions are fourfold. First, we describe a genre-conditioned extraction pipeline that combines explicit ontology specifications with Qwen3-32B and source-linked graph storage. Second, we develop a KG evidence-retrieval procedure integrating sense-aware resolution, local graph structure, lexical anchors, and relationship-vector search. Third, we compare KG alone, SV alone, and their unions with mapped Entity Notes under matched per-question candidate budgets and two rerankers, including a comparison of full reranking and intersection pinning. Fourth, we assess category-specific relevance and complete-evidence retrieval using the metrics of Bench~, compare against the reviewed Gemini reference from ~, and characterize retrieval and reranking time in a separate deployment study. The central finding is a distinction between early relevance and evidence coverage. Standalone KG achieves the highest MRR@20 in Counting, Composite, and Multi-hop among the evaluated internal configurations under both rerankers, whereas unions generally recover more annotated evidence within twenty results. With Qwen, the KG + SV + EN union has the highest overall MRR@20, Recall@20, and nDCG@10, while KG retains the highest Precision@5. Complete-evidence scoring similarly distinguishes earlier completion from completing more questions: Qwen KG has the highest Multi-hop CE-MRR@20 among the internal configurations, but both full unions complete ten of fifteen questions by rank twenty compared with nine for KG. These descriptive findings motivate choosing the retrieval configuration according to the evidence objective and expected question mixture. Retrieval-augmented generation separates access to external evidence from the parameters of a language model . Our task lies at the evidence-retrieval stage: the output is an ordered list of original corpus chunks. We do not evaluate generated answers, answer faithfulness, or end-to-end conversational quality. This distinction matters because an improvement in evidence recall does not by itself demonstrate an improvement in generated responses. Multi-vector retrieval models, including ColBERT, retain token-level representations and aggregate fine-grained query--document interactions . BGE-M3 supports multilingual and multiple retrieval representations . These approaches motivate the use of dense retrieval as a complementary evidence channel. The evaluated SV and EN components use the project's established single-vector and Entity Notes-remapped retrieval procedures; we do not equate those implementations with ColBERT or introduce a new dense architecture. Graph-augmented retrieval provides explicit access to entities and their connections. GraphRAG develops a graph-based approach to query-focused summarization with community-level information , while LightRAG integrates graph structure into retrieval-augmented generation . Our emphasis is narrower: ontology-conditioned extraction for heterogeneous IKS texts, ambiguity-sensitive entity grounding, and projection back to the original chunk identifiers used by expert annotations. We do not benchmark directly against GraphRAG or LightRAG, and therefore make no empirical superiority claim over those systems. The language-model components serve different purposes. Qwen3-32B supplies structured extraction ; BGE-M3 supplies embedding representations ; and BGE-reranker-v2-m3 or Qwen3-Reranker-4B orders candidate passages in the current comparison . Reciprocal rank fusion offers a score-scale-independent way to combine ranked evidence lists . Our contribution is the domain-grounded integration of these components and its analysis, rather than a new foundation model or rank-fusion rule. The registry includes the families in Table~. The extraction pipeline consumes structured text records carrying source references. It removes designated supplementary markers, including appended cross-concept and footnote sections, before segmentation. A recursive splitter constructs extraction chunks with a target size of 3,000 characters and an overlap of 250 characters, using paragraph and sentence boundaries where possible. These are character limits, not token limits. Overlap preserves local context but can produce repeated entity mentions and relations, which are subsequently normalized. Source, chunk, and entity objects receive internal identifiers. Extraction retains source references and chunk associations so that downstream processing can recover evidence. Checkpoints are written at record boundaries, and raw model responses can be retained in JSONL traces. These mechanisms support recovery and inspection; they do not constitute semantic validation of extracted facts. We used Qwen3-32B to extract graph entities and relations, with eight concurrent second-pass requests. Both passes use temperature zero, disable model thinking, and permit up to 8,192 output tokens. The first pass uses top-$p$ 0.85, top-$k$ 10, and repetition penalty 1.15; the relation pass uses top-$p$ 0.6, top-$k$ 1, and repetition penalty 1.0. The optional named-entity recognition stage was disabled. In the first pass, the model receives the extraction chunk and the selected ontology. It identifies named entities and domain-relevant concepts, assigns a permitted entity label, and generates a short summary for each entity. Naming instructions retain meaningful diacritics, remove honorifics where appropriate, and discourage inconsistent surface forms. A label and summary provide a local semantic description that can later support entity disambiguation. In the second pass, extraction is performed for each entity using the full extraction chunk, the first-pass entity record, the extracted entity inventory, and the domain ontology. Candidate relation targets are restricted to the supplied entity names, and relation labels follow the ontology's naming conventions. The prompt distinguishes explicit assertions from co-occurrence, preserves semantic direction, permits an empty relation list, and requests supporting text. Domain guardrails prohibit unsupported kinship, causal, therapeutic, legal, or ritual relations where applicable. They also discourage conflating intended actions with completed actions and collapsing mediated interactions into unsupported binary claims. Concurrency changes throughput rather than the logical inputs to each entity-level request. The response can include textual details and auxiliary annotations, but not all response fields are persisted by graph upload. Evidence strings are model-selected spans and were not independently verified as quotations. Although the relation pass receives the source chunk, graph extraction can still omit qualifications, select an incorrect direction or target, or generate an unsupported edge. The ontology is enforced through instructions and downstream processing, not through a formally constrained decoder. JSON handling attempts direct parsing and limited recovery of incomplete responses. Invalid or incomplete entity records may be skipped. Such failures can reduce graph coverage even when the source text contains relevant information. Entity consolidation combines normalized names with semantic similarity. The entity-merging procedure uses MiniLM-L6-v2 name embeddings and a cosine threshold of 0.88, together with normalized-name agreement or a stricter similarity condition of 0.95. A summary-based sense guard can reject incompatible merges at a threshold of 0.55. Representative entities are chosen through the merge procedure, labels are consolidated, and relation synonyms are normalized. These heuristics reduce fragmentation, but neither name similarity nor summary similarity establishes identity with certainty. Transliteration and alias utilities generate capped sets of spelling variants, while selected epithets supply additional lexical links. An optional cross-book linker creates non-destructive links using folded names, label agreement, and BGE-based summary similarity. We do not attribute the reported retrieval gains specifically to this optional stage. Neo4j stores source nodes, extraction chunks, typed entities, and semantic relations. We distinguish these construction stages from the evaluation of ranked source passages. {!}{} The retrieval procedure first interprets a question into entity mentions, canonical forms, possible epithets, sense hints, relation hints, answer type, and textual scope. Structured parsing limits the number of primary entity mentions to three, with heuristic fallback when parsing fails. This representation allows the resolver to combine lexical identity with contextual evidence instead of selecting an entity by name alone. Candidate entities are collected from exact names and aliases, transliteration variants, full-text lookup, and an entity-vector index. The vector channel retrieves up to eight candidates above a cosine threshold of 0.4; alias and equivalence links can expand the pool. The combined resolver pool is capped at twenty entities. Exact matching is valuable for distinctive names, while semantic and alias channels help when the question uses an alternate form or a descriptive mention. Candidates are scored using name agreement, type compatibility, summary similarity, textual scope, and relation-neighborhood compatibility, with penalties for mixed senses and known homograph confusions. For grounded entities, retrieval collects either relation-compatible edges or non-structural neighboring edges. Candidate triples and their available evidence details are compared semantically with the interpreted question. The local procedure uses bounded edge counts and retains a small fallback set when similarity filtering would remove all evidence. When multiple entity groups are present, the robust path channel searches for direct connections and paths of exactly two hops between bounded candidate sets. Source and chunk nodes are excluded as semantic intermediate nodes. The channel caps returned paths and evidence edges; it is not an unrestricted graph-reasoning engine. Such traversal can expose passages linked by a shared entity even when no single passage has a strong dense match to the whole question. Nevertheless, connecting two graph entities does not establish that the resulting path answers the question. Direction is represented in extracted relations and used by path processing, but not every retrieval channel applies the parsed direction constraint consistently. The reranker therefore remains important for distinguishing an evidential passage from a merely related neighborhood result. We do not interpret every retrieved edge as a logically entailed answer. The robust procedure also collects source chunks associated with selected entities and full-text matches for focused query terms. These lexical anchors are useful when graph extraction has captured an entity but omitted the relation needed by the question. Additional keyword rules identify list-oriented and relation-oriented evidence, with capped full-text candidate lists. These rules use question text, not the ground-truth category label, at retrieval time. Relationship-vector search provides a second recovery route when entity grounding or local traversal is incomplete. Stored relationship embeddings use BGE-M3 with 1,024 dimensions. The cached relationship matrix contains 319,463 relationship vectors; this is an index population, not a count of all edges in the database. Search computes query--matrix similarities directly rather than using an approximate nearest-neighbor index. It retrieves the top fifty relationship rows and maps them to at most twenty-four distinct original chunk identifiers. The fixed-budget results distinguish candidate quality from candidate quantity. KG's high MRR and Precision@5 indicate that its retrieved passages often place annotated, question-relevant evidence near the beginning of the list. Relations, entity neighborhoods, and source-linked provenance provide plausible mechanisms for exposing evidence that is distributed across passages or expressed through different surface forms. This interpretation is consistent with the category results, although the experiment does not isolate the causal contribution of traversal, extraction, normalization, or relationship-vector search. The metrics measure relevance against the annotated evidence sets. KG is therefore an appropriate candidate when the application prioritizes the first useful source or a short, precise evidence list. It is not uniformly the best early-ranking system: Qwen KG + SV + EN slightly exceeds standalone KG in overall MRR and nDCG. The architecture and reranker must be evaluated together, rather than treating a graph as sufficient to guarantee superior ranking. The strongest first-hit case for standalone KG arises in Counting, Composite, and Multi-hop questions, where it has higher MRR@20 than SV and either full union under both rerankers. Multi-hop provides the clearest structural motivation: a useful evidence set can require connections across entities and source passages. Composite questions aggregate several evidence requirements, while counting questions require identifying members of a relevant set. The graph's explicit organization is useful for these tasks, but a high MRR alone does not establish that every required member or supporting passage was recovered. The category-specific benefits must consequently be weighed against completeness. Qwen unions provide higher Recall@20 than KG in all three focal categories, and the EN union improves Counting nDCG despite its lower MRR. A workload emphasizing rapid identification of a relevant relational source can justify KG; an exhaustive counting or multi-requirement workflow may instead favor a union. Composite questions are evaluated as complete multi-requirement questions rather than through an interactive dialogue with retrieval after each turn. No claim of multi-turn conversational competence follows from chunk-level retrieval scores. The opposite precision--recall trends provide a practical choice between a concentrated evidence list and broader annotated coverage. Standalone KG has the highest overall Precision@5 under both rerankers, whereas the Qwen EN union raises Recall@20 from 0.4347 to 0.5134. For a reader or generator operating with a small context window, early precision and MRR may be the primary objectives. For evidence synthesis, audit, or exhaustive collection, recall and the completeness of supporting sets may matter more. Broader coverage here means more annotated relevant passages within the evaluation cutoff, not a larger candidate-count allowance: all primary internal systems retain exactly the original per-question KG budget. A service can omit KG and use SV when its category-specific quality and latency are sufficient, but SV alone does not achieve the strongest overall recall in this experiment. The largest overall recall comes from combining KG, SV, and mapped EN evidence. The complete-evidence results in Section~ make this distinction concrete: Qwen KG completes Multi-hop evidence earlier on average, whereas the EN union completes ten rather than nine of the fifteen questions within twenty results. Retrieved-set completeness and downstream answer correctness remain separate evaluation requirements. The GPU study establishes a substantial practical latency trade-off. KG retrieval averaged 20.374 s versus 0.646 s for SV, an additional 19.728 s, or approximately twenty seconds per query. With passage reranking included as separately measured stage sums, the KG--SV gap remains approximately twenty seconds under either BGE or Qwen. Relational and aggregation gains must therefore justify a material response-time premium; the measured advantage on Multi-hop does not make KG the appropriate default for every query. Hardware capacity is part of this decision. Graph generation with Qwen3-32B incurs offline extraction and maintenance work, while online KG processing and reranking require additional model residency and accelerator capacity. In the sampled deployment, KG's per-domain embedding-model caches occupied approximately 22.6 GiB of A100 memory after initialization. This is an implementation-specific observed footprint rather than a minimum requirement. The isolated KG instance was stopped after retrieval to release that cache before scoring; simultaneous serving and reranking would require its own capacity and concurrency assessment. A deployment should therefore select among SV, KG, and unions according to the available hardware, expected question mixture, and affordable latency. SV is a strong starting point for strict interactive response limits; KG is more defensible when relational quality gains outweigh an approximately twenty-second retrieval premium on representative questions. Category-aware routing is a plausible way to concentrate that cost on suitable requests, but it was not evaluated here and must not rely on ground-truth category labels at inference time. The measured gap is a property of these shared-machine implementations, not a hardware-independent constant or a validated routing threshold. Qwen generally improves quality across the internal systems, with local BGE exceptions that can matter for a particular category or metric. Its approximately 7.3-fold reranking-time increase should be interpreted in absolute as well as relative terms: moving from BGE to Qwen adds about 2.2 s per question for either source pool. This is modest relative to KG retrieval, which contributes 88.8\ BGE is attractive when its category-level quality is adequate and the latency or memory budget is restrictive; Qwen is attractive when its relevance gains justify the additional scoring work. These comparisons apply to the documented precision, batch sizes, and 1,024-token passage cap. Reranking time is more strongly correlated with capped document-token volume than with candidate count, so an equal-count budget should be accompanied by token-volume and model-specific measurements. Construction, retrieval, and reranking should be budgeted separately rather than attributing the entire runtime difference to the scorer. The external comparison supports category- and metric-specific claims rather than a universal superiority claim. Qwen KG is stronger than the archived Gemini reference on all four reported metrics in Lexical Understanding, Composite, False Premise, and Mathematical/Logical/Computational questions. Multi-hop is closely matched on MRR and precision, with a KG recall advantage and a small Gemini nDCG advantage. These results indicate useful internal retrieval strengths, while the large Verse/Textual gap and Gemini's List/Set and Counting ranking advantages identify important weaknesses. Unions frequently improve coverage against the external reference without reversing every ordering disadvantage. Both Qwen full unions exceed Gemini's Recall@20 in ten categories, yet Gemini retains the highest overall MRR and nDCG. Consequently, the intended evidence objective and category distribution should determine which comparison is decisive. Gemini's archived output depth and retrieval budget differ from the internal candidate budgets, and its runtime was not measured in the paired timing study. Neither an equal-compute superiority claim nor a Gemini latency comparison is supported. The merging-policy comparison holds candidate membership fixed and changes ordering, showing that source agreement has conditional value. Pinning intersection evidence improves MRR in Multi-hop and Mathematical questions under both models, but can sacrifice deeper recall or ranked-list quality. Full reranking is the stronger general default in the observed aggregate and consistently improves every displayed metric in Fact Retrieval and Lexical Understanding. Shared IDs can therefore identify useful evidence without guaranteeing that every shared passage belongs ahead of every exclusive passage. The two policies serve different hypotheses: pinning imposes a fixed agreement prior, while full reranking allows question-conditioned passage scores to override it. A category-specific policy could be investigated on held-out data, but the present category exceptions do not establish a validated selector. When the KG-reranked order is already available, the pinned policy reranks only the non-intersection remainder. However, this study does not provide a controlled timing comparison between the two merging policies; no policy-specific latency saving is claimed. The pipeline relies on model-generated entities and relations, prompt-based ontology adherence, and heuristic entity merging. Errors at any of these stages can produce plausible but unsupported graph connections. No separate expert-labeled triple-quality experiment is reported, and the evaluated construction run omitted optional relation validation. Retrieval gains therefore must not be recast as a measured improvement in graph factuality. Aliases and summary similarity help reconcile name variants but can conflate different senses. Conversely, conservative merging can fragment a single entity. Hand-authored ambiguity rules address selected cases rather than solving identity resolution generally. Endpoint-based relation merging and fallback chunk mapping can also lose or broaden evidence associations. Scoring original passages assesses candidate relevance after graph discovery but cannot recover absent evidence, certify extracted triples, or guarantee complete support. Quality comparisons use the exact 320-question cohort and frozen Dhara-Bench v3 ground truth, with per-question candidate counts matched across the primary internal systems. The benchmark contains 325 questions overall; the five absent KG predictions are excluded rather than imputed. Gemini has the same evaluation questions but an unmatched retrieval budget. Precision@5 retains denominator five, recall depends on the breadth of the annotated evidence sets, and binary nDCG does not distinguish degrees of relevance. Complete-evidence metrics use only the 34 group-annotated questions , all of which are evaluable. All fixed-budget, complete-evidence, and category-resolved comparisons are descriptive. The timing study uses a deliberately category-balanced 24-question sample rather than all 320 questions. Two passes expose repeat effects, but two unique questions per category do not establish stable category-specific latency estimates. GPU placement was explicit for both retrieval systems, while their serving stacks, device residency, database access, and background load differed. The reported totals sum separately measured retrieval and reranking stages; they do not include answer generation, concurrent serving, or a controlled end-to-end union request. The live databases were not frozen to the historical quality export. The retrieval heuristics were developed within the project benchmark. We do not report an independent held-out evaluation or a blinded annotation audit. The results therefore characterize the implemented pipeline against the recorded evidence sets, not label-independent or out-of-domain superiority. Equal candidate counts do not equalize token work, construction cost, or total compute, and the comparisons do not isolate each graph component causally. Complete-evidence retrieval is assessed separately from ordinary relevance; neither type of retrieval score alone establishes downstream answer faithfulness. Exact reconstruction of the evaluated graph requires the source texts, extraction and serving configurations, provenance mappings, and a database snapshot tied to the extraction run. The current deployment census and timing measurements do not establish an identical historical graph snapshot. This limits exact reconstruction and the interpretation of the timing results as a quality--latency measurement for the historical evaluation.
