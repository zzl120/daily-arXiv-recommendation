Unlike traditional lexical approaches, dense retrieval represents queries and documents as vectors, allowing semantic matching beyond exact word matches. However, long documents remain challenging to represent . A single vector may provide a broad and imprecise representation of a document containing multiple topics, making relevant content harder to distinguish from unrelated information. One approach to this problem is to represent documents using smaller retrieval units, allowing the retriever to identify specific portions of a document that are relevant to a query. This problem is relevant in legal information retrieval , which applies IR techniques to legal documents. Legal documents differ from general-purpose text in several ways. They are often long, contain domain-specific terminology, and are highly structured, with relationships between their content and their organizational structure . Relevant information in a legal document may be concentrated in a single provision or section, while the remainder of the document may address different topics. Treating the whole document as a single retrieval unit can therefore result in an overly broad representation in which relevant information is diluted by unrelated content . The structure of legal documents provides an opportunity to address this problem. Structural components can be indexed separately, allowing retrieval models to match queries against more focused portions of the document. At the same time, smaller units may lose information needed to interpret their content. The choice of retrieval unit therefore involves a trade-off between representing localized information and preserving the context in which that information occurs. Legal documents are suitable for studying this trade-off because their structural organization is explicitly defined . Brazilian legal documents provide a useful setting for this investigation. Legislative proposals, laws, and other normative acts follow formal drafting rules that organize their content into explicit structural units . These units include articles and their subdivisions, as well as higher-level components such as sections and chapters. They provide natural boundaries for document segmentation, enabling retrieval representations based on the structure of legal acts. The Brazilian legal retrieval context also presents challenges for current retrieval approaches. A recent benchmark of five Brazilian legal datasets showed that lexical retrieval models remained competitive with and, in some cases, outperformed dense retrieval models . The largest gaps occurred on NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus, two collections of highly structured legal documents . In this paper, we investigate structural segmentation of Brazilian legal acts for document retrieval. We represent each document as a set of structural chunks and evaluate different representation strategies. During retrieval, the model matches queries with individual chunks, and the resulting scores are mapped back to the original documents for document-level ranking. We evaluate these strategies on NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus using both lexical and dense retrieval models. We address two research questions: . Does structural segmentation improve Brazilian legal document retrieval compared with whole-document indexing? . Which representation components contribute most to retrieval effectiveness? This work investigates the representation and retrieval of Brazilian legal acts. Its main contributions are as follows: We investigate structural segmentation as a document representation strategy, comparing it with whole-document indexing using both lexical and dense retrieval models; We evaluate different structural representations by varying the structural chunks that are indexed and the contextual information associated with them; We show that context-enriched structural representations improve dense retrieval over whole-document indexing, while a simplified representation based on core chunks and document metadata can achieve comparable or better results than the full hierarchical representation. Passage-based retrieval addresses the limitations of whole-document representations by splitting documents into smaller textual units and scoring them independently. The passage scores can then be aggregated to produce a document-level ranking. A document can thus receive a high score when one or more of its passages are considered relevant to the query. Early studies showed that passage-level retrieval can outperform document-level retrieval . Later work has extended this approach through language models, probabilistic passage scoring, and alternative strategies for aggregating passage scores into document-level rankings . In Legal IR, passage-based retrieval has also been explored. Rosso et al. applied it to treaties, patents, and contracts in their experiments to find relevant sections rather than full documents . Aumiller et al. study the trade-off between document-level and smaller representations using a collection of English Terms of Service documents . They argued that document-level representations may be too broad, whereas sentence- or paragraph-level representations may omit the contextual information needed to understand a topic. They proposed structural text segmentation based on topical coherence, identifying coherent sections that may span multiple paragraphs. Similarly, Malik et al. investigated semantic segmentation of legal documents using rhetorical roles . Their corpus consists of English-language documents from the Indian legal system, and their method identifies semantically coherent units corresponding to functions such as facts, arguments, issues, precedents, and rulings. Nguyen et al. investigated the retrieval of sets of legal articles related to a query or its negation in Japanese statute law, using individual legal articles as retrieval units rather than entire legal documents . Another approach uses structural information that is already present in legal documents. Structure-aware retrieval methods consider organizational or functional information to represent legal documents and their components. SAILER, for example, incorporates the structure of legal case documents into a pre-trained language model for legal case retrieval . The authors argued that legal cases contain logical structures and key legal elements that are important for determining relevance, and they designed the model to exploit relationships between these structural components. Yang et al. also incorporated structured information into legal case retrieval, combining textual representations with structured factual representations based on event chains and participant roles . These studies show that document structure can provide information about how different parts of a legal document should be represented. The importance of document structure is especially clear in legal acts that follow formal drafting conventions. In such documents, structure is part of how information is organized. Brazilian legal acts provide an example. Complementary Law No. 95/1998 establishes drafting rules for Brazilian laws, including rules concerning their organization and hierarchical structure . Other normative documents, including regulations, resolutions, ordinances, and legislative proposals, are organized according to similar principles . This formal organization provides explicit boundaries that may be used for document segmentation and retrieval. Legal IR has developed around a range of specialized tasks and benchmarks, with much of the established literature focusing on judicial case retrieval and legal datasets from a limited number of jurisdictions. More recently, interest in Legal IR for Brazilian Portuguese has grown, with the development of publicly available datasets, retrieval pipelines, and benchmarking initiatives . These resources have enabled systematic comparisons of retrieval methods across different collections. However, relatively little work has considered the internal organization of Brazilian legal documents for retrieval . In the Brazilian context, Lima proposed a multi-layered representation for Brazilian legal acts . Originally developed for Retrieval-Augmented Generation applications, it exploits their hierarchical organization to represent each document at different structural levels. In this work, we investigate its use for Legal IR, where the goal is to retrieve the source document rather than individual chunks. We also conduct ablation studies to evaluate simplified representations derived from Lima's approach by varying which chunks are indexed and which contextual information is included with them. The methodology is organized into five subsections. First, we describe the datasets used in the experiments. Second, we segment the documents according to their structural organization, representing each document as a collection of chunks. Third, we consider four document representation strategies based on these chunk collections, varying which chunks are indexed and what contextual information is included with them. Fourth, we index the chunks according to each representation strategy and retrieve them using lexical and dense retrieval models, mapping chunk scores to document scores. Finally, we evaluate the resulting document rankings using nDCG@10 and MRR against whole-document baselines. Figure shows an overview of the methodology. {fig1_pipeline_methodology.png} We conduct the experiments on two public Brazilian legal test collections: NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus . NormasTCU contains 14,469 normative acts from the Brazilian Federal Court of Accounts. Its 46 queries are based on real user searches and mostly consist of short keywords. Query--document pairs were assessed by a panel of four domain experts using three relevance levels: irrelevant, partially relevant, or relevant Ulysses-RFCorpus contains 105,669 legislative proposals from the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies. Each proposal also contains a justification section in which the deputy explains the motivation for the proposal, which is not present in the documents in NormasTCU. Its 693 queries correspond to requests made by deputies for legislative proposals. The relevance judgments were provided by 54 domain experts, with each query and its associated documents assessed by a single expert using three relevance levels: irrelevant, partially relevant, or relevant. Both collections consist of raw text that generally follows the official legal drafting standards discussed in Section~ . Brazilian legal acts follow drafting rules that organize their content into explicit structural units. In these documents, articles are the main units of the normative text. Each article consists of a lead paragraph and optional paragraphs that complement it. Each lead paragraph or paragraph may contain one or more levels of enumeration . Articles can be organized into higher-level structural units, such as subsections , sections , chapters , titles , books , and parts . Together, these elements organize the main body of the normative document . Documents also have a title identifying their type, number, date of enactment, and a summary . They may also contain other structural elements, such as tables, annexes, or a justification of the proposal . In this work, we adopt the segmentation proposed by Lima , which represents each document as a multi-layered collection of chunks at the following levels: Document level: the whole text; Document component level: the main normative text and non-normative components, such as tables, annexes, and the justification of legislative proposals; Basic unit hierarchy level: each higher-level grouping of articles ; Basic unit level: each article, including all of its subdivisions; Basic unit component level: each lead paragraph and each paragraph within an article; and Enumeration level: each individual enumerated item within a lead paragraph or paragraph. Each chunk can be enriched with contextual information, including the document title, summary, and titles of all enclosing structural units . To evaluate structural segmentation, we focus on segmentation levels from the document component level to the enumeration level. Document-level retrieval has already been evaluated in a public benchmark and is used as a baseline for comparison . The characteristics of Ulysses-RFCorpus may help explain this difference. Its whole-document baselines are already strong, leaving less room for improvement through segmentation. This may be related to both the documents and the queries. On the document side, each legislative proposal includes a justification in which the deputy describes the problem to be solved and the proposed solution. This section semantically enriches the document and mitigates the vocabulary mismatch problem of lexical retrieval. On the query side, deputies use natural-language descriptions of the intended legislative proposal. In contrast, NormasTCU has no justification sections and usually has short keyword queries whose terms may not appear in the normative text. The gains on Ulysses-RFCorpus are also limited by the documents that do not follow the expected hierarchical structure. Approximately one-third of the collection cannot be segmented and is therefore represented as a single indexed unit. For these documents, the segmented indexes behave like whole-document indexing, reducing the potential gains from segmentation on this collection. The results allow us to answer the proposed research questions: Does structural segmentation improve Brazilian legal document retrieval compared with whole-document indexing? For dense retrievers, the three representations that include contextual information outperform whole-document indexing, whereas does not. Structural segmentation, however, does not consistently benefit BM25. Which representation components contribute most to retrieval effectiveness? For dense retrievers, representations based on core chunks with contextual enrichment use substantially fewer indexed units than while achieving comparable or better retrieval results in most cases. We found no clear evidence that full hierarchical context outperforms core chunks enriched with document metadata . We therefore recommend the simpler representation. The results suggest that legal retrieval systems can use the structure of legal acts without indexing all levels of their hierarchy. The representation achieved results comparable to or better than while indexing substantially fewer units. They also show that contextual information is important, as removing document metadata consistently reduced retrieval effectiveness. Thus, practical implementations can focus on core structural units, such as articles, enriched with concise document-level metadata. This study has four main limitations. First, we evaluated only two Brazilian legal test collections, NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus. They are the only available datasets that match the characteristics required by this study and cover different types of legal documents and query formulations, but they do not represent the full diversity of Brazilian legal documents. Moreover, about one-third of the Ulysses-RFCorpus documents do not follow the expected structure and therefore cannot be represented using the proposed hierarchical segmentation. Second, we evaluated BM25 and three dense embedding models using max-score aggregation only. Our findings may not generalize to other retrieval models or chunk aggregation methods. Third, we tested four representation strategies based on Lima . Although our experiment show the effects of core chunks and contextual enrichment, we dit not test every possible combination of structural components and contextual information. Finally, our evaluation focuses on retrieval effectiveness measured by nDCG@10 and MRR. These metrics capture different aspects of ranking quality, but do not measure other practical dimensions of a retrieval system, such as storage or computational costs. We investigated whether structural segmentation improves the retrieval of Brazilian legal documents. We tested four document representations based on their multi-layered structure: one hierarchical representation and three simplified representations based on core chunks with different levels of context. The results show that context-enriched representations improve dense retrieval over whole-document indexing. On the evaluated test collections, increased nDCG@10 for all three dense retrievers relative to whole-document indexing, despite substantially increasing the number of indexed units. The same pattern was generally observed for MRR. For BM25, however, structural segmentation did not improve nDCG@10, although it improved MRR in some configurations. Ablation studies show that the complete hierarchical representation is not necessary to achieve these gains. The and representations achieved results close to -- and in some cases better than -- , while indexing substantially fewer indexed units. In contrast, removing context consistently reduced retrieval effectiveness. Overall, structural segmentation effectively boosts Brazilian legal information retrieval. However, the complete structure of a legal act is not necessary to achieve these gains, and a simplified representation based on core chunks enriched with document metadata offers a favorable trade-off between representation complexity and retrieval effectiveness.
