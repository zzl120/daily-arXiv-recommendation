Examples are lawyers finding relevant prior cases for a legal case and academic researchers studying the background material for a paper. In Chapter 2 we outline the most common domain-specific tasks, datasets and applications. Domain-specific IR has a number of challenges that make set it apart from simple web search tasks: they often deal with long documents, long queries and long sessions with specific user needs. We discuss these in Chapter 3. We also address issues related to bias and fairness of models. In Chapter 4, we describe the models and methods developed for domain-specific tasks and data, addressing the specific challenges, including user aspects related to domain-specific IR tasks, dealing with queries, user models, and user interactions. The ubiquity of Web Search has ensured that the dominant search paradigm is a query box through which a query is submitted by a user to a search engine accessing an index over a huge number of documents on multiple topics. The results of this search are displayed as a ranked list of search results. While this paradigm is suitable for a large variety of search tasks, for domain-specific, specialised search tasks more comprehensive tools are required. This is particularly true in the area of { professional search} . Professional search focuses on the work of paid professionals who are undertaking a work task that is predominately search-related and performed under a number of constraints such as budget and time . Professional search covers a wide range of domains, tasks, and tools. At one end of the range, a professional search task could last several hours to days, involve a formal search language , and have potential legal repercussions if relevant documents are not found. This applies to prior art patent search as well as prior case search in case law legal systems . Search for publications on clinical trial outcomes as the basis for a systematic review in the medical domain also last several hours to days, use Boolean search, but missed documents could lead to an incorrect conclusion about a medical intervention , resulting in potentially less effective treatments being used on patients. At the other end of the range are search tasks that can best be solved by simply applying the web search paradigm using an index that is restricted to a certain set of documents, }. This has also been used as a simple definition of , e.g., by . Google has created usable generic tools for patent} and academic paper search} by essentially using its web search approach restricted to patents and academic papers. While these tools are far removed from satisfying the requirements for the professional search tasks mentioned in the previous paragraph, they are sufficient, for example, for a scientist to get a rapid overview of the state-of-the-art in a narrow area, or find patents or papers useful for solving a problem being faced. Domain-specific search can also be conducted in a non-professional context. For example, patients often search for medical information on the internet, or a university student may search for options provided by the law to extend the maximum study time due to illness, so as to avoid losing tax benefits. Such searchers often need support in other areas, such as assistance in determining the credibility of the information found. In this chapter, we discuss four domains that are prominent in IR research: legal, health and biomedical, academic, and patent. As introduced in Chapter~, these domains have large communities of professional searchers with standards for retrieval practices, but also common users conducting domain-specific web search tasks without adhering to particular standards. In other words: in each of the domains, a variety of tasks can be distinguished. For our four domains, we discuss the common tasks, challenges, and list the available benchmark collections. Legal IR encompasses all search activities carried out in the legal domain by laywers, paralegals, legal scholars~. The domain covers a range of tasks. The most prominent tasks in the context of IR benchmarking and evaluation are high-recall tasks, i.e. e-discovery~ and case law retrieval~, but the area of legal information search is wider than these strictly defined tasks and includes also legal web search~, legal question answering~, and legal news monitoring~. The legal domain has clear national boundaries, with different law systems in different countries and documents published in local languages~. The national differences are also reflected in the benchmark datasets that are available, which focus on one language at the time . is the task of, given a new case, to identify prior cases that are relevant to it. This task is considered a high-recall or even a full-recall task that is carried out by lawyers and attorneys in common law systems. Similarly, statute or law retrieval is the task of, given a legal case, to identify which formal statutes and laws are relevant to it. Both tasks are relevant preparations for a lawyer who represents a case. Interestingly, the text genres are quite divergent: where statutes and laws are written in very formal language, cases are generally more informal, describing the situation of real persons. Case law retrieval is considered a challenging task with notoriously low scores on benchmark data~. The first reason is that the documents in the collection are long and not all information in the document is relevant to the query. The second reason is that the query is a full document itself and again, not all information in it is relevant. Tasks that take a document as input for a search goal are query-by-document retrieval tasks: in case law retrieval, the input is a document describing a case. .} That case law retrieval is a difficult task, becomes immediately clear when we look at the results of .} is the term for information retrieval tasks performed by legal professionals in the context of lawsuits and governance investigations~. E-Discovery is considered a full-recall task, because any evidence that is missed by the professional might have legal implications. Because of the large collections and full-recall requirement, E-Discovery specialists are used to manually reviewing thousands of documents called , which can be more effective than fully manually done reviews~. This form of legal Information Retrieval comes the closest to open -domain web search: needs can be navigational and informational in nature , and can be focussed on precision or recall or both~. A distinction from general web search is that the indices of legal web search engines are limited to field-specific documents, and that the access to those documents may depend on the user~. A similarity between open-domain web search and legal web search is that there is a large variety of document types and lengths of documents, ranging from government reports that can be hundreds of pages long to short newspaper items. This distinguishes legal IR from patent search and academic search, where the collections usually contain documents of roughly similar length. Further, documents in a legal collection differ in terms of structure: While case law often follows a strict structure, like in patent search, other documents, like newspaper articles or legal blog posts, may have completely different formats. In legal web search, the aspects of documents that define relevance are partly overlapping with relevance aspects in general web search but also include domain-specific aspects. A study among legal professionals and legal scholars indicated that the most important relevance factors for legal document retrieval are title relevance, document type, recency, level of depth, legal hierarchy, law area , and authority/credibility~. In the use of citations to other legal documents, the legal domain has similarities to the academic domain, although citations are sparser and do not occur among all types of legal documents~. Where legal scholarly articles are similar to academic papers in other domains in terms of the use of citation references, more informal legal document types like legal blogs or news articles do not adhere to these strict structures. We concentrate in this section on three tasks that have been the focus of IR research in recent years. involve the application of , in ways that limit bias, to the retrieval, critical appraisal, and synthesis of all relevant studies that address a specific clinical question . Given a question about the efficacy of a certain medical intervention, the relevant primary studies are retrieved, reviewed, and subjected to a statistical analysis to provide evidence in favour of or against the intervention. This eventually leads to the creation of clinical guidelines for medical practice. In order to limit the bias in systematic reviews, a standard protocol is followed which includes the following tasks: stating the research question and developing a search strategy performing a Boolean search on the literature, but also following references in retrieved papers to ensure that no relevant publication is missed. appraising the retrieved papers for relevance to the research question, also referred to as { citation screening}. This is usually done in two steps: in the first step only the title and abstract is considered while in the second step, the full text of the papers selected in the first step is considered. Extract relevant information from the papers and perform a statistical analysis. The methodology for conducting the systematic review is written up and peer-reviewed before the systematic review is done. The final results are also written up, peer-reviewed and published. We outline the challenges of domain-specific IR: long documents and long queries, complex and specific user needs, session-based and exploratory search, reproducibility, bias and fairness, and evaluation. Not all these challenges are unique to domain-specific applications; especially the latter three are relevant aspects of many or even all IR problems. We discuss them in the light of our domains, and highlight literature that discuss these challenges in domain-specific contexts. In domain-specific IR, we often deal with relatively long documents. Examples are scientific articles and patent documents . Ranking these documents poses challenges for models because relevant information might be dispersed throughout the document~. Notably, the complete document is not necessarily relevant to the user, but only parts of it. Apart from these fundamental challenges of information in long documents, long documents may also pose practical and technical challenges to retrieval models. If we organize ranking models in terms of the representations of document content and queries, there are two types of ranking models: lexical rankers and embeddings-based rankers . Lexical rankers such as BM25~ represent documents and queries by the words they contain, weighted by frequency. Embedding-based rankers encode documents as dense embeddings, typically created with transformer encoders . With lexical rankers, a document can only be retrieved for a query if there is word overlap between the two. An embeddings-based ranker might retrieve documents that do not contain query terms, but semantically similar terms. In research and practice, both rankers are commonly used and often combined~. BERT-based rankers are technically limited because of the computational complexity of their text processing approach : a transformer encoder computes the attention weight between each pair of input tokens. When encoding a document, this leads to quadratic complexity relative to the length of the document, therefore posing a high load on the computational memory. For this reason, BERT models have been by default limited to an input length of 512 tokens. Transformer architectures that can cover longer input sequences such as Longformer have not shown high effectiveness in retrieval tasks, perhaps due to their sparsified attention mechanism: for in the estimation of relevance~. The most commonly used IR benchmark is the MS Marco passage dataset~. The median length of documents in this benchmark is 50 words. This is convenient for the evaluation of BERT-based rankers, which by default are limited in the length of the input that they get, but it is not a very realistic evaluation setting, especially not in domain-specific IR. In the heterogeneous BEIR benchmark~, the datasets with the longest documents are TREC-News and Robust04 . When optimizing for fairness, the developer has the choice to prioritize parity-based group fairness or opt for disparate treatment fairness . In the first case, every group or topic has equal opportunity to be retrieved. In static ranking, this means equal numbers of each group should be presented in the ranking . There are a few characteristics of domain-specific IR that make evaluation challenging~. First, explicit relevance judgements are expensive to collect because most users of domain-specific search systems are highly skilled professionals . Second, in professional search tasks, high recall is often necessary because the cost of missing results is high , while at the same time, precision at high ranks is required because professionals work under time pressure and therefore are not very tolerant to browsing many irrelevant results. Third, since information needs in domain-specific search are highly user-specific, the number of user interactions that can be used for implicit feedback is limited. Fourth, in many professional IR systems, different users see different results in their results list, based on the journals/sources they are subscribed to, and thus have access to. Fifth, recency is considered very important, playing a large role in the ranking algorithm. Because of this, and the high frequency with which new documents are published, the top of the results list is highly dynamic )}. In the remainder of this section, we discuss two most common forms of evaluation in domain-specific search: benchmarking with static test collections and simulation studies. Evaluation in IR mainly relies on static test collections, consisting of a document collection, queries, and relevance assessments for query-document pairs. These test collections have been created in successful benchmarking initiatives in TREC~ and CLEF~. For domain -specific tasks that often take place in professional contexts, creating relevance assessments can be an expensive undertaking. As mentioned above, relevance assessments by experts are expensive. As a result, the existing benchmarking collections often suffer from shallow relevance assessments~: each query only has a small number of assessed documents, and hence, a small number of relevant documents. Unassessed documents will all be marked as non-relevant in the evaluation~. This hampers evaluation because the comparison of ranking models is not sufficiently fine-grained if many documents are unassessed. This is an even larger problem when we deal with high-recall tasks, where we need deep relevance assessments but an insufficient, shallow pool with only a few assessed documents per query is available. Take, for example, the iSearch collection, which is a small collection of scientific search tasks in the physics domain~. The collection has a rich definition of search tasks, including explicit information needs, ideal answers, and search terms. The relevance assessments, however, are shallow and relevant documents are unevenly distributed over topics, which makes benchmarking on this data problematic . We follow the definition of~ that user simulations can be viewed as experiments: by simulating a range of user behaviour, we can evaluate how the search engine performs under the influence of specific behaviours. This is important because the perceived quality of IR systems highly depends on the behaviour of the users~. In the interaction with a search engine, a user will first decide to formulate an initial query. Since a poor query will not lead to relevant results, the content of the query matters for the effectiveness of the search. After the query has been issued, the user inspects the results, and the more persistent the user is, the more documents they will inspect and the more relevant information they will potentially gather. For example, if the most relevant document is ranked does not reflect the user's success experience. In addition to query processing and results ranking, search engines have interactive features, such as query suggestions. The effectiveness of the search directly depends on user choices in accepting query suggestions. User simulations are based on probabilistic models informed by real user observations~. For example, we can model the inspection behaviour of web search based on log data from a web search engine : what is the probability that the user sees the snippet at position 5? And at position 20? Noise can be added to these probabilistic models to reflect unpredictable user behaviour~. It is good to note here that realistic models of user behaviour quite quickly become complex. A simpler approach is to assign each user action a time limit and define user types by the time budget that they have for their search task~. Since the rise of BERT models for Natural Language Processing , the IR field has adopted transformer encoder models for ranking~. The first approach to a BERT-based ranking was the monoBERT model~. MonoBERT is a : the query is concatenated with a candidate document and the relevance relation of the pair is learned in a supervised manner on labelled training data. At inference time, a new pair is concatenated, fed to the trained model, and a relevance prediction is made. This approach, however, is computationally not feasible at inference time, as it requires to concatenate each document in the index to the query and, then, measure the relevance according to the trained model. This would entail millions of prediction computations by the transformer encoder while the user is waiting, with the result that the query latency is much higher than acceptable for a search engine. Therefore, an alternative and much more efficient approach to use BERT embeddings for semantic matching is the architecture, first introduced by under the name Sentence -BERT . With bi-encoder models is is possible to embed all documents at index time and use the principle of at inference time to find the most similar documents in a collection given a query. This type of retrieval models are called . The typical approach used in current search engines is a two-stage retrieval set-up. . In the second stage}, an effective re-ranker that was trained offline is applied to re-rank these top-$k$ retrieved documents. Both lexical rankers such as BM25, and dense retrieval models such as DPR~ and ANCE~ are commonly used for first-stage retrieval. One of the benefits of BERT-based models is that they can be further pre -trained on specific domains: starting from a regular BERT model, it can be made to be better suited for domain -specific texts by feeding it a collection of domain -specific texts with self -supervised training. This process adapts the weights of the transformer encoder to the semantics of the added domain data. Many domain -specific BERT models have been pre -trained since 2019, most notably BioBERT~, PubMedBERT~, LegalBERT~, PatentBERT~, SciBERT~. Domain-specific non-English models are less common~. We need to stress here that having a domain-specific BERT model is not synonymous to having a domain-specific ranker. To perform well on relevance ranking, BERT models have to be fine-tuned on pairs of queries and documents. This can be done either using a cross-encoder or a bi-encoder architecture. One of the challenges of cross-encoders, like MonoBERT, is that they need a large amount training data to outperform unsupervised rankers such as BM25 . This makes cross-encoders suboptimal for domains with limited training data available. As a result, ranking in specific domains is often done with a ranker that was not fine-tuned for the specific domain but instead on general-domain MS MARCO data. The general goal of domain adaptation for ranking models is to enable effective training on a smaller domain-specific dataset after a model was trained on a much larger dataset from a resource-rich domain. The adaptation can be seen as a machine learning problem where the model weights are not trained from scratch but adapted based on the smaller dataset~. In the common transfer learning paradigm with transformers, the model is pre-trained in a self-supervised way on a large amount of unlabelled data and then fine-tuned for a specific task on a labelled data set~. Starting from the same pre-trained BERT model, supervised fine-tuning for multiple tasks is possible: for text classification, labels are added on the document level; for named entity recognition, labels are added on the token level~. If the target domain is very different from the one on which the base model was trained, inserting domain vocabulary may help improve the quality of the model~. In the case of relevance ranking as a supervised task, a BERT model has to be fine-tuned on pairs of queries and documents. If there is sufficient data available, a pre -trained BERT model can be fine -tuned in a cross -encoder setting on pairs of queries and documents, e.g. the Chinese BERT-large was fine-tuned on the LeCaRD dataset by . Since for many domains there are not sufficient labelled query--document pairs available to effectively train a domain-specific ranker , the common fine-tuning setup for transformer-based rankers consists of two steps: A pre-trained model is first fine-tuned for the task on a general-domain dataset , and then further fine-tuned on smaller labelled data for the specific topic domain~. In the first fine-tuning stage the model learns the relevance relation between queries and documents, while in the second fine-tuning stage it further specifies the learned relevance relation to the specific domain at hand. When previously trained models are used on a new dataset without fine-tuning , we refer to this as . For both bi- and cross-encoders pre-trained models are available}. Queries in domain-specific tasks are typically more specific and often more complex than in the general domain , owing to the information needs being user- and context-specific~. In this section, we focus on the challenges of Boolean queries in particular , and describe approaches to deal with query ambiguity and domain specific query interfaces. In some knowledge-intensive tasks, Boolean queries are common. Examples are patent prior art search, systematic reviewing, and news monitoring. Creating Boolean queries is a challenging activity: it is common that the Boolean query for a particular systematic review task consists of dozens of components . Even experienced professional information specialists may have challenges in creating good Boolean queries~. It is, therefore, useful for experts to have some assistance, when formulating new or adapting existing queries. This motivates research on automatic Boolean query generation or suggestion, especially in the context of systematic reviews . define possible transformations of Boolean queries -- of the the AND and OR clauses inside a query -- that lead to better retrieval results than the original human queries. In a follow-up study, proposed automatic query refinement strategies, aimed to assist the professional searcher formulate better queries. After the rise of Large Language Models , the same group of researchers experimented with ChatGPT for Boolean query generation~. They find that, when instructed well, the LLM has potential for query formulation in the context of systematic reviews, especially in settings where time is limited and higher precision is prioritized over high recall. One of the challenges identified is that ChatGPT sometimes generates non-existing vocabulary terms. Solving this requires more guided uses of generative models. Boolean queries are not only challenging for users to formulate, they can also be complex for search engines to process. Boolean queries were originally used in a strictly Boolean retrieval context: any document in the collection would be matched to the Boolean pattern and the set of matching documents was returned. In long and complex queries, this has the effect that one additional OR statement may heavily increase the set of retrieved documents, while one additional AND statement may reduce the set of returned documents to zero. Current-day search engines that allow Boolean queries for complex search tasks combine the specificity of Boolean operators in the query with relevance ranking. This is not a trivial undertaking, since the AND and OR Boolean operators do not naturally constitute a relevance ranking. As a solution, propose to use rank fusion to the clauses of a Boolean query, where the intuition is that documents that are returned by many clauses are more likely to be relevant. A challenge in many IR contexts is that user queries tend to be ambiguous with respect to the underlying information need. In web search, queries are typically short and can have multiple different interpretations depending on who asks it and when, e.g. the query ``einstein leiden'' can refer to either a visit of Albert Einstein to Leiden or to the cafe called Einstein in Leiden. Domain-specific queries are generally longer than open-domain web search queries, but may also suffer from ambiguity. For example, the query ``search behaviour'' has a different meaning in the context of the information sciences than it has in the context of predator biology. For that reason, methods for query disambiguation have been proposed in domain-specific contexts. Approaches to query disambiguation can be based on domain information~ or query expansion . In the patent domain, the information need is represented by a seed patent document and the patent specialist needs to find good query terms to formulate an effective Boolean query. Finding good query terms requires disambiguation of terms based on semantic similarity to prevent query drift~. As introduced in Section~, long documents pose challenges to ranking models. In this context, we define `long' as too long to fit in the input of a regular BERT model, which is limited at 512 tokens. Roughly speaking there are four different approaches to deal with long documents. The most straightforward and most used approach is truncating the input ~. For many document types, this does not reduce the effectiveness of the model too much because the most important information is in the beginning of the document. This is particularly the case for genres where documents starts with a title and abstract . more specific information needs where information from later sections is required, e.g. queries related to , which are generally towards the end of the documents. Truncating is problematic also for documents in which the information is evenly distributed, such as Wikipedia articles. For case law, looked into finding the best cut-off truncation location. Compared to BERT-based rankers, BM25 can deal with any input length, and ~. It was shown that for domain-specific collections, which are often relatively small, it is difficult to beat the BM25 baseline with BERT-based models~. When truncating collection documents would lead to information loss, the alternative is to split them into short segments of less than 100 tokens and select the most relevant ones from each document given an input query. The most relevant segments are then concatenated into a pseudo-document that is used for relevance ranking . This approach can also be combined with rankers that apply so-called late interaction between a query and a document. Late interaction as proposed by~ in their ColBERT model embeds the query and document separately . At index time, the model computes the dot product between each query term and each document term, identifies the most similar document term for each query term, and sums over these similarities. apply this principle to long documents, using pre-stored term embeddings to compute passage scores, which are then aggregated into one document score. A viable alternative to splitting documents into shorter segments is summarizing the document into a shorter pseudo-document~. This has two advantages: First, as explained above, the shorter document can be more easily processed by transformer-based models that are limited in their input length by memory burden. Second, it potentially reduces the amount of noise in the document, which makes the shorter document more informative and relevant to the retrieval task. This approach requires the availability of high-quality summarization models that can take long inputs -- i.e. long-context LLMs, or LongFormer-based sequence-to-sequence models~ -- and have been trained for the domain at hand. In query-by-document retrieval tasks, such as patent prior art retrieval or case law retrieval, special attention is required in processing the query document. Since the information need might be spread over the complete input document, truncating is often not a good idea. Lexical models are sometimes used in combination with keyword extraction: instead of feeding the whole document to the ranking model, extract, first, the most salient terms from the query document, and use those as a pseudo-query . Another solution when using transformer models to process long query documents is paragraph- or sentence -level retrieval and aggregation . In this approach, the query document is split into smaller segments, and for each segment the top-$n$ relevant segments are retrieved from the document collection. In order to create a ranked list of documents, the relevance of paragraphs is interpolated to document relevance through aggregation. For search engines, inference speed is of great importance. The waiting time for a user to receive the results for their query affects user behaviour~. This is why in the development of ranking models, optimization of ranking quality is not an only goal: inference speed cannot be sacrificed for the sake of a better ranking. Due to privacy restrictions in some domain-specific search applications, , domain-specific search applications sometimes have requirements to run on in-house hardware. As such hardware can be less performant than what would be available on the cloud, efficiency is particularly important for these domain-specific search applications. In this section we discuss approaches to make ranking efficient. As introduced in Section~, two-stage retrieval is the most viable strategy to balance speed and quality: in the first stage, a set of documents is retrieved from the index using a very efficient indexing and search strategy. Traditionally, this is implemented as an inverted index combined with a lexical retrieval model. In the second stage, a more powerful ranker will re -rank the much smaller set of documents obtained in the first -stage. One potential problem of lexical matching in the first-stage retrieval is that relevant documents that do not have a lexical overlap with the query, are not retrieved from the index, and cannot be ranked in a high position in the second stage. For that reason, for first-stage retrieval, transformer-based bi-encoders were proposed as a good alternative to BM25~. In a bi-encoder architecture documents are embedded at index time, and, at query time, the model efficiently finds the most similar document in the dense, pre-trained vector space using Approximate Nearest Neighbours search~. ColBERT is a hybrid implementation, combining the effectiveness of a cross-encoder architecture and a bi-encoder architecture . The model is highly efficient and can be used for first-stage retrieval as well as re-ranking. In domain-specific IR, the longer documents can negatively impact the ranking efficiency even more . Conversational information seeking~ is a form of IR in which the user does not enter one keyword-based query to retrieve a single list of documents, but goes into a sequence of interactions with the search engine to collect relevant information. Research in conversational search addresses four main tasks: query reformulation, search clarification, answer retrieval, and response generation~. In this section we focus on retrieval methods. The queries in conversational search are typically formulated as natural language questions instead of the short queries in conventional web search engines. This aspect makes conversational search related to question answering . In conversational search, effectiveness can be measured not only for individual requests but also for the session as a whole. In that regard conversational search is related to session-based search and exploratory search. Traditionally, there are three different approaches to conversational search: : select the best possible response from a collection of responses with the use of indexing and retrieval techniques), : generate a response in natural language with the use of generative language models, : first retrieve potentially relevant information from an index, then generate the response based on the retrieved information. The main advantage of these approaches is that search engine responses come directly from the index, and are, thereby, transparent and reliable. This is advantageous for both laypeople and expert use cases. In a professional legal context, show that users can achieve better retrieval effectiveness with a conversational legal case retrieval system than with traditional search. For the use of laypeople, models for passage retrieval in conversational search can be trained or fine-tuned on question-answer pairs or on informational conversations between humans, e.g. legal or health forum posts where experts answer questions of laypersons. This approach was applied to the legal context by . In critical contexts like these, if we want to partially replace the experts by automation, it is a safe option to restrict the output to answers already written by experts before. In RAG, the retrieval component provides sources for a generative LLM to base its answer on. RAG brings several advantages to generative models: it reduces hallucination~, it makes the source of the generated text transparent to the user, and it enables the generation of responses for specific, pre-defined collections, like private collections. The relation between domain-specific RAG and IR is discussed further in Section~. There are two main purposes for data augmentation in IR. The first is to augment documents in the index for improving the relevance mapping between queries and documents. The second is to create more training data in contexts where labelled data is lacking. In this section, we discuss both. The vocabulary gap is a long-standing issue in IR: the query might contain different words than the relevant documents in the collection~. This problem is predominant in lexical retrieval models. For example, when searching with the query ``bike path'', a document about ``bicycle paths'' would be relevant, even though none of the terms overlap. Dense retrieval models alleviate this problem, but are less efficient than lexical models. One option to bridge the vocabulary gap without losing efficiency at query time is to expand documents with potential query terms during index time. A common method for this is doc2query and its successor docT5query~. The idea of this approach is that a sequence-to-sequence model is trained on pairs of queries and relevant documents that, given a document, produces potential queries for which the document would be relevant. These potential query terms are then added to the document in the index. DocT5query uses a transformer -based model that is capable of generating semantically similar query terms. In domain-specific contexts it is sometimes necessary to create additional query--document pairs . For training data-hungry ranking models like BERT, expert labelled data is often sparse .} show that a BERT cross-encoder can only improve over BM25 when there are more than 5000 relevant query–passage training instances~ {lin2022pretrained}. In specific domains, the shortness of training data is aggravated because the information needs tend to be user-specific and have a long-tail distribution . Thus, in domains with limited training data, it can be valuable to use automated methods to expand it. In many scenarios, there will be an abundance of unlabelled data: often a document collection and in some cases query logs or questions. If a document collection is available but no queries, a generative model can be used to generate synthetic queries from documents~. A commonly adopted method is InPars~. The assumption is that with a properly trained sequence-to-sequence model on document -query pairs, or a well -instructed generative LLM, the input document will be relevant for the generated queries. Generative Pseudo Labelling combines a T5-based query generator with pseudo-labelling with a strong retrieval model to create not only relevant query-document pairs, but also non -relevant documents that are similar to the relevant documents . If a collection of queries or questions is available, an option is to generate synthetic documents for queries~. Starting from real user queries instead of documents could ensure more diversity in the training collection, better reflecting the long tail of user needs. On the other hand, synthetically generated documents may not be completely representative of real documents in the collection. In a transformer, each token is represented by an embedding vector. This idea was investigated by~, proposing a method called PromptReps. In PromptReps, an LLM is prompted to represent a text into a single token that represents the complete text . Given a query pre-retrieved set of documents, the idea is to prompt the LLM for relevance, or directly for ranking. Four different prompt types are distinguished, adopted from learning-to-rank settings: : getting a relevance estimate of a single query--document pair. A pointwise prompt is, given ``Passage: }. Query: }: ``Does the passage answer the query?''~ or ``Is this passage relevant to the query?''~. The LLM is answering the question with one of the two tokens `Yes' or `No'. The logits or probability that the generative model assigned to the `Yes' token can be used as a proxy for how certain the model is that the document is relevant; this is then used to rank the documents. ~: getting a preference ranking between two documents for the same query. A pairwise prompt is ``Given a query }, which of the following two passages is more relevant to the query?'' The LLM selects the more relevant documents from all pairs, and a full ranking is deduced from the pairwise preferences of the model, e.g. using Heapsort ~. ~: getting a ranking of a list of retrieved documents. An example of a listwise prompt is shown in Figure~. {Images/listwise_prompt.png} } The advantage of a listwise prompt over a pairwise prompt is that it is more efficient: a listwise prompt generates a ranking for all documents at once, compared to a pairwise prompt that needs to be repeated for each pair of documents. ~: asking the LLM to select the most relevant document from a set. Give a query and a set of retrieved documents, the model is asked to output the DocID of the most relevant document. The logits or probabilities assigned by the LLM to each DocID are used to rank the documents. Compared to listwise, the setwise approach is faster because it only generates a single output instead of a full list. LLM output is known to be sensitive to changes in prompts, and this is no different for ranking prompts. show that the specific instructions in the prompt have a large effect on the success of LLM-based rankers. They pointed out that with an optimized prompt, pointwise ranking can be as good as listwise ranking. Irrespective of the prompt used, LLM-based re-ranking has the advantage over cross-encoder re-ranking that it does not require supervised learning on a training set of query-document pairs with relevance assessments, but is completely unsupervised. This makes it very useful in domain-specific settings where no relevance assessments are available. We argue that changing the paradigm for indexing and retrieval does not invalidate IR, because many of the tasks and problems that are studied in IR continue to be relevant, irrespective of the methods or tools for indexing and retrieval: the right knowledge corpus needs to be identified for a query; a ranking is needed to provide the most relevant information to the user ; a distinction is needed between navigational, informational, exploratory, and high-recall tasks; a solution is needed for information being spread in long and over multiple documents; diversification is necessary when handling ambiguous queries; transparency is needed for the user to show why the retrieved documents are considered relevant ; evaluation of the results is needed, depending on the task; and, finally, we need the user to formulate the queries and select the relevant information. This is all even more important when it comes to problems. Research into domain-specific IR can set examples and provide case studies for method development in the IR field at large: problems that seem specific to certain domains at first sight occur in many other IR contexts as well, especially because regular web search users also commonly engage in complex search tasks. Information Retrieval and its related fields are developing fast. By definition, a survey of models and methods that is written in 2025 will be partially outdated in 2027. We have therefore tried to focus on the long-standing challenges and model-agnostic research topics. This includes the domain aspects in Chapter~ and the challenges discussed in Chapter~. The majority of methods discussed in Chapter~ are model-agnostic and therefore will still be relevant in the coming years. The relations between IR and LLMs are subject to fast development, and 2023 papers may no longer be relevant in 2027. The overall argument of the importance of sources and source attributions, however, will remain relevant and may even become more important as search behaviour is moving more and more from traditional ranking systems to conversational, LLM-driven systems. Especially in domain-specific contexts , it is important that the output of search systems is verifiable and factually correct. In terms of scope, we have originally excluded Question Answering , with the addition that ``the retrieval step could be used as a first step in Question Answering.'' In the course of the survey writing, this topic has come back a couple of times, because of the increased interest in conversational search and retrieval-augmented generation in domain-specific contexts. In our discussion of the literature on QA and RAG , we have focused on the retrieval aspects of these topics. A limitation of this survey, and of the field as a whole, is the focus on English; the large majority of papers we discussed worked on models and methods for English domain-specific retrieval. In the scientific and biomedical contexts, this is reasonable since the majority of scientific literature is written in English. In the arts and humanities, briefly discussed in Section~, we have mentioned a number of papers working in non-English European contexts, but we have not explicitly provided an overview of work along the dimension of language. In the legal and patent domains, other languages than English are definitely relevant, because of the different law systems and national patent offices. We have discussed this in the respective sections . The challenges discussed in Chapter~ form the basis for our research agenda for domain-specific IR: The challenge of long documents remains relevant in the age of LLMs: just like humans, LLMs can suffer from information overload and struggle to identify the most relevant information in long contexts~. In the implementation of RAG-systems, therefore, mostly only the top-5 passages are sent to the generator, and there is only a small positive correlation between relevance of the retrieved passages and the utility of the output~. In domain-specific contexts, documents often are much longer than passage-length nuggets and information is spread throughout the whole document . For that reason, domain-specific RAG poses challenges that need attention from the scientific community. With the increased use of conversational search interfaces with instruction-tuned LLMs, users have higher expectations of search engines to answer complex questions. LLMs can be prompted to answer complex questions that require multiple reasoning steps, a task that is referred to as Multi-Hop QA~. In each step of the multi-hop process, a partial answer is found, and the partial answers need to be combined into a final answer to the question. Although there is a small number of medical and scientific benchmark sets for multi-hop QA available~ {mavi2024multi}, there is still much to gain in terms of methods development for these and other domains~ {mavi2024multi}. In that light it is interesting that {mavi2024multi} warn that new datasets for multi-hop QA should not depend on knowledge bases and instead be in the open-domain setting. This raises the question: what makes complex retrieval and question answering in domains like legal, health, or science, different from complex retrieval and question answering in the open-domain? And is there even a real difference, or can modern techniques for RAG-based complex question answering be applied in either domain? Only once these questions are answered, the community can focus on developing methods that solve the problems specific to the domains. The IR field has long recognized the significance of session-based evaluation~: Users can be guided much better in exploratory search, e.g. by providing query suggestions, summarization, and transparency about query interpretation. Experimental assessments however still mostly rely on query-level test collections~. For future research, advancing session-based retrieval models will be crucial for optimizing retrieval effectiveness, especially in the context of conversational interfaces. While the TREC Session Track~ provided valuable insights, these principles should be extended to domain-specific settings. Also in evaluation of retrieval effectiveness, the session context should play a bigger role: which part of the task was solved in previous query turns, and which subset of the provided information in the current turn is novel and useful? deserves attention because in domain-specific problems, not everyone has access to the same data. The data may be proprietary, enterprise-level, personal, or behind publisher paywalls with different access levels. With compute-heavy transformer models, differences in compute availability also play a role: not everyone can train their models on full datasets and with equal number of epochs; the sheer size of these models sometimes forces researchers to reproduce the results with smaller models . In addition, generative models are more difficult to reproduce than discriminative models because their output may vary from run to run. In this light, it is that IR conferences have reproducibility paper tracks. In a somewhat different angle of reproducibility, motivated by the importance of controlled search strategies, reproducibility of search results by diverse users or user groups is a relevant topic that has a direct connection to domain-specificity. Societal bias has received limited attention in IR until now. We have described some work that proposes metrics for measuring bias in a particular dimension ; the work that proposes methods to mitigate bias in rankings is even more limited. The role of bias in RAG systems has received attention recently, but it is still an open question what the role of bias is in domain-specific IR and RAG. This deserves attention in the coming years. Top-$k$ rankings are commonly evaluated for relevance with retrieval metrics such as Precision@k, Recall@k, and Mean Average Precision . Relevance is context-dependent, however, and not synonymous with utility: a document in itself may be relevant but have low utility in the context of a full search session for a complex user need; a document that is relevant for one user may not be relevant to another user; and a document that is relevant to a human may not be useful by an LLM in a RAG context.
