Existing methods typically treat narratives as flat keyword combinations or unconstrained generative queries, failing to capture how complex preferences hierarchically refine and contextualize each other. We propose the Preference Dependency Graph Framework , a narrative-driven recommendation framework centered on explicit preference dependencies. Specifically, PDG-A first parses unstructured requests to organize user preferences and their mutual qualifications into a directed acyclic Preference Dependency Graph . Through this graph, candidate evidence from the open Web is acquired via directed path retrieval, and final recommendations are determined by topological path ranking. Comprehensive experiments across three diverse domains spanning English and Chinese contexts confirm that PDG-F consistently achieves superior recommendation performance over competitive baselines, open-domain retrieval agents, and frontier language models. Further diagnostic analyses show that our dependency-guided retrieval markedly raises the attainable candidate-pool ceiling, demonstrating the fundamental value of modeling preference dependencies in narrative-driven recommendation. Traditional recommender systems on Web platforms largely rely on historical interactions or structured profiles to approximate user intent~. However, these signals often fall short as modern Web users increasingly turn to free-form natural language narratives~. While the emergence of large language models offers new opportunities to interpret such narratives~, retrieving candidates from open Web resources and evaluating their suitability require preserving how users' preferences condition and constrain one another. Against this backdrop, we study narrative-driven recommendation in open-web environments, aiming to provide personalized recommendations by deciphering rich, unstructured user requests~. Existing efforts have explored different paradigms to transform narratives into recommendation evidence. Early work primarily relied on explicit clues. For example, Bogers and Koolen~ combined example items with narrative context. Eberhard et al.~ used annotated genres and keywords for post-filtering. Subsequent work further explored automated element extraction to reduce manual annotation~. Another line explores semantic matching between narratives and items through embedding representations~. Recent work further explores the potential of directly generating recommendations from narratives with LLMs~. To further empower LLMs with external evidence, Zhu et al.~ introduce collaborative retrieval to provide behavioral signals. Shi et al.~ apply open-domain retrieval and attribute completion to rank candidates. Kanchi et al.~ utilize generated plot tags for fine-grained reranking. However, preferences expressed in narratives often mutually qualify each other~, a dimension still insufficiently modeled in existing methods. User preferences in narratives are not merely flat collections of independent elements, but rather hierarchical and dependency structures formed through conditional qualifications. Consider the following movie recommendation request: ``I'd like fantasy with romance as a subplot, rather than the main attraction. I need believable individual characters and a relationship shown through conversation, humour, and handling disagreements, not mostly sex.'' In this query, ``subplot'' constrains the prominence of romance within the overarching narrative, while conversation, humour, and handling disagreements further qualify the specific manner in which this relationship unfolds. Neglecting such qualifying dependencies can easily cause a film that superficially covers fantasy, romance, and humor to be falsely identified as a match, such as . Yet the humor belongs to camaraderie instead of romance, failing the qualification. In contrast, develops the romance through bickering and everyday interaction, satisfying the requested dynamic. This contrast reveals the necessity of explicitly modeling qualification and dependency structures among narrative preferences to guide the retrieval and evaluation of Web evidence for recommendation. We propose PDG-F , a narrative-driven recommendation framework comprising a graph construction agent, directed path retrieval, and topological path ranking. Specifically, the graph construction agent, PDG-A, extracts diverse elements from the narrative description and unfolds them into discrete nodes through a two-layer structure of perspective and instantiation. On this basis, PDG-A evaluates the qualification relationships between node pairs within the context of the entire narrative to form directed edges. After resolving cycles, it generates the concrete preference for each current node in topological order conditioned on the preference context of its ancestral nodes, allowing upstream preferences to be progressively refined with each additional qualification to complete the preference dependency graph . Relying on this PDG, we first acquire candidates and their associated evidence from the open Web via directed path retrieval, and then compute candidate scores through topological path ranking, ultimately delivering recommendation results that better fit the specific preferences of the user. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. We propose PDG-F , a narrative-driven recommendation framework that organizes user qualifying constraints into a progressively refined directed dependency structure, providing a principled structural representation for complex composite preferences. We develop a structure-guided decision mechanism built directly upon the constructed PDG , comprising directed path retrieval to acquire candidate evidence from the Web under specific qualification constraints and topological path ranking to compute candidate scores, thereby unifying evidence gathering and final recommendation under a coherent structure. We conduct comprehensive experiments across three distinct domains encompassing movies, books, and anime in both English and Chinese contexts, demonstrating that PDG-F consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance against competitive baselines as well as modern commercial retrieval engines alongside frontier models and LLM agents, with ablations and diagnostic analyses verifying the efficacy of dependency modeling. Translating natural narratives into accurate item recommendations hinges on effectively understanding and operationalizing the multifaceted needs woven into unstructured text. Guided by varying depths of textual interpretation and representation paradigms, existing efforts have broadly evolved across three paradigms: explicit feature matching, representation learning, and LLM-based recommendation with evidence augmentation. Explicit feature matching focuses on distilling concrete recommendation signals from unstructured narratives through signal integration, request element extraction, and narrative trope generation. Early work by Bogers and Koolen~ combined narrative context with referenced example items to exploit the complementarity between explicit exemplars and descriptive intent. To obtain more specific recommendation cues, Eberhard et al.~ automated the extraction of request elements into discrete recommendation cues, while Kanchi et al.~ generated narrative trope tags from book descriptions to incorporate specific story patterns into candidate reranking. However, these methods primarily organize recommendation around feature matching, still lacking explicit representations for the modifier and qualification relationships among preference elements. Representation learning approaches compute relevance by learning semantic correspondences between narrative requests and items, expanding from embedding-based matching~ to task adaptation, data augmentation, and general-purpose representation transfer. Early work by Eberhard et al.~ leveraged embedding representations to analyze the predictive utility of distinct request elements, while Chaganty et al.~ adopted contrastive learning to train dual-encoder retrieval models between conversational context and songs. Addressing the sparsity of narrative supervision, Mysore et al.~ utilized LLMs to synthesize narrative queries from user interactions, providing supervision for dense retrieval models. To better capture domain semantics, Epure et al.~ fine-tuned models to align natural language requests with high-level track descriptions. Broadening the scope to general recommendation and search, Attimonelli et al.~ validated the zero-shot capabilities of general-purpose text embeddings. Although these efforts substantially advance semantic relevance modeling, they still lack explicit characterization of the qualification scope and inter-dependencies among diverse preference criteria. LLM-based methods leverage powerful language understanding and reasoning capabilities to interpret narrative intents, evolving from direct generation relying on internal parametric knowledge toward evidence-augmented paradigms that integrate external retrieval and structured attributes~. Eberhard et al.~ systematically evaluated different models and prompting strategies, benchmarking the feasibility and boundaries of directly generating recommendations from parametric memory. To alleviate the limitations of static parametric knowledge, subsequent efforts focus on equipping LLMs with external grounding evidence: Zhu et al.~ integrated collaborative retrieval into conversational recommendation to inject behavioral signals for candidate generation, while Shi et al.~ utilized dynamic attribute templates, open-domain retrieval, and attribute completion to construct structured profiles for fine-grained candidate comparison. Following the experimental setup of OCG-Agent~, we first adopt Reddit MovieSuggestions, a widely used benchmark for narrative-driven recommendation~. We seek to further validate the capability of PDG-F across diverse tasks. Given that the proprietary ~ dataset is not publicly accessible, we instead collect two datasets from public online communities, namely with 30 English queries from Reddit and with 30 Chinese queries from Xiaohongshu and Zhihu. We adapt the curation methodology of Reddit MovieSuggestions~ to construct these two benchmarks. For each sample, the input query is obtained by faithfully summarizing an informative recommendation post while preserving user preferences, constraints, and contextual details. The ground-truth items are scored based on community support, defined by the peak engagement achieved across recommending replies, namely the net vote score on Reddit or the like count on Xiaohongshu and Zhihu. When reliable engagement scores are unavailable for a query, we quantify community support by recommendation frequency within the collected replies. We evaluate recommendation performance using Precision@10, Recall@10, and NDCG@10~. For each query, we follow the original setting of OCG-Agent~ by performing retrieval once and running RankGPT reranking~ three times, reporting the averaged results. For our method, PDG-F, ranking scores are computed deterministically from the constructed graph and collected evidence, so a single ranking run is sufficient. The empirical comparison between PDG-F and OCG-Agent naturally constitutes an ablation over dependency modeling. Specifically, our preference nodes refine the dynamic attribute representations of OCG-Agent through redesigned extraction sources and field specifications, while the overarching innovation lies in establishing directed qualification graphs. In the absence of directional dependency edges, candidate acquisition reduces to uncoordinated retrieval across isolated preference nodes. Furthermore, lacking the underlying graph structure precludes our topological path ranking, forcing candidate evaluation to revert to standard prompting-based reranking via RankGPT. Under this double degradation, the framework essentially degenerates into the baseline pipeline of OCG-Agent. The substantial performance margins across all evaluation domains, as shown in Table~, therefore confirm that directional qualification modeling serves as the decisive driver for enhanced candidate acquisition and relevance discrimination. We presented PDG-F, a framework for narrative-driven recommendation that captures how user preferences qualify one another through directed acyclic graphs. By carrying these qualification relationships into candidate acquisition and topological evidence accumulation, the framework connects narrative understanding with evidence-based recommendation. Experiments across three domains demonstrate broader relevant-item coverage and improved recommendation performance over competitive baselines. Oracle analysis further shows that this broader coverage raises the attainable performance ceiling, while the remaining ranking gap motivates more accurate evidence scoring to realize that potential.
