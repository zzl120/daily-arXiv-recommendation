Transformer-based Large language models have rapidly become a major focus of research due to their ability to process, generate, and analyze human language at scale. For this reason, LLMs have heavily impacted many research areas, including Natural Language Processing and Information Retrieval . One specific area of research concerns exploiting learned semantic representations, i.e., embeddings, which are a powerful byproduct of LLM training. Embeddings encode linguistic meaning in context-sensitive, high-dimensional representations. In fact, by learning from large-scale textual data, embeddings can capture relationships between words, sentences, and broader discourse structures, enabling tangible advances in tasks such as classification, retrieval, reasoning, and knowledge extraction. Their ability to model semantic similarity and contextual meaning has made them important tools for studying and applying language understanding across diverse research domains. IR is one of these domains where learned representations have been widely advanced and exploited for text retrieval, i.e., to retrieve the most ``relevant'' documents/passages from a collection given a user query. With learned representations, the task of identifying ``relevant'' content becomes computing a given similarity between query and document vectors in the learned space. Typical examples of this similarity are inner product or cosine. Learned representations can be grouped in three main categories: 1) representations, where documents and queries are projected by a transformer-based encoder in a dense vector of hundreds/thousands dimensions , 2) representations, which lower the granularity of the embeddings from the whole document to single tokens , 3) embeddings, which are characterized by higher dimensionality of the encodings, spread in a sparse regime, i.e, only a small fraction of the embedding dimensions are activated with weights different from zero . Learned sparse representations form an attractive class of embeddings. A solid body of literature shows that these embeddings are able to provide very high effectiveness performance with interesting characteristics that cannot be achieved by both dense and multi-vector ones. First, most LSR approaches are grounded in the vocabulary of the collection, meaning that they are interpretable by design, as 1) dimensions activated by the encoder correspond to terms in the language, and 2) their corresponding weights can be interpreted as contextualized importance scores for the specific term. Second, it has been shown that LSR provides a better generalization in out-of-domain scenarios . The reason for this lies in an important feature of this class of encoders, i.e., learned query and document . This means that these encoders can expand the original query and document texts with semantically related terms. This led to naturally addressing the well-known problem, thereby improving retrieval performance. Although effective, several contributions in the literature over the last three years show that LSR is not well-suited for use in combination with well-known, decades-old retrieval data structures. This is because LSR techniques produce representations with term distributions that are significantly different from the standard Zipfian distribution observed in text. As a consequence of this mutated property, old retrieval machineries, especially the ones based on inverted indexes, heavily struggle in efficiently retrieving results . This trend has also been confirmed by the Big ANN Challenge at NeurIPS, which in late 2023 organized a ``Sparse Track'' soliciting contributions on efficient retrieval mechanisms for ANN search. Turns out that the two winning solutions of the track are two adaptations of HNSW, a well-known---graph-based---approximated nearest neighbors index. Vector search has become a core primitive in modern data management and machine-learning systems. A wide range of applications---including semantic search, recommender systems, retrieval-augmented generation, classification, question answering, and knowledge-intensive reasoning---can be cast as the problem of finding the vectors most similar to an incoming query in a learned representation space. This abstraction is now routinely supported by vector databases and vector-search engines, which index large collections of learned embeddings and answer nearest-neighbor queries under similarities such as inner product or cosine similarity. The success of this paradigm is largely due to transformer-based large language models and neural encoders, which map text and other data objects into high-dimensional representations that capture semantic and contextual information. In text-centric applications, for example, queries and documents are encoded as vectors, and retrieving relevant content reduces to comparing their representations in the learned space. Learned sparse representations form an attractive class of embeddings for vector search over text. LSR techniques work by embedding queries and documents in a large space, but activates only a small fraction of dimensions, each associated with a non-zero weight~. A solid body of literature~ shows that they can achieve very high retrieval effectiveness while retaining interesting properties. First, most LSR models are grounded in the vocabulary of the collection: active dimensions correspond to actual terms, and their associated weights can be interpreted as contextualized importance scores. Second, LSR models are known to generalize well in out-of-domain scenarios~. A key reason is learned query and document : the encoder can activate terms that are not explicitly present in the input text but are semantically related to it, thereby mitigating the well-known problem. Despite their effectiveness, learned sparse representations are challenging to index efficiently. Classical sparse retrieval engines were designed around term distributions observed in natural text, where postings follow highly skewed Zipfian patterns and inverted indexes can exploit this property effectively. LSR models, however, produce sparse vectors whose activated dimensions and weights follow markedly different distributions~. As a result, traditional inverted-index machinery struggles to provide low-latency top-$k$ retrieval on learned sparse vectors. This difficulty has also been reflected in the Big ANN Challenge at NeurIPS, whose 2023 Sparse Track explicitly solicited efficient ANN methods for sparse vectors~; notably, the winning solutions were adaptations of HNSW~, a graph-based approximate nearest-neighbor index, rather than classical inverted-index engines.
