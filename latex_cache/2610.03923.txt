In this paper, we proposed , a learnable prompting framework for LLM-driven sequential recommendation that addresses the brittleness caused by discrete natural-language prompt formulations. Our work is motivated by the observation that existing LLM-driven recommenders are highly sensitive to prompt wording, and that naively applying standard prompt tuning to recommendation faces two fundamental challenges: lack of personalization and semantic drift risk. To overcome these challenges, decomposes the prompt into instance-invariant instruction components and instance-dependent evidence, replaces the brittle discrete instruction templates with continuous learnable vectors initialized from handcrafted templates, and introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, we propose a personalized prompt injection mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding via the frozen LLM and directly adds it to the shared instruction prompts, enabling user-level adaptation while maintaining parameter efficiency. Second, we introduce a semantic drift constraint that regularizes the shared learned prompts to remain within a trust region around their initialization anchors, preventing gradient-based optimization from pushing prompts into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully. By applying the drift constraint to the shared component while allowing personalization to operate additively, these two mechanisms are decoupled so that stability and expressiveness can be optimized without mutual interference. Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component, and generalization analysis shows that remains robust regardless of which handcrafted template is used for initialization, effectively eliminating the need for manual tuning of background and task inference templates. Sequential recommendation ~ aims to model users' evolving interests by leveraging their chronological interaction histories, and has become a fundamental component in modern recommender systems . Early research in this area mainly adopts ID-based techniques , which focus on capturing item transition patterns or co-occurrence signals from user behavior sequences. Despite their effectiveness, such approaches are inherently limited when facing data sparsity and cold-start scenarios, where item identifiers alone provide insufficient semantic information . To alleviate these limitations, content-aware SR methods have been proposed to incorporate auxiliary item information, including textual descriptions, visual content, and other multimodal features . By enriching item representations with semantic cues beyond interaction logs, these methods attempt to enhance recommendation quality under sparse data conditions. Nevertheless, their effectiveness is often restricted by the quality of multimodal representations, which are typically derived from task-agnostic or suboptimal pre-trained encoders and fail to fully capture fine-grained semantic relationships . {pic/intro.png} Recently, the rapid development of Large Language Models has opened new opportunities for sequential recommendation, owing to their strong capabilities in semantic understanding, reasoning, and generation . Existing LLM-based recommendation approaches can generally be divided into two paradigms : methods, which utilize LLMs as content encoders to generate item representations consumed by conventional recommenders , and methods, which directly formulate recommendation as prompt-conditioned autoregressive generation . While LLM-powered approaches benefit from expressive embeddings, they essentially treat LLMs as feature extractors without exploiting their generative capabilities, and often suffer from semantic misalignment between LLM-derived representations and downstream embedding spaces . In contrast, LLM-driven methods leverage the intrinsic reasoning and language understanding of LLMs to produce personalized results end-to-end, representing a more promising direction. Representative methods include TALLRec~, which reframes recommendation as instruction-tuned generation, and LLaRA~, which combines textual prompts with collaborative embeddings projected from conventional recommenders. We observe that existing methods are highly sensitive to the prompt formulation: even prompts that are semantically equivalent can produce substantially different rankings when small variations in wording, phrasing, or syntax are introduced . Such prompt sensitivity undermines robustness and reproducibility, and forces practitioners to spend considerable effort on manual prompt engineering before deployment. This issue is particularly problematic in SR, where the same system must reliably serve diverse users, one would expect consistent behavior as long as the essential information is provided, yet performance fluctuates noticeably across seemingly interchangeable templates. Several prompt learning methods have been proposed to reduce the reliance on handcrafted discrete prompts. POD~ distills discrete prompts into continuous vectors to bridge user/item IDs and natural language. LASER~ inserts trainable virtual tokens at both the prefix and suffix of a frozen LLM to inject collaborative signals. PPR~ generates personalized prefix prompts conditioned on user profiles. RPP~ employs multi-agent reinforcement learning for instance-wise prompt assembly. PromptAgent~ uses Monte Carlo Tree Search for automatic prompt optimization. Among these, methods that operate in the discrete prompt space still search over natural-language templates, meaning their outputs remain subject to the same wording sensitivity they aim to resolve. In contrast, continuous prompt learning methods eliminate template dependence by replacing discrete prompts with learnable vectors, representing a more principled direction. Our key insight is that while replacing discrete templates with learnable continuous prompts eliminates prompt sensitivity, naively applying standard prompt tuning to recommendation is insufficient due to two fundamental challenges unique to this setting. Critically, these two challenges are not independent, they interact in a way that makes addressing them jointly non-trivial: injecting user-specific signals into prompts inherently increases the risk of semantic drift, as personalization pushes each user's prompt in a different direction away from the initialization; conversely, overly strict drift constraints can suppress the expressiveness needed for effective personalization. Our framework is designed to navigate this tension. Standard prompt tuning learns a single task-level instruction shared across all instances, which is reasonable for generic NLP tasks where the objective is uniform . However, in sequential recommendation, different users exhibit vastly different preference patterns. For example, a user with concentrated interests requires different reasoning guidance than one with diverse tastes. A fixed, instance-invariant prompt cannot provide optimal inference context for all users simultaneously. To address this, we introduce a mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding and directly adds it to the shared instruction prompts, enabling user-level adaptation while maintaining parameter efficiency. Unlike discrete tokens that are inherently within the LLM's vocabulary , continuous vectors can be pushed by gradients into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully. Our experiments confirm this: random initialization leads to severe degradation, demonstrating that prompts must reside in semantically valid regions. While template-based initialization provides a good starting point, it offers no guarantee against drift during training, especially when personalized signals are injected, which amplifies the optimization dynamics. To mitigate this, we propose a that regularizes the shared instruction prompts to remain within a controlled neighborhood of their initialization anchors, ensuring that optimization improves upon the template without departing from the LLM's effective semantic space. By applying the drift constraint specifically to the shared prompt component while allowing the personalized injection to operate additively, our design decouples the stability and personalization objectives, enabling both to be optimized without mutual interference. This yields a robust and effective prompting framework that can be directly deployed by simply providing interaction histories and candidate pools. Sequential recommendation has become a prominent research topic due to its effectiveness in modeling the dynamic evolution of user interests over time . Early studies typically relied on the Markov Chain assumption to capture sequential dependencies among user interactions . With the advancement of deep learning, a variety of neural architectures, including Recurrent Neural Networks , Convolutional Neural Networks , and Graph Neural Networks , have been introduced to model complex user--item interaction patterns. To alleviate inherent issues such as data sparsity and cold-start scenarios, content-aware sequential recommendation methods incorporate auxiliary side information, including knowledge graphs and textual descriptions , leading to substantial performance improvements. More recently, multimodal learning paradigms have further advanced representation learning in SR , enabling the capture of richer sequential behaviors across heterogeneous modalities . Owing to their strong representation learning and text generation capabilities , Large Language Models have recently emerged as a promising direction for boosting sequential recommendation performance . Existing recommendation approaches can generally be categorized into two paradigms : and frameworks. The paradigm integrates LLMs into traditional recommender systems to enhance contextual understanding or semantic representations . For instance, NoteLLM utilizes LLMs to generate compact note-level embeddings for recommendation refinement, while SAID leverages LLMs to construct semantically consistent item representations. However, such methods fail to fully exploit the generative strengths of LLMs and often incur notable information loss when transforming LLM outputs into fixed embeddings for downstream tasks . In contrast, the paradigm directly formulates recommendation tasks as prompt-based generation problems, allowing LLMs to produce recommendation results end-to-end. Representative methods include TALLRec , which reframes sequential recommendation as a binary prediction task conditioned on user histories, and LLaRA , which combines textual prompts with collaborative embeddings projected from recommenders. Recent studies have explored prompt learning~ as parameter-efficient paradigms to adapt pre-trained models for recommendation~, especially under data sparsity and cold-start settings. A foundational line of work reformulates recommendation as language processing with personalized prompt templates~. Early continuous prompt tuning methods introduce learnable soft prompts to guide pre-trained encoders without full fine-tuning~. POD~ distills discrete prompts into continuous vectors to bridge user/item IDs and natural language, while LASER~ proposes Bi-Tuning that inserts trainable virtual tokens at both the prefix and suffix of a frozen LLM to inject collaborative signals and enable controllable generation. Multi-stage approaches such as SCRec~ separately learn semantic and collaborative prompts before joint prediction~. Beyond data-driven prompts, knowledge-enhanced methods such as KPT~ transform structured knowledge graphs into prompt representations to improve generalization~. To address the inherent lack of collaborative filtering capability in LLMs, A-LLMRec~ injects pre-trained CF embeddings as prompt inputs, and Recommendation Critic~ trains a separate collaborative model whose outputs serve as soft prompts for the LLM. Automatic prompt optimization methods, including RecPrompt~ with self-tuning prompt engineering for news recommendation, RPP~ with multi-agent reinforcement learning for instance-wise prompt assembly, Chat-Rec~ with multi-turn dialogue prompts, and PromptAgent~ with Monte Carlo Tree Search, further enable the discovery of expert-level prompts beyond manual design~. Following LLaRA~ and MLLM-MSR~, we adopt a candidate-based evaluation protocol with a candidate set size of 20 . Because LLM-based methods embed item descriptions directly into the prompt, an excessively large candidate pool would exceed the context window or cause out-of-memory issues during training and inference. For fair comparison, and all baselines that incorporate a candidate pool in the prompt use the same pool size of 20. Each method is required to identify the correct next item from this pool, and we assess performance using the HitRatio@1 metric, which is computed only over valid predictions : $ = / $. When properly prompted, LLM-based recommendation models are able to directly output a single predicted item. In contrast, for conventional recommendation approaches, we treat the item with the highest predicted score as the final recommendation. Notably, as adopts a generative prediction framework, it may occasionally produce invalid outputs, such as meaningless tokens or items that do not belong to the predefined candidate pool. To account for this issue, we further report an auxiliary metric, termed ValidRatio, which measures the fraction of predictions that correspond to valid candidate items across all sequences, thereby reflecting the models' ability to follow task instructions. Inspired by~, some models may achieve a high , yet fail to generate valid or meaningful recommendations. To mitigate this issue, we introduce a new evaluation metric, Valid HitRatio@1 , defined as $VHR@1 = ValidRatio HitRatio@1$. This composite metric penalizes models that achieve high accuracy only among valid outputs while producing a large fraction of invalid predictions, thereby preventing inflated performance estimates from models that ``gamble'' on a subset of valid generations. By jointly accounting for both validity and accuracy in a single score, VHR@1 provides a more faithful reflection of real-world deployment quality than reporting the two metrics independently.
