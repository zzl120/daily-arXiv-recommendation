 {llncs} Researchers frequently encounter difficulties re-finding scientific papers when they remember aspects of the content but cannot recall reliable identifiers, such as titles or author names. This type of re-finding task, known as tip-of-the-tongue retrieval, is particularly challenging for scientific documents, where recollections may involve textual information and visual elements, including figures, diagrams, and plots. Existing ToT retrieval benchmarks do not address scientific search. We introduce a multimodal benchmark for scientific ToT retrieval, comprising 108,512 open-access scientific documents across five domains and 11,499 synthetically generated queries. To construct the benchmark, we propose a scalable query elicitation pipeline that independently extracts textual and visual memory cues from source documents and uses large language models to generate queries resembling incomplete human recollections. We characterize scientific ToT information needs through an empirical analysis of publicly available re-finding requests and use the outcomes to assess the generated queries using human annotations and automated quality control. The benchmark includes lexical, dense, late-interaction, and visually grounded retrieval baselines. We release the dataset, extracted document representations, generated memory cues, evaluation splits, and accompanying generation and retrieval pipelines under an MIT license to facilitate research on multimodal scientific paper re-finding. Scientific literature search is a critical task for researchers, used for collecting related work, checking previously proposed methods, and known-item search~. Known patterns from queries persist in scientific literature search: expanded queries often contain imprecise or incorrect information, relying on attributes from remembered documents~. Scientific literature relies on figures for supporting text, which means effective representations of visuals are essential for understanding~. In retrieval, the visual component is highly relevant, as clues uttered in queries rely on remembered elements, which are often visual in nature~. Our analysis of real-world queries reveals that visual elements are frequent, and can be highly distinctive elements. Hence, evaluating retrieval for scientific literature should account for remembered clues that stem from visual content. Despite the prevalence of style query failures in scientific literature search, no datasets exist to evaluate systems on scientific queries. Prior work in has mostly focused on casual~ domains such as movies, music, and games~, which can be sourced from community question-answering platforms~. Importantly, for many domains, including the scientific domain, no such platforms exist with sufficient coverage for dataset construction, due to confidentiality, privacy, or data scarcity~. In this work, we address the lack of evaluation resources for scientific retrieval by introducing a scalable approach for generating multimodal recollection queries, an example of which is shown in~. We extend the -based query elicitation framework proposed by to scientific documents and incorporate both visual and textual information. We parse PDF documents into visual and textual elements, and generate potential clues. We then generate a query based on these clues. We validate the resulting resource and establish retrieval baselines to support future research. Queries} Previous work in retrieval~ and studies of scientific literature search~ identify characteristics of queries such as verbosity and uncertainty. However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior work has empirically characterized scientific queries in practice. We annotate a sample of publicly available re-finding requests collected from CQA platforms. The annotation codebook is constructed using a hybrid deductive--inductive procedure~. An initial set of codes was derived from prior work on retrieval~. We then applied and refined these codes while examining the collected queries, introducing additional categories when recurring forms of recollection are not captured by the initial scheme following~. The final set of codes was determined through discussion by the authors. Named-entity associations were excluded from codes to remain consistent with prior and scientific re-finding work, which typically excludes these. In this section, we discuss the construction of the SciToT benchmark, following the pipeline shown in~. We extract visual and textual elements from scientific documents, generate clues and queries, and validate and filter the resulting queries through human annotation and an judge. We construct our document collection from three sources and five scientific domains: Computational Linguistics from the ACL Anthology,} Medicine and Biology from PubMed Central ,} and Engineering and Physics from arXiv.} We include only documents explicitly released under CC0 or CC BY licenses, permitting redistribution and reuse of documents, with CC BY requiring appropriate attribution~. The final collection consists of five sets of $20+$ documents, resulting in a final corpus size of $100+$ documents, and allows for evaluation across a range of different domains and visualizations. {images/Visual_Clue_Generation.png} To generate clues and enable textual and visual retrieval methods, we extract visual and textual elements from PDF documents. Content extraction from documents is a substantial field of research~. Since content extraction is not the focus of this work, we rely on MinerU~, which satisfies the requirements of being open-source, has a low compute cost and is fine-tuned for scientific documents. For each document, we extract the text into markdown, and extract figures and tables into separate images. Qualitative inspection of extracted documents indicated that textual content generally preserved the source material and that figures and tables were generally correctly identified. To facilitate query generation, we generate potentially remembered clues from the target document. Memory clues are extracted from both images and text. To ensure that clues are only related to a single modality and element, we separate clue extraction for text and images, following previous work on query generation~. For textual clues, we apply Qwen3-4B~, providing it with instructions and the text of the paper, truncated to the token limit, following~. The model is instructed to output JSON-formatted text with a set of unstylized derived clues that are non-specific, and to exclude named entities or directly identifying information. For visual clues, we apply Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct~. The model is provided with a single image and is instructed to derive clues about the structural appearance as well as high-level semantic elements. Query Elicitation} Our query elicitation generates a query from the set of clues of the target document. We apply Phi-4 14B~, using a different model provider from cue extraction to reduce single-provider bias and stylistic monotonicity~. We generate queries based on both modalities, selecting the visual clues of at most 15 figures to stay within token limits. We closely follow the prompt style of~, providing the model with general role-play instructions, the clue set, a set of strict requirements , and optional requirements . The prompt was adjusted to align with the scientific re-finding task, instructing the model to apply the clues exactly, and to follow the clue style elements described in~. We exclude instruction of the model to provide temporal or physical contextual clues, as these would require further complex user simulation, without adding a significant new challenge for retrieval. For visual elements, we instruct the model to focus on figures that stand out to it, as these are most likely to be remembered~. All prompts are made available in our code repository. To assess the quality of generated queries, we conduct a manual evaluation of 100 queries sampled from the test set with 20 queries per scientific domain. Two independent annotators with research backgrounds, neither involved in the development of the benchmark, evaluate all 100 query–document pairs. Annotators are presented with the generated query and the full source document and independently assess two dimensions: relevance, measuring the correspondence between the query and its source document, and well-formedness, assessing the clarity and interpretability of the query. Both dimensions are evaluated on three-point scales , corresponding to non-relevant, partially relevant, and highly relevant for relevance, and not well-formed, partially well-formed, and well-formed for well-formedness, aligning with previous work~. Annotators received written guidelines containing label definitions and illustrative examples and completed a calibration round using a separate set of examples covering all annotation categories during a one-hour training session. We describe annotator judgments and report the label distributions and inter-annotator agreement using weighted Cohen's $ $ for each dimension. Annotator instructions are made available in our code repository. We systematically verify the alignment of the target documents with the generated query. We do so by applying LLM-as-a-Judge to verify whether the query components are supported by the document, and if the final query aligns with the document in general. We apply Gemma4-12B-IT~, aligning with previous patterns of applying a different model family for evaluation~, as well as using a model with long-context capacity to ensure sufficient encoding capacity~. The judge is supplied with the figures and full text of the target document, as well as the target query. The judge is instructed to assess each remembered component conservatively, and provide judgments with justifications; following this, it is instructed to take all components into account, and grade the target query on the same three-point scale as used by annotators. In filtering documents, we follow the approach of~, discarding documents that do not align with the target. To facilitate future work and provide insights into the challenges associated with our dataset, we provide implementations and evaluations of several baseline methods. We evaluate scientific retrieval as a known-item search task, where the objective is to retrieve the single target document from a collection based on an incomplete recollection query. Each scientific domain is evaluated independently, allowing future work to investigate both domain-specific retrieval effectiveness and cross-domain generalization. Since each query has a single target document, we report MRR@10, Recall@1, Recall@10, and Recall@100. We evaluate pretrained retrieval baselines across four configurations: textual, visual, page-level, and multimodal retrieval. For textual retrieval, we consider BM25~ for lexical matching, E5-small-v2~ and Qwen3-Emb-0.6B~ for dense semantic retrieval, and ColBERTv2~ for token-level late interaction. For visual and page-level retrieval, we evaluate OpenCLIP~ and Qwen3-VL~. For page-level retrieval, we also apply ColQwen2.5, a variant of the ColPali late interaction method for VLM retrieval~. We additionally evaluate Qwen3-VL for multimodal retrieval using both extracted text and figures. All exact model versions and implementation details can be found in our public repository. All methods are evaluated without fine-tuning. Textual methods use extracted paper text, truncated to satisfy model input limits. OpenCLIP encodes individual figures or pages, using maximum cosine similarity for relevance scores. For Qwen3-VL we jointly encode all available inputs for each configuration, truncated to the token limit, producing a single document-level embedding to allow for in-document context. ColQwen2.5 is designed as a page-level method, to retain this architecture, we apply individual page embeddings and use the max-similarity in the ranking stage~. To reduce the size of the index to a feasible size for our storage budgets, we apply built-in hierarchical token pooling. In~ we show the retrieval effectiveness results. For textual methods, we observe that Qwen3-Emb substantially outperforms traditional retrieval baselines. Textual baselines BM25, E5, and ColBERTv2 achieve relatively low effectiveness , compared to 0.710 for Qwen3-Emb using text alone. These results may be explained by noise in queries, limited context length . For visual methods, we find poor performance for OpenCLIP, indicating that visual information alone is insufficient without representations capable of capturing more complex semantic relationships. Notably, we find that Qwen3-VL performs relatively well using only image elements, indicating that visual query descriptions can be a useful retrieval signal in this task. Page-level retrieval with Qwen3-VL achieves the highest effectiveness . Furthermore, including both text and visual modalities also improves retrieval effectiveness for Qwen3-VL. For ColQwen2.5, we find that it performs worse than Qwen3-VL, and requires significantly more index storage, which aligns with retrieval results reported in previous work~. We do note that Qwen3-based models are used for both clue extraction and retrieval; it is possible that representational biases may favor these retrievers, although the query generation model is different. Our findings highlight that traditional retrieval methods can struggle with scientific retrieval, and reveal opportunities for developing efficient yet fine-grained multimodal retrieval methods that align uncertain memory cues with localized document content. Furthermore, data augmentation methods such as query rewriting, document summarization, or image description extraction may lead to improvements for methods which are limited by context length. Furthermore, we release the dataset generation pipeline} and retrieval implementations,} which support further development and dataset downloading. SciToT supports the development and evaluation of scientific retrieval methods, particularly multimodal retrieval from noisy and incomplete recollections. The generation pipeline also enables the construction of related benchmarks in other tasks or domains. The resource is intended for permanent availability, with updates managed through the public repositories. We have introduced SciToT, a benchmark for scientific retrieval, comprising over 100K scientific documents across five domains. We have proposed a scalable query elicitation pipeline that combines textual and visual memory cues to generate queries reflecting incomplete recollections of scientific papers. Human evaluation has demonstrated high query–document alignment and well-formedness, while an empirical comparison with real-world scientific re-finding requests showed that the generated queries capture many of their characteristics. Our retrieval experiments highlighted the challenges of scientific retrieval. Conventional lexical and dense retrieval baselines achieved limited effectiveness, but more expressive document representations improved retrieval performance, highlighting the importance of matching remembered information to localized content within scientific documents and capturing visual elements. The synthetic nature of the queries in the SciToT benchmark and their deliberate emphasis on visual clues may limit their representativeness of naturally occurring requests. Users of SciToT should be mindful of these limitations when training or fine-tuning their retrieval models. We encourage future work on expanding the coverage and resources of SciToT, in terms of both query types and additional scientific domains.
