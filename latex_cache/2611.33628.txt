Multi-domain sequential recommendation seeks to exploit such cross-domain behavior, yet two obstacles persist in practice. First, joint optimization across heterogeneous domains is prone to negative transfer, with high-traffic domains dominating the shared parameter space and suppressing weaker domains. Second, conventional ID-based collaborative signals fail to bridge domains that are semantically related but exhibit little behavioral overlap. This paper presents , a multi-modal causal disentanglement framework for multi-domain audio recommendation. From a causal perspective, user behavior is modeled as the joint outcome of a domain-invariant preference and domain-specific preferences. Guided by this view, factorizes user representations into domain-invariant and domain-specific components and enforces domain adversarial invariance together with orthogonality, restricting cross-domain interaction to the invariant subspace and suppressing harmful transfer paths. Such cross-domain consumption indicates that user preferences are simultaneously coupled and heterogeneous: the same user may exhibit very different interaction granularity, temporal patterns, and content focus across domains, while a relatively stable latent preference underlies all of them. Beyond behavior, this coupling is also reflected in content semantics, where topics, styles, and moods form a shared space across domains that cannot be captured by user--item interactions alone. Conventional sequential recommenders~ operate within a single domain and therefore cannot exploit cross-domain complementarity. Cross-domain sequential recommendation partially addresses this limitation by transferring sequential information from a source domain to a target domain~. However, most CDSR methods adopt a target-centric, pairwise transfer paradigm and rely on item-ID collaborative signals for cross-domain alignment. When the recommendation scenario is extended to three or more domains, the number of pairwise relations grows combinatorially, and pairwise alignment yields fragmented knowledge sharing without a unified fusion mechanism. Multi-domain sequential recommendation ~ mitigates these issues by jointly modeling all domains under a unified framework. Each domain serves both as a prediction target and as an information source for the others. This symmetric formulation introduces richer cross-domain signals but also amplifies the risk of negative transfer~, since harmful information now propagates globally through shared structures rather than locally between two domains. Despite recent progress, two challenges remain open in MDSR. In MDSR, all domains share parts of the representation space and parameters, turning the local cross-domain transfer of CDSR into a global coupling~. When domains differ substantially in interaction density, behavior patterns, or semantic distributions, irrelevant or harmful signals are repeatedly amplified across domains~. This effect is particularly pronounced in music platforms: the song domain typically attracts far more interactions than playlists or podcasts. The gradient of shared parameters is therefore dominated by the high-traffic domain, biasing the shared representation space and systematically degrading recommendation quality on the lower-traffic domains. Existing MDSR methods rely primarily on item-ID collaborative signals, whose effectiveness is conditioned on the existence of users active in multiple domains. In real-world platforms, however, such multi-domain active users constitute only a small fraction of the user base. Without sufficient ID co-occurrence, traditional methods cannot establish reliable cross-domain item associations. Multi-modal content semantics provide a more general bridge: items from different domains can still be aligned in the semantic space even when they are never consumed by the same user~. Effectively integrating such content semantics for cross-domain modeling has received limited attention. This paper introduces , a multi-modal multi-domain sequential recommendation framework for music platforms. To address , adopts a causal view of multi-domain preference generation: user behavior is jointly driven by a domain-invariant preference and a set of domain-specific preferences . materializes this generative structure through a structural causal graph that identifies three spurious paths responsible for negative transfer. To cut these paths, decomposes user representations into domain-invariant and domain-specific factors under three constraints: orthogonality between the two factors, domain adversarial training on the invariant factor, and architectural restriction of cross-domain interaction. To address , converts pre-trained multi-modal features into compact semantic IDs using an offline residual-quantized variational autoencoder and maintains a trainable, cross-domain shared codebook within the recommender. This design aligns items from different domains in the semantic space without requiring behavioral co-occurrence. Finally, a lightweight cross-domain transfer network imputes user representations on domains where the user is inactive, alleviating the cold-start and partial-observation issues common in multi-domain settings. The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. We propose , a unified multi-modal multi-domain sequential recommendation framework, and introduce a causal formulation of multi-domain preference generation that decomposes user preference into domain-invariant and domain-specific factors. Guided by the resulting causal graph, we derive three complementary constraints to jointly suppress negative transfer in multi-domain joint optimization. We design a trainable cross-domain semantic-ID codebook that integrates text, vision, and audio semantics into the recommender, aligning items across heterogeneous audio domains without behavioral co-occurrence, and further introduce a lightweight transfer network that imputes user representations for inactive domains to alleviate cross-domain cold start. Extensive offline experiments on two real-world datasets and online A/B tests verify the effectiveness of the proposed method. Sequential recommendation predicts the next user action conditioned on historical interactions. Early approaches build on Markov chains or recurrent networks such as GRU4Rec~, which capture short- and long-term interest with gated units but suffer from limited efficiency and degraded long-range dependency in long sequences. Self-attention models---SASRec~ and BERT4Rec~, both built on the Transformer architecture~---have since become the dominant paradigm. Beyond single-paradigm encoders, session- and graph-based formulations inject high-order collaborative structure into sequential modeling: session-based models exploit local invariance~ and fine-grained interaction graphs~ to capture intra-session item transitions, while graph collaborative optimization~ couples sequential behavior with graph collaborative signals, and a cascaded cross-attention model~ further fuses review semantics with behavior sequences. More recently, Mamba4Rec~ replaces self-attention with linear-time state-space modeling for more efficient sequence encoding. These efforts focus mainly on single-domain settings and leave the joint modeling of user preferences across multiple domains largely open. Cross-domain recommendation leverages auxiliary signals from related domains to mitigate sparsity and cold start in a target domain. Classical CDR methods such as CMF~, EMCDR~, and DTCDR~ transfer knowledge through shared latent factors or feature mappings. More recently, some CDR methods exploit multi-modal data to further boost transfer, e.g.,\ MuSiC~ combines multi-modal large language models with cross-domain diffusion generation, and P2M2-CDR~ disentangles multi-modal features under a privacy-preserving transfer scheme. All the above methods, however, treat user preferences as static. CDSR extends cross-domain transfer with temporal dynamics by modeling users' chronologically ordered interactions. Existing CDSR methods typically rely on gated transfer modules~, graph neural networks , mixed-attention encoders~, or Transformer-based cross-domain encoders~. Most of these models are inherently pairwise and scale poorly when the number of domains exceeds two. To address this scalability barrier, MDSR jointly models multiple domains within a unified sequential framework. CGRec~ formulates cross-domain knowledge sharing as a cooperative game and reweights domains via Shapley values; MDSR-DSL~ learns per-domain spaces and a domain-to-domain adaptation module; SyNCRec~ reconciles single-domain and cross-domain branches to preserve domain-specific patterns; AutoCDSR~ introduces a Pareto-optimal self-attention to suppress harmful information flow; and MOSAIC~ factorizes user preferences into orthogonal components through a triple-encoder. PMMRec~ further discards item IDs altogether and learns transferable multi-modal representations for cross-platform recommendation. These methods focus mostly on structural information sharing and largely overlook the explicit causal structure underlying multi-domain preference generation. Causal inference has been adopted in recommendation primarily for bias correction and for invariant learning~. In cross-domain recommendation, DisenCDR~ learns disentangled shared and domain-specific user representations from a mutual-information perspective. Grace~ casts cross-domain modeling as causal invariant learning, treating user behavior as the joint outcome of invariant and domain-specific factors. C$^2$DR~ constructs a causal graph for cross-domain recommendation and derives independence constraints from it to address negative transfer, while CausalInt~ cuts harmful causal paths in multi-scenario modeling through interventions. These methods validate the value of causal disentanglement but largely focus on the two-domain setting; complex cross-domain dependencies in MDSR remain under-modeled. In this section, we conduct a variety of offline experiments and online tests to evaluate the proposed method. Specifically, our aim is to address the following research questions: How does perform against state-of-the-art single-domain and multi-domain baselines? What is the contribution of each component ? How well does transfer knowledge to serve users who are cold in a target domain? Does deliver consistent gains in an industrial online A/B test? Two datasets covering three domains each are used. The Industrial Dataset is collected from anonymized production logs of NetEase Cloud Music, one of the largest music streaming platforms in China, and covers users' full-lifecycle interaction history across three content domains: Song, Playlist, and Podcast. Specifically, interactions in the Song domain are constructed from users' like behaviors, interactions in the Playlist domain are constructed from like, completion, and favorite behaviors, and interactions in the Podcast domain are constructed from playback and favorite behaviors. All records are anonymized at the user level, and only aggregated statistics are reported. To obtain a clean multi-domain benchmark, only users who are active in all three domains are retained, yielding a shared user set of approximately $3.5\! \!10^{6}$ users. For each retained user, all observed interactions form the cross-domain interaction history. For each domain, the earliest interaction occurring after the history cutoff point is used as the recall ground truth. To evaluate the generalizability of the proposed framework beyond the audio domain, we additionally adopt the Amazon Reviews dataset as a public benchmark. It contains three categories: Books, Kindle\_Store, and Movies\&TV. In this dataset, we use only the textual information of items as item features. Users active in at least two domains with no fewer than five interactions per active domain are retained. For each retained user, the last interaction in each active domain is taken as the positive sample, while the preceding interactions constitute the cross-domain input history. To this end, we design a strict cross-domain cold-start protocol on the Industrial Dataset. We randomly split the test set into three equal folds, each assigned a single cold-start target domain. For every user, we mask interactions belonging to their assigned target domain from the cross-domain history and keep only the behaviors from the other two domains; the user thus has zero history in the target domain and is equivalent to a genuine cold-start user there. The masked target-domain ground-truth item is then ranked over the full item vocabulary of that domain. All compared models use the same user-fold split for a strictly fair comparison, and performance is measured by Hit@$K$ and NDCG@$K$ with $K \{5,10\}$. {3.png} Fig.~ reports the per-domain results. consistently outperforms the strongest MDSR baselines on all three target domains. Because the target-domain history is entirely absent, the gains come purely from cross-domain knowledge transfer: TransNet completes the missing target-domain representation at the $}$ level, while the shared invariant Transformer and the domain adversarial loss keep the domain-invariant preference domain-agnostic and therefore transferable to the unseen target domain. The advantage is most pronounced on the traffic-sparse Podcast domain, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed approach under user cold-start conditions. In May 2026, we conducted a two-week online A/B test on NetEase Cloud Music, a large-scale audio streaming platform delivering song, playlist, and podcast recommendations to tens of millions of users daily.
