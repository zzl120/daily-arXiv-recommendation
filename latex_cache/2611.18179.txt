In this context, fine-tuning general models on medical datasets can improve retrieval relevance. Yet the dense embeddings underlying modern retrieval systems remain largely opaque, making it difficult to understand the mechanics behind their results. Moreover, adapting to a specific medical dataset risks losing transferability to new clinical corpora. In this work, we tackle these issues by applying Sparse Autoencoders to medical information retrieval, transforming dense text embeddings into semantically interpretable representations. To do so, our model combines a reconstruction objective with an InfoNCE contrastive loss that directly preserves query-document relevance. We evaluate three embedding models across six medical retrieval datasets, both in-domain and out-of-domain. Trained on a medical corpus, our sparse codes improve in-domain retrieval over the original embeddings, and lose less out-of-domain performance than their dense counterparts, especially when the target corpus is distant from the training one. Importantly, our experiments show that the method yields interpretable features whose semantic content can be inferred from the queries and documents that activate them most strongly. The interpretation of these features sheds light on why a particular document was retrieved, and transfers beyond the training distribution. Some works explore the mechanics behind LLM retrieval but none specific to medical IR. Yet explainability is key in medical applications in order to ensure safety and accuracy of the generated answer. Moreover, one should ensure the interpretability mechanism is able to generalize to a diversity of datasets, as medical queries are concerned with a variety of topics . In this work, we bridge this gap and learn an interpretable set of features from dense embeddings via the use of Sparse Autoencoders . SAEs have emerged as promising tools for interpreting entangled, high-dimensional representations of large models and have later been applied to LLM activations . SAEs are trained to project their input into an overcomplete, higher-dimensional basis and reconstruct it as a sparse combination of features in this basis, disentangling complex representations into components that can be interpreted. We train SAEs on the dense embeddings of medical documents and queries, adding a contrastive learning constraint to the SAE original objective, allowing us to preserve IR performance while enabling explainability. The superposition hypothesis posits that neural networks, such as Large Language Models , represent more features than they have dimensions by encoding them as overlapping directions in their representation space. Sparse Autoencoders aim to resolve this issue by projecting these embeddings onto a higher-dimensional basis where only a few neurons are active. Unlike the dimensions dense embeddings, these sparse features are often substantially more interpretable and closer to single-purpose concepts, making them easier to inspect and analyze . Our second way to interpret a neuron is to look at which words appear most often in the texts that activate it, weighted to account for overall vocabulary statistics. We do this with term frequency–inverse document frequency . Simply said, TF-IDF weights each term in a document by how often it appears in that document times how rare it is across the whole corpus of documents and queries. In our case, TF-IDF is fit once over all training documents and queries, giving a matrix whose rows are those documents and queries and whose columns are the retained unigrams and bigrams. Then for each neuron, we average the TF-IDF rows of its top activating corpus documents/passages and top activating queries, and take the vocabulary entries with the highest mean weight. In this work, we proposed an interpretable medical retrieval information model that produces explanations while retaining most retrieval performance. On the three R2MED subsets whose corpora differ most from our training corpus, sparse representations match or beat the dense model thanks to capacity reduction, while the dense model’s extra capacity mainly yields in-domain gains. Importantly, the learned features carry meaningful, robust interpretations. Our experiments show interpretation of neurons' meanings, built solely on CURE texts, can be used to predict activations on five unseen medical corpora, with concept-level interpretations outperforming raw exemplar snippets. On the OOD datasets, retrieval performance and interpretation transfer move together, suggesting both rest on features that generalize. We then used these interpretations to reveal which concepts activate in queries and documents, thereby providing users with an explanation. Our work has several limitations that are interesting future research directions. First, our human clinicians annotation campaign remains small scale, and a larger study could be for future work. Second, our interpretability evaluation relies on an LLM, which may introduce biases or calibration errors, that we try to correct by using several LLMs. Finally, while expected due to capacity constraint, sparse representations do not match dense retrieval performance on all datasets. We select the configuration with the highest average validation NDCG, then, among configurations overlapping in 95\ Our training processes three independent loaders: a corpus loader and a query loader for the reconstruction objective, and a pair loader over positive query-document pairs for the contrastive objective. The corpus is split / for the reconstruction term only; InfoNCE and validation NDCG@10 always score against the full corpus. The corpus loader, as it is the biggest, defines the epochs, and we cycle over the other two. We train for $100$ epochs and evaluate validation performance every $100$ steps, to keep the best validation NDCG checkpoint. Because the corpus can contain substantially more documents than queries, one epoch passes over queries multiple times for one pass on the training corpus, thus the query contribution in the reconstruction loss is reweighted by $ _q = 0.1$: it prevents the query reconstruction term from being dominated by the corpus term for datasets with a large document-to-query ratio. Contrastive pairs are batched by a sampler that guarantees each query appears at most once per batch, sampling one positive document per query per batch . The InfoNCE loss takes each query embedding as the anchor and its paired document as the positive; negatives are the other documents in the pairs batch, plus the documents in the MSE-term corpus batch. To ensure that a query doesn't by chance get one of its positives as a negative/distractor, a mask sets the logits of all other known positive documents among its distractors to $- $, so that they disappear from the InfoNCE denominator.
