In the news domain, this raises a societal concern that models may repeatedly surface already popular articles and thereby narrow the diversity of information to which users are exposed. We investigate training-data memorization as one mechanism behind this concentration. Using approximately 20 million browsing sessions from a Japanese news service, we continually trained an instruction-tuned Llama 3 8B model to generate the next article title in a session. The generated-title distribution was more concentrated and less diverse than the distribution of titles observed in the browsing logs. Frequently repeated target titles exhibited greater verbatim and approximate memorization, and more strongly memorized titles were generated in response to a wider range of prompts. These findings support a pathway in which popularity in behavioral logs becomes repeated training text, is preferentially memorized, and is subsequently reproduced by a generative recommender. An exploratory target-level deduplication condition reduced both memorization and output concentration, while substantially reducing the training-set size and recommendation accuracy. Our results suggest that popularity bias in generative recommendation may arise not only from user preferences or ranking objectives, but also from the model’s tendency to reproduce frequently encountered strings, with important implications for information diversity on news platforms. generative recommendation, popularity bias, memorization, news recommendation Large language models have expanded recommender systems beyond ranking a fixed candidate set. In , a model directly generates an item identifier, title, or natural-language recommendation conditioned on a user's interaction history~. This formulation can exploit semantic knowledge learned during pretraining, accommodate textual item descriptions, support sparse-history users, and provide natural-language explanations~. Recent work has further reformulated large-scale recommendation as sequential transduction and demonstrated that generative recommender architectures can scale to industrial deployments with billions of users~. The generative formulation also changes the mechanism by which exposure is allocated. A conventional ranker scores existing candidates; a generative model learns a token distribution from repeated strings and may directly emit titles that occur frequently in the training corpus. {figures/overview_en.png} This distinction matters for news. Popularity concentration in entertainment or commerce can reduce catalog coverage, but concentration in news recommendation also shapes the diversity of information encountered by citizens. Unlike entertainment platforms , where popularity concentration primarily limits catalog exploration, news recommendation carries a normative responsibility to keep citizens informed about emerging social conditions without reinforcing stereotypical views of society. When a recommender privileges already popular or historically frequent headlines, it risks recycling familiar viral stories at the expense of timeliness, freshness, and diverse civic perspectives. Prior work has linked diversity in news consumption to engagement and has emphasized that popularity diversity is distinct from topical diversity~. More broadly, recommendation feedback loops can increase homogeneity even when they optimize standard utility measures~. Recent evidence from agentic e-commerce likewise suggests that AI-mediated choice can concentrate demand on a small set of modal products, with outcomes varying substantially across models~. If an LLM-based recommender simply reproduces frequently observed title transitions, the appearance of semantic intelligence may conceal a distributional mechanism that favors already dominant content. Popularity bias is a longstanding problem in recommender systems~. Recent studies have examined whether LLM-based recommendation exacerbates or alleviates it, with results depending on the task, model, and prompting setup~. However, most analyses measure the resulting exposure pattern without identifying why a generative model concentrates its outputs. We investigate one possible mechanism: , the tendency of a model to reproduce strings that appeared in training data. Memorization is known to increase with repeated examples and can be mitigated by training-data deduplication~. Recent work has begun to study memorization in recommendation data, either as leakage from general-purpose LLMs~ or as one-hop transition reuse in generative recommenders~. We complement this work by studying string-level memorization as an explanation for output concentration in a large-scale real-world news setting. We formulate the mechanism as one premise and two hypotheses. item occurrences in behavioral logs are highly unequal; when item titles are used as generation targets, popularity also appears as repeated strings. frequently repeated target titles are more strongly memorized. strongly memorized titles are emitted for more prompts, producing a concentrated output distribution. Figure~ summarizes the analysis. Our contributions are as follows. First, we connect popularity bias in generative recommendation to string-level memorization and operationalize the proposed pathway using frequency, verbatim memorization, and edit-distance similarity. Second, we evaluate the pathway on approximately 20 million sessions from a Japanese news service rather than on a popularity-obfuscated public benchmark. Third, we show that the generated distribution is more concentrated than the observed successor distribution and that the two links in the proposed pathway---frequency to memorization and memorization to generation frequency---are both present, although their correlations are moderate. Finally, we report an exploratory target-level deduplication condition and explicitly document its accuracy--diversity trade-off and its data-size confound. Generative recommendation treats user--item interactions as sequences to be modeled with language-model objectives. Recent work distinguishes generative recommenders by how items are tokenized, including sparse identifiers, natural-language text, and semantic identifiers~. Our setting belongs to the text-based family: article titles themselves serve as item representations and generation targets. This distinction is central to our analysis because item popularity is instantiated as repeated lexical sequences, making string-level memorization a potential mechanism of output concentration. P5 unifies recommendation tasks as language processing~, while generative retrieval maps user histories to item identifiers~. HSTU reformulates large-scale recommendation as sequential transduction and reports scaling behavior and online gains in industrial deployments~. RecGPT reports that autoregressive pretraining can outperform instruction-style formulations for text-based recommendation~, and LLM-based modeling has also been applied to article-viewing flows~. LLMs have additionally been used as user simulators to synthesize click probabilities and train lightweight news recommenders without real interaction data~. Whereas that approach addresses data scarcity and privacy concerns, we study a complementary risk of adapting a generative model directly to real interaction logs: the reproduction of their frequency concentration through memorization. Popularity bias occurs when a small set of frequently consumed items receives disproportionate exposure~. Text matching can reduce popularity bias in sequential recommendation~, and prompting history-aware LLM rankers can alter their bias~. Lichtenberg found that a simple general-purpose LLM recommender could exhibit less popularity bias than conventional baselines in a movie task~, illustrating that LLM effects are not uniform. Our setting differs because the model is continually trained on proprietary interaction logs and generates article titles without a candidate set. We ask whether this adaptation process turns frequency into memorized text and then into concentrated exposure. Language-model memorization is commonly measured by whether training strings can be reproduced exactly or approximately~. Across model families, repeated sequences are more likely to be memorized~; deduplication reduces both memorization and privacy risks~. Similar tendencies have been observed in Japanese language models and Japanese news corpora~. Recommendation-specific memorization has recently attracted attention. Colacicco test whether general-purpose LLMs reveal MovieLens information~. Kim analyze one-hop memorization in LLM-based generative recommendation and show that much of the performance gain occurs where a target is a direct successor observed during training~. Their focus is generalization and recommendation accuracy. Our focus is the social distribution of generated exposure: whether string memorization helps explain why many distinct prompts collapse onto the same popular outputs. News recommendation is not evaluated solely by accuracy. Sonoda distinguish content diversity from popularity diversity and show that both are related to engagement in a news application~. Work on algorithmic confounding further demonstrates how repeated retraining on behavior shaped by earlier recommendations can increase homogeneity~. Related evidence from AI shopping agents shows that model-mediated decisions may concentrate demand on a few modal products, although that work attributes concentration to agent decision-making, interface position, and model-specific preferences rather than memorization~. These findings motivate our emphasis on aggregate output concentration. We do not claim that a single next-title model directly causes political polarization or filter bubbles; rather, we identify a technical pathway that could narrow the set of articles exposed at scale if deployed without diversity controls. We use browsing logs from Nikkei Online, a Japanese subscription news service. Public recommendation datasets may transform or anonymize business metrics in ways that obscure true item frequencies~. Given a prompt of the form ``article title ,'' generates the next title. We use greedy decoding because the goal is to characterize the model's highest-probability continuation rather than stochastic recommendation diversity. Generation stops after at most 50 tokens. For the analysis, we restrict attention to the first transition in each session. The first viewed title is the prompt and the second is the successor. Because the same first title may be followed by different articles, we retain its most frequent successor as the reference. This yields 193,815 distinct prompt titles and 101,026 distinct reference titles. We generate one continuation for each prompt and extract the first predicted article title. First, observed next-title frequencies are highly unequal. Second, frequently repeated reference strings are more closely reproduced. Third, titles with stronger reproduction are emitted for more prompts. The model therefore appears to convert behavioral frequency into a lexical prior that can dominate generation. This mechanism differs subtly from simply recommending popular items because they are globally popular. The model is trained on local title transitions, and the same successor can be associated with many different prompts. Memorization allows that successor string to become a high-probability continuation across contexts. The result is a many-to-one collapse that reduces catalog coverage and semantic diversity. The proposed mechanism suggests that reducing repeated targets may reduce both memorization and output concentration. We conduct an exploratory intervention by restricting each session to its first two titles and selecting sessions such that no target title is duplicated. Because the original logs needed for that control are no longer available for this study, we treat the result as a feasibility case study: frequency-flattened training can reduce memorization and broaden output coverage, but may also sacrifice predictive accuracy and does not necessarily improve semantic diversity. The results suggest that an LLM-based news recommender may reproduce popularity through a mechanism that is partly hidden by natural-language generation. A generated title can appear contextually plausible while functioning as a memorized default. If such outputs are repeatedly exposed and subsequently enter future training logs, the process could form a feedback loop in which historical popularity is converted into model probability and then into new exposure. Our experiment does not observe such a deployed loop, but prior work shows that recommendation feedback can increase homogeneity~. This makes memorization relevant not only to privacy and copyright, but also to the distribution of attention. For news providers, output coverage and popularity diversity should therefore be evaluated alongside next-item accuracy. The exact-match decline in the deduplicated condition illustrates the accuracy–diversity trade-off~. To align generative news recommendation with journalistic norms—delivering timely updates on emerging events without reproducing stereotypical or stale head articles—production systems should combine three methodological adjustments rather than relying on indiscriminate deduplication: frequency-aware loss reweighting with recency decay so older viral headlines do not dominate lexical priors; candidate-grounded or constrained decoding to guarantee that generated strings map strictly to valid, current catalog articles without suffix corruption; and diversity-aware reranking enforcing editorial exposure floors across topics and viewpoints. Beyond this single snapshot, our insider auditing framework—linking training-log duplication directly to decoding behavior—can be extended to track temporal freshness decay, compare sparse/semantic-ID tokenization against title generation, and monitor deployed feedback loops in real time. First, the study uses one Japanese news service, one observation period, one 8B model, and greedy decoding. Moreover, our setting is a text-based generative recommender in which article titles themselves serve as natural-language item representations and generation targets. The proposed mechanism may therefore depend on the item-tokenization scheme: our character-level memorization measures do not directly apply to recommenders based on sparse or semantic identifiers, and whether analogous popularity--memorization relationships arise under those representations remains an open question. The absolute concentration values may also differ across domains, model scales, languages, and stochastic decoding strategies. Second, the analysis is correlational. Although frequency, memorization, and output concentration align with the hypothesized pathway, unobserved properties of article titles or transitions may affect all three. Third, the evaluation uses a modal successor for each prompt and therefore does not represent the full conditional distribution of user behavior. Fourth, the target-deduplication experiment has a major data-size confound, as discussed above. Fifth, the raw logs and model cannot be released, limiting independent reproduction. We therefore report aggregate statistics and detailed metric definitions, and future work should release evaluation code or equivalent reproducibility materials where possible. Sixth, the paper studies aggregate item exposure rather than fairness across user groups or viewpoints. It should not be read as evidence of demographic unfairness or ideological polarization. The study uses proprietary behavioral logs under Nikkei's internal data-governance procedures for research use. User and session identifiers were removed or pseudonymized before analysis. The study analyzes observational service logs and involved no intervention with users. Only aggregate statistics and selected article-title examples are reported; no user identifiers or individual session traces are included in the manuscript. Raw logs and trained weights are not distributed. We analyzed popularity concentration in an LLM-based generative news recommender through the lens of training-data memorization. Generated titles were markedly less diverse than observed successors. Repeated reference titles were more strongly memorized, and memorized titles were generated for more prompts, supporting a mechanism in which behavioral frequency is transformed into repeated lexical output. An exploratory target-level deduplication condition reduced frequency concentration and memorization but also reduced the training set by two orders of magnitude, worsened exact-match accuracy, and did not improve semantic diversity. The central contribution is therefore not a definitive mitigation method, but an empirical account of how generative recommendation can reproduce popularity.
