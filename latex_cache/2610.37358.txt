On 60 previously unseen QASPER training-split articles , the policy triggers hybrid retrieval on 46 questions (24.5\ Retrieval-augmented generation has emerged as an effective approach for improving the factual grounding of large language models by incorporating information from external knowledge sources . By combining parametric knowledge with retrieved evidence, RAG systems can support knowledge-intensive question answering while offering greater transparency and access to domain-specific information. Nevertheless, the reliability of generated answers remains dependent on both the quality of retrieved evidence and the generator's ability to interpret and use that evidence correctly. A central limitation of conventional RAG pipelines is their reliance on fixed retrieval strategies. Regardless of the difficulty of a question or the quality of initially retrieved evidence, the same retrieval procedure is typically applied. However, different queries may benefit from different retrieval strategies. Dense retrieval captures semantic similarity, while lexical retrieval can recover information involving specific terminology or exact expressions. Combining these approaches may improve evidence coverage, but additional candidates can also introduce irrelevant information, displace useful passages during reranking, and increase computational cost. Recent studies have explored adaptive retrieval as a way to address these limitations. Adaptive-RAG selects retrieval strategies according to question complexity, while active retrieval approaches such as FLARE dynamically retrieve additional information during generation. Corrective RAG introduces retrieval-quality assessment to trigger corrective actions. These studies demonstrate the value of adapting retrieval behavior, but also motivate a more specific question: whether the incremental benefit of an additional retrieval strategy can be predicted from the evidence already available. Importantly, improvements in retrieval effectiveness do not necessarily translate into more reliable generated answers. A retriever may identify relevant evidence without providing all the information needed to answer a question. Conversely, a generator may produce an incomplete, incorrect, or unsupported answer even when sufficient evidence is present. Previous studies on attribution and factuality evaluation, including ALCE , FActScore , and RAGAs , highlight the importance of distinguishing factual correctness from evidential support. Consequently, evaluating a retrieval strategy solely through evidence recall or hit rate may provide an incomplete picture of its downstream utility. This paper investigates the following research question: We study a deliberately constrained two-action retrieval policy. Given an initial dense-retrieval and cross-encoder-reranking result, the policy selects either , which retains the baseline evidence, or , which uses lexical--semantic retrieval followed by reranking. Rather than predicting retrieval failure directly, the policy estimates the expected incremental evidence benefit of switching to hybrid retrieval using features observable from the baseline retrieval process. The decision additionally accounts for the cost of invoking the more expensive retrieval strategy. A distinguishing aspect of our study is the separation between the policy's training objective and its downstream evaluation. The policy is trained to predict improvements in evidence Hit@10, whereas its practical usefulness is assessed through both retrieval-level outcomes and generation-level reliability. This design allows us to examine whether improvements in retrieved evidence translate into corresponding gains in answer correctness and faithfulness. Our contributions are threefold: We develop an operational two-action, cost-aware adaptive retrieval policy that predicts the incremental evidence benefit of hybrid retrieval using only baseline-observable runtime features. We conduct a document-disjoint, prediction-first held-out evaluation, comparing adaptive retrieval against fixed dense and hybrid baselines with paired paper-level uncertainty estimates. We investigate the relationship between evidence retrieval effectiveness and downstream answer reliability through an exploratory generation-level evaluation of correctness, faithfulness, and abstention. Our findings highlight both the efficiency potential and the limitations of evidence-gain-based routing. Retrieval-augmented generation integrates external knowledge retrieval with language-model generation to improve performance on knowledge-intensive tasks. introduced a foundational RAG framework combining parametric sequence-to-sequence models with non-parametric knowledge retrieval. Subsequent research has explored different retrieval and augmentation mechanisms, including retriever adaptation and hierarchical evidence organization . provide a broader overview of these developments. Despite substantial advances in retrieval architectures, the availability of relevant evidence does not guarantee that a language model will produce a factually correct and well-grounded answer. Our work focuses on the decision of whether to invoke an additional retrieval strategy rather than proposing a new retrieval architecture. Adaptive retrieval methods aim to allocate retrieval effort according to the needs of individual queries. proposed Adaptive-RAG, which selects retrieval strategies according to question complexity. introduced FLARE that performs active retrieval during text generation by identifying potentially unreliable upcoming content. proposed Self-RAG that integrates retrieval and generation with learned reflection mechanisms that support adaptive retrieval and self-evaluation. These approaches demonstrate that retrieval need not follow a uniform procedure across all queries. Our approach differs in its narrower decision setting: instead of selecting retrieval depth from question complexity or controlling retrieval during generation, we use the output of an initial retrieval procedure to estimate the marginal evidence benefit of a predefined hybrid alternative. Corrective Retrieval-Augmented Generation evaluates retrieved evidence and triggers corrective retrieval operations when the available information is unreliable. Hybrid retrieval provides another mechanism for improving evidence coverage by combining complementary lexical and semantic signals. However, additional retrieval is not guaranteed to improve the final evidence ranking, since newly introduced candidates may displace previously relevant passages. We therefore frame hybrid retrieval as an optional action with potentially positive, neutral, or negative incremental outcomes. Rather than treating low retrieval confidence as sufficient justification for correction, our policy estimates the expected change in evidence retrieval success. Evaluating RAG systems requires distinguishing retrieval quality from generation quality. RAGAs introduces a framework for assessing different components of RAG pipelines, including faithfulness and answer quality. introduced RAGChecker which further emphasizes fine-grained diagnosis of retrieval and generation behavior. Complementary work on factuality and attribution, such as FActScore and ALCE , demonstrates the importance of verifying generated claims and their supporting evidence. Motivated by these findings, we evaluate retrieval-level effectiveness separately from answer correctness and faithfulness. This distinction is particularly important because our adaptive policy is optimized using an evidence-level proxy rather than direct supervision from answer-quality labels. Overall, our work investigates whether a lightweight, cost-aware policy trained on incremental evidence retrieval outcomes can provide a useful efficiency--reliability trade-off. Our results separate three claims often conflated in RAG efficiency studies: retrieving more appropriate evidence, deciding when to incur the cost of a stronger retrieval arm, and improving an answer's factual correctness and grounding. The first two are partly supported by the held-out evidence evaluation: a lightweight policy maintained the always-hybrid hit rate while invoking that arm much less frequently. The third is not established. The routing regressor was trained against a binary evidence-hit difference, which is not a calibrated estimate of answer correctness or faithfulness; the disconnect is therefore unsurprising in principle and informative empirically. The small number of rescue and harm instances limits policy learning. The final ridge is deliberately simple, but its predictions may be weakly identified when almost all training differences are zero. Moreover, per-article question dependence requires clustered rather than question-independent uncertainty estimates. These considerations make conservative interpretation essential. We do not use the post-evaluation held-out results to refit the model or readjust its threshold. Generation labels were obtained with an LLM judge using the same model family as the generator. Only a targeted subset underwent assistant-assisted review; this is not an independent blinded human-annotator study. A defensible final answer-quality claim requires independent assessment, a clear disagreement protocol, and explicit analysis of judge agreement/error. Finally, the available evidence does not demonstrate strong statistically conclusive downstream correctness or faithfulness improvements. For auditability, retained artifacts include development and held-out question/document identifiers, input and model hashes, frozen feature order and threshold, per-arm evidence rankings, immutable generated prompts, generator outputs, provisional answer labels, and article-level paired bootstrap tables. We evaluated a cost-aware, baseline-feature-only policy for choosing between dense-plus-reranked and hybrid-plus-reranked evidence in scientific-document RAG. On a held-out set of QASPER articles, the policy matched always-hybrid evidence Hit@10 while selecting hybrid for fewer than one quarter of questions. However, exploratory generated-answer evaluations did not establish a corresponding correctness or faithfulness advantage, and the quality labels remain insufficiently independently validated. The main empirical lesson is therefore a distinction, not a victory claim: efficient evidence selection is possible in this setting, but better retrieval metrics cannot be assumed to imply more reliable generated answers.
