For each dataset and seed, we estimate the corpus mean and centred covariance from up to one million documents sampled uniformly without replacement, using the full corpus when it is smaller. Embedding compression seeks compact representations that preserve retrieval effectiveness. We review approaches to learning and encoding these representations, spectral transformations of existing embeddings, and relevance-aware coordinate weighting. Compact representations can be learned alongside an encoder or constructed from its existing outputs. Matryoshka Representation Learning~ trains nested representations with informative prefixes, enabling compression by prefix truncation~. With a fixed encoder, ConAE learns a conditional autoencoder for retrieval-oriented dimensionality reduction~. Search-Adaptor uses relevance supervision to adapt existing embeddings~, while Matryoshka-Adaptor learns compressed representations at multiple dimensions through supervised or unsupervised objectives~. SMEC combines sequential Matryoshka training with adaptive dimension selection and cross-batch learning~. CSR learns sparse codes over frozen embeddings as an alternative to Matryoshka-style truncation~. These approaches learn how information is distributed across output coordinates; RRC instead calibrates weights in a fixed principal subspace without iterative model training. =-1 Simpler alternatives reduce dimensionality without learning a compression module. Random projection uses a data-independent linear map~, and random coordinate selection has been reported to preserve much of the retrieval effectiveness of the full embeddings~. Quantization is complementary to dimensionality reduction: it reduces storage by encoding vectors more compactly. Product quantization~ encodes subvectors using separate codebooks~, optimized product quantization~ optimizes the space decomposition and codebooks~, and RaBitQ provides error-bounded distance estimation through randomized quantization~. PCA and PQ have also been combined in a retrieval compression pipeline~, and Section~ examines how relevance calibration affects such combinations. Unlike random maps, spectral compressors use the covariance structure of a collection to select principal directions and rescale the projected coordinates. A common family projects embeddings onto the leading principal directions and assigns them weights proportional to $ _i^{- }$, with PCA and whitening as endpoints and intermediate values giving partial whitening~. PCA preserves the variances of the projected coordinates~, whereas whitening equalizes them and can amplify noise in low-variance directions~; Soft-ZCA uses eigenvalue regularization to control whitening strength~. SpecTemp selects the exponent from the corpus spectrum and the target dimension~. PCA can also improve retrieval effectiveness in some domain-adaptation settings~. In each of these weighting rules, the weight of a retained direction is a fixed function of its eigenvalue, derived from corpus statistics without relevance judgments for the target retrieval task. RRC uses these judgments to calibrate direction-specific weights while retaining the prior's spectral trend. For retrieval, a useful coordinate is one that helps distinguish relevant documents from competing candidates, and several methods estimate this usefulness from feedback. Dimension importance estimation~ uses pseudo-relevance feedback or generated pseudo-documents to select a query-dependent subspace of the original embeddings~. Follow-up work examines its reproducibility and feedback aggregation~, contrasts positive with pseudo-irrelevant feedback~, and determines the retained dimensions from a risk criterion~. Learning to Select trains a predictor of dimension importance from labeled positive and negative documents and masks query coordinates at inference~. These methods select coordinates separately for each query while retaining full-dimensional document representations. RRC aggregates relevance evidence across training queries into shared weights in a fixed spectral basis, producing a single map that compresses both document and query embeddings. The margin-based weights estimated by RRC have a mean--variance structure related to diagonal linear discriminant rules, which weight class-mean differences by inverse feature variances~. RRC applies this weighting to coordinate-level retrieval margins and decomposes the estimated log weights into a fitted power-law trend and a direction-specific residual. Spectral compression reduces the storage and scoring costs of dense retrieval by projecting embeddings onto a smaller principal subspace. However, its corpus-derived weighting rules tie all retained directions to a shared spectral trend, without directly accounting for their individual contributions to relevance. To address this, we propose , a closed-form method for refining spectral weights using relevance judgments. RRC estimates margin-based weights from the means and variances of positive--negative score contributions along each retained direction. It retains directions with positive estimated weights, decomposes their log weights into a fitted power-law trend and a residual, and adds the residual to the compressor's log weights on this support. This allows relevance judgments to adjust individual directions while preserving the compressor's overall spectral trend. RRC applies to PCA, Whitening, and SpecTemp, and incorporates the corrections into the existing transformation shared by documents and queries, without iterative model training or an additional inference stage. Experiments with three encoders on nine datasets covering conventional and reasoning-intensive retrieval show that SpecTemp\,+\,RRC achieves the highest mean effectiveness among tested compressors on the BEIR group and BRIGHT at every target dimension. On the five evaluated BRIGHT datasets, it improves over SpecTemp by an average of 2.34 nDCG@10 points at 768 dimensions. The gains also persist under scalar and product quantization, improving the trade-off between per-document storage and retrieval effectiveness. Recent encoders built on large language models produce embeddings~ with thousands of dimensions; for example, Qwen3-Embedding-8B and Harrier-27B produce embeddings of 4096 and 5376 dimensions, respectively~. For Web search systems, the storage and similarity-computation costs of these representations grow with both the embedding dimensionality and the collection size. Embedding compression addresses this deployment challenge by reducing representation size while preserving the information needed to rank relevant documents. We focus on post-hoc compression of existing embeddings, a practical setting when the encoder is fixed or accessible only through an API. =-1 Within this setting, learned compression methods, including conditional autoencoders~ and Matryoshka-Adaptor~, optimize transformations over existing embeddings. These methods offer flexible mappings but introduce additional training costs and optimization choices, such as learning rates and stopping criteria. For supervised variants, scarce relevance judgments can further constrain validation and model selection. Spectral methods construct a fixed compression map directly from corpus statistics~: they project embeddings onto the $k$ leading principal directions and rescale the projected coordinates. Common choices assign weights proportional to $ _i^{- }$ to the retained spectral directions, where $ _i$ is the $i$-th eigenvalue and the shared exponent $ $ controls whitening degree~. Varying $ $ interpolates between PCA , which leaves the projected coordinates unscaled~, and Whitening , which equalizes their variances~. SpecTemp~ selects this exponent from the corpus spectrum and the target dimension. These methods yield a transformation that requires no iterative model training and apply identically to document and query embeddings. Their weighting rules, however, are derived from corpus statistics without using relevance judgments for the target retrieval task. =-1 Corpus variance describes how much a projected coordinate varies across documents, but does not directly measure whether its associated direction helps distinguish relevant documents from competing candidates. Within the retained principal subspace, a shared exponent shifts weight between high- and low-variance directions, while tying all weights to the same power-law trend. This rule cannot independently strengthen or suppress a direction according to its contribution to relevance. Relevance judgments provide evidence about these direction-specific contributions. Learned adaptors exploit such supervision to improve retrieval with fixed embeddings~, including compressed representations~. We investigate how this relevance information can directly calibrate spectral weights through a closed-form update, retaining a single fixed compression map for document indexing and query compression. =-1 We propose , which refines the weights assigned by an existing spectral compressor using relevance judgments. We refer to the compressor's weights before calibration as the . RRC first estimates margin-based weights from training triples, accounting for each direction's mean contribution to the positive--negative score margin and its variance. These estimates contain both an overall spectral trend and direction-specific departures from that trend. RRC separates the two by fitting a power law to the positive estimated weights in log space. It then adds the residual to the prior's log weights on the positive support and sets the remaining weights to zero. For a fixed spectral basis and training triples, the same residual can calibrate PCA, whitening, or SpecTemp. Calibration requires only moment estimation and a least-squares fit, and the resulting weights are incorporated into the existing compression map without adding a separate inference stage.
