Modern recommender systems are typically optimized to maximize ranking or accuracy metrics such as NDCG and Recall. However, this optimization often leads to popularity bias, the systematic over-exposure of head items and under-representation of the long tail~. As a result, recommendations tend to concentrate on already popular content, limiting diversity, reinforcing exposure feedback loops, and providing a suboptimal experience for users with niche interests. The problem is particularly relevant in sequential recommendations, where next-item prediction is performed over large, highly imbalanced catalogs using standard likelihood-based training objectives. A range of approaches has been proposed to mitigate popularity bias, including re-weighting or re-sampling training data~, incorporating regularization terms that penalize exposure imbalance~, applying logit adjustment based on item priors~, and post-processing ranking lists to promote long-tail items~. While these methods can reduce the dominance of head items in recommendations, they often introduce a trade-off between bias reduction and relevance. In practice, suppressing popular items may lead to degraded ranking accuracy, as popularity is often correlated with relevance signal. Consequently, mitigating popularity bias without sacrificing recommendation performance remains a challenging problem. We propose , a method for reducing popularity bias in sequential recommendation models that are typically trained with objectives based on softmax or its sampled variants~. Our central idea is to make popularity correction context-dependent. We derive a sparse prior-regularized objective from a generalized variational principle, obtaining a sparse probability mapping whose active support is jointly determined by contextual relevance and item popularity. The sparsity is provided by entmax~, which assigns non-zero probability only to items with sufficiently large logits and effectively acts as a differentiable form of hard negative selection~. By incorporating item prior directly into the variational principle, we make this selection popularity-aware: popular items are more likely to enter the competition, but only when they are contextually plausible. Thus, the prior influences that items receive a gradient signal, and the model is trained to discriminate the target item from a dynamically selected set of negatives that are both contextually relevant and popular. We evaluate PR-entmax across five datasets and two widely-used sequential recommendation architectures , demonstrating that it consistently mitigates popularity bias while preserving or improving ranking quality. The proposed approach modifies only the training objective and requires no architectural changes. At inference, the prior term is removed, and items are ranked by the learned contextual relevance alone. These properties make it straightforward to integrate our method into existing sequential recommendation pipelines. The proposed objective allows item popularity to affect the active support of the loss, i.e., the set of items receiving non-zero probabilities and gradients during training. We derive the proposed objective from a generalized variational principle that unifies several existing training objectives as special cases, providing a principled foundation for context-dependent debiasing. Through experiments across five datasets and two sequential architectures, we show that the method advances the relevance–debiasing trade-off. Additionally, we analyze how the sparsity parameter controls this trade-off, identifying regimes with strong performance. In this work, we revisited popularity bias in sequential recommendations from the perspective of the training objective. We showed that making popularity correction context-dependent through a sparse probability mapping provides an effective and principled alternative to standard softmax-based training. Our experiments further show that sparsity alone, through Entmax, consistently improves ranking accuracy but has a mixed, dataset-dependent effect on popularity bias. Approaches to mitigating popularity bias are commonly grouped into pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing methods~. Pre-processing methods modify the training data distribution through re-weighting, re-sampling, or balancing strategies, reducing the dominance of head items before model training~. Post-processing methods instead modify the final recommendation lists to improve long-tail exposure, diversity, or calibration without changing the trained model~. In-processing methods, which our work belongs to, modify the training procedure itself: examples include regularization terms that penalize exposure imbalance~ and corrections based on item priors~. While these directions can reduce the dominance of popular items, they often introduce a relevance-debiasing trade-off: aggressive promotion of less popular items may reduce ranking quality when popularity is correlated with user preference. Most relevant to our work are methods that mitigate popularity bias by modifying the loss function. Inverse propensity weighting~ re-weights training examples to counteract exposure imbalance. Logit adjustment~ shifts logits by the log-prior of each item or class, training the model to estimate a de-popularized score. It has been studied in long-tailed classification and imbalanced learning~. In recommendation, related corrections also appear in sampled softmax and log-Q correction, where item sampling probabilities are accounted for during training~. Margin-based methods~ introduce popularity-dependent margins. Other recent approaches include causal interventions based on popularity~ and popularity-aware negative sampling that control which negative items are used during training~. These methods typically apply their corrections within a dense softmax, where every item contributes to the loss. Our approach instead operates over a sparse mapping, where the correction interacts with a context-dependent subset of competing items. A separate line of work studies sparse probability mappings as alternatives to softmax. Sparsemax and entmax replace dense softmax distributions with sparse distributions, assigning exactly zero probability to part of the output space~. These mappings can be derived from the variational principle and Fenchel--Young loss frameworks~. Entmax and related sparse mappings have been applied as an alternative to softmax in attention mechanisms and output distributions~. In recommender systems, sparse mappings have been used mainly inside model architectures, for example, in sparse attention mechanisms~ or for combining multiple intent representations~. To our knowledge, however, their use as output probability mappings for training sequential recommenders remains underexplored. We first review softmax cross-entropy, the standard training objective in sequential recommendation, and then describe entmax and the Fenchel–Young framework for sparse probability mappings. Sequential recommendation models such as SASRec~, BERT4Rec~, and GRU4Rec~ are typically trained using the cross-entropy loss, either over the full item catalog or with sampled negative items~. Given a user interaction sequence, the model produces logits $ ^{||}$, where $$ is a set of items and each logit $z_i$ represents the relevance score of item $i$. The objective is to distinguish the ground-truth next item from all other items in the catalog. Prior work has shown that softmax-based CE training substantially outperforms binary cross-entropy loss in sequential recommendation settings~, and CE training has become the standard optimization objective for modern sequential recommenders. To obtain a probability distribution over items, logits are transformed using the softmax function: p_i ={ _{j } } The softmax mapping produces a valid probability distribution, ensuring that $p_i > 0$ for all items and that probabilities sum to one. Let $y $ denote the ground-truth next item, and let $$ denote the corresponding one-hot vector with $y_i = 1 $. The CE loss is defined as the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth item $y$: _{} = - p_y The gradient of the CE loss with respect to the logits has the well-known form _{}}{ z_i} = p_i - y_i, where $y_i$ is equal to one for the target item and zero otherwise. For the positive target item, the gradient equals $p_y - 1$, which increases the corresponding logit during optimization. For every negative item $i y$, the gradient equals $p_i$, pushing its score downward proportionally to its predicted probability. Consequently, optimization reallocates probability mass from negative items toward the ground-truth item. An important property of softmax is its full support: every item receives strictly positive probability regardless of its relevance to the current context. This induces dense competition over the entire item catalog. Every negative item participates in the loss computation and receives a non-zero gradient update on every training example, so gradient mass is spread across a very large number of irrelevant negatives. This observation motivates sparse probability mappings, which replace dense global competition with context-dependent sparse competition among a subset of candidate items. {images/pr_entmax.pdf} Recently, was introduced as a sparse generalization of softmax~. In the variational principle, entropy acts as a regularizer controlling the smoothness of the solution. The active support is determined entirely by current logits, with no mechanism to account for global item popularity. Consequently, entmax can be expected to improve ranking accuracy on its own, while its effect on popularity-related metrics remains limited and dataset-dependent.
