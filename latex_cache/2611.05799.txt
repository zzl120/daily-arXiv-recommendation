Generative retrieval techniques enable modern recommender systems to perform large-scale next-item retrieval by learning the next-item distribution and generating recommendations from it, using either discrete tokens~ or continuous item embeddings~. Either way, a recommender needs an item representation table as input, i.e., the map from a catalog to the representations it generates or scores against, and how that map is built is a lever in its own right, open to external information. Traditionally, it is : one independently parameterized vector per item, with no explicit parameter tying between item rows. Recently, discrete methods have replaced that default, whereas continuous methods have retained it. models tokenize each item embedding into codes and emit those codes autoregressively~ or by masked diffusion~. They owe much of their strength to those codes being Semantic IDs ~. A residual quantizer~ turns each item's pretrained content embedding into a tuple of codes, so the item vocabulary becomes a clustering~. Two things follow: items sharing a code share the parameters attached to it, so their gradients are aggregated through the shared codeword, introducing explicit cross-item coupling and thereby pooling training signal across items; and because the codes are quantized from a content embedding, that grouping imports semantic structure. However, tokenization has two costs: committing each item to a single codeword at each level generally introduces reconstruction error, and distinct items may also collide on the same code tuple. A recent line of work makes the tokenizer itself adaptive by regularizing it~, coupling it to the recommender~, or relaxing the assignment so gradients pass through it~, but recommendation is still organized around emitting discrete codes, and both costs remain: they stem from tokenization itself, not from when the tokenizer is fit. models instead represent each item as a vector in $^d$ and retrieve those nearest to the one they predict~. They do not tokenize and are free to seed the content embedding straight into their item table, so semantic structure reaches them without any codes. What they do not obtain automatically is explicit cross-item parameter sharing: every item row remains independently parameterized. The discrete branch commonly replaces an atomic item identifier with a by quantizing a pretrained content embedding~. This is generally done through residual schemes that cluster either directly in the embedding space (RQ-KMeans and RQ-GMM~) or in a learned latent space . A recommender then predicts the resulting tuple autoregressively, as in TIGER~, OneRec~, and MiniOneRec~, or through masked discrete diffusion, as in MaskGR~. SIDs provide two distinct benefits. The first, , arises because reusing codewords ties parameters across items and pools supervision from their interactions. The second, , comes from deriving the assignments from pretrained content embeddings, which places semantically related items under shared parameters. Together, these benefits improve generalization~. However, an SID exposes only hard code assignments and preserves a partition of the content space rather than its full continuous geometry. Continuous generative recommenders replace discrete token prediction with generation in a continuous space. DiffRec~ denoises a user's interaction vector, whereas DreamRec~ and DiffuRec~ diffuse a target item embedding for next-item prediction. FMRec~ and FlowRec~ use flow matching~ to transport a source distribution to the target embedding, with FlowRec conditioning the source on the user's history. These methods improve the generative dynamics while retaining a flat item table, thereby avoiding discretization into code sequences but forgoing the cross-item parameter sharing induced by shared SID codewords. \ retains continuous generation while introducing that sharing through co-adaptive mixtures of prototypes.
