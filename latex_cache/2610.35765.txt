assumed to be similar when the same users consume them. Despite the sparsity and scale of interaction data, the implicit assumption across the field is that item co-occurrence reflects genuine shared preference—capturing what users like together. In this paper, we demonstrate that a substantial fraction of observed item-item similarity is an artifact of variance in user activity rather than shared taste. Even if every user consumed items completely independently—differing only in their total consumption volume—standard similarity measures still detect strong associations between items. We term this phenomenon : spurious item association that emerges strictly from heterogeneous user activity in the complete absence of preference structure. Starting from minimal formal assumptions, we show that independent consumption under varying activity is described by a Rasch model, and we derive how the resulting association depends on item popularity for each similarity measure. By comparing our theoretical baseline against real-world benchmark datasets, we reveal the extent to which popular similarity measures reflect phantom correlation, which bears on how item co-occurrence is interpreted and used in collaborative filtering. Item-item similarity is one of the oldest signals in recommender systems: two items are considered similar when the same users consume them~. It scales to large catalogs, and it stays reliable on sparse data, where interactions among more than two items are rarely observed~. For these reasons, item-item signals remain in use in many of the most successful recommender systems, and they are often used as a baseline to compare more complex models~. Item-item similarity drives recommendations on Web platforms such as streaming services, online shops and social media, where it is computed from logs of implicit feedback whose users differ widely in activity: understanding what these similarities measure is therefore a question about the Web data that recommender systems are built on. In practice, a user's history is multiplied by an item-item similarity matrix to score and rank the items. The simplest example is item-kNN~, which scores an item by adding up its similarities with the items in the user's history; SLIM~ learns a sparse similarity matrix by regression, and EASE~ learns a dense one in closed form. The idea behind item-item similarity is pretty intuitive: if two items are consumed together more often than expected by chance, it is because users like them together, and a similarity measure quantifies this information~. Another important aspect of the typical data of recommender systems is that the distribution of user activity is extremely heterogeneous: some users consume a handful of items, while others consume hundreds or thousands. The distribution of the history length of users is often described as heavy-tailed, and often modeled as a power law~. We show that a large part of the observed similarity can arise from these differences in activity alone, not from users liking items together. We call this phenomenon : like Simpson's paradox~ and confounding in recommendation~, it is association created by a hidden common factor, here activity. To better illustrate this, consider the following example: A small streaming service comprises films. Users on the platform like the movies equally, so no movie is preferred over another. But the activity differs between users: half of them are , who watch any given film with probability $ $. The other half are , who watch any given film with probability $ $. Every user picks films completely independently: watching one film says nothing about which other film they will watch. Trying to recommend films to users is futile: every film is equally good, and no two films are related to each other. Yet the recommender sees a different picture. If one computes the similarities between items, every pair turns out to be positively correlated. More precisely, movies appear together about $ $ times more often than chance would predict. This might seem counter-intuitive at first. There is basically no information about taste in the data, so how can it be that every pair of films is positively correlated? The main reason is that the similarity measure is not predicting the taste of the user, but rather how much they watch. Knowing that you have seen any film makes you more likely to be a binge watcher, and so more likely to see any other film. The phantom correlation phenomenon arises because three conditions coexist: Each user consumes items independently: nobody has a taste for pairs. A more active user is more likely to consume item. The only difference between users is how active they are. With identical items this is easy to model; with items of different popularity, we first have to formalize what it means to be ``the same as a casual viewer, only more active''. We formalize the three conditions, show that they lead to a unique model, derive its consequences for similarity measures, and measure how much of the association in real datasets it explains. The model we obtain is known in psychometrics as the Rasch model~, and heterogeneous activity is known to bias covariance and correlation in bipartite networks~. Our contributions are a derivation of the model from three assumptions on consumption, the exact dependence of the induced association on item popularity for lift, cosine and Pearson correlation, and a measurement of how much of the observed association activity alone explains, also for a learned model . Item-based collaborative filtering scores items by their similarity to the items a user consumed, measured by cosine, conditional probability or lift~, or learned by regression, as a sparse matrix in SLIM~ or a dense one in EASE~. Heterogeneity has long been handled by adjusting the measures: inverse user frequency down-weights items that many users liked~, and PMI is known to favor rare items~. We instead derive how much association differences in activity create, and how it depends on popularity for each measure. Recommenders tend to over-recommend popular items~, and this bias is usually studied in what a recommender outputs. We show that part of the popularity structure of item-item similarity is already in the data, before any learning, as a consequence of differences in activity. The activity model is the Rasch model of psychometrics~, here with a nonparametric distribution of the latent trait~; Cressie and Holland~ characterize the data it can produce, and positive association holds in every unidimensional model with monotone item curves~. Latent factor models of collaborative filtering~ use several dimensions to encode taste; we use a single one on purpose, to isolate what activity alone does. Saracco et al.~ project user-item networks onto items by keeping the pairs whose number of common users is unlikely under the bipartite configuration model, the maximum-entropy model with fixed expected degrees~. Its distribution is that of the activity model with one fixed parameter per user, so their expected co-occurrence already contains the association we study. We describe activity instead by a distribution over the population, estimated from all history lengths, and make the association it creates the object of study rather than a baseline to subtract. Degree-normalized projections~ and swap randomization~ also account for degrees, without describing the association that degrees induce. Closest to our work, Puccio et al.~ show that heterogeneous degrees bias the sample covariance and correlation in bipartite networks, positively and depending on the degrees of the two nodes, and propose corrected estimators; their analysis uses two classes of nodes and small heterogeneity, and does not cover lift, cosine or learned models. Where the activity model falls short, what remains is visibly structured by popularity and genuine preferences. Before continuing, we want to be clear about what we are claiming. We are not claiming that collaborative filtering signals are fake: activity explains the level of association, not which pairs stand out. We are not proposing a statistical test: the activity model is not a hypothesis to reject, but a mechanism whose consequences we want to understand. And we are not claiming that activity is the only source of popularity bias. What we claiming is that every similarity measure computed on implicit feedback mixes two things, and that one of them, the activity baseline, has a precise mathematical form. This matters for the choice of a similarity measure. Cosine similarity, the default in item-based collaborative filtering~, inherits a baseline that grows with popularity: whenever recommendation scores aggregate similarities, popular items receive a head start that has nothing to do with taste~. Lift and PMI inherit a baseline that is largest for rare items, which is consistent with their known tendency to favor rare items~. Whether and how much this baseline biases the final recommendations is the natural next question, and the subject of ongoing work. Where activity alone is not enough, as on Amazon Books and Last.fm, users differ not only in how much they consume, but also in how mainstream they are~; modeling that second dimension is beyond the scope of this paper. We started with a streaming service where nobody likes anything together, and every pair of films looked similar anyway. The reason was simple: some users watch more than others. We showed that this simple reason has a precise form. Users who differ only in activity must differ along one axis, along which the odds of every item grow by the same factor; this makes every pair of items positively associated, fixes the total amount of item-item covariance, and shapes it according to item popularity. On real data, this activity baseline accounts for the typical association between films, while genuine taste lives in the deviations from it.
