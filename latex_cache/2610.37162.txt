This underlies recent nugget-based evaluations, where answers are scored for completeness and grounding against nuggets: ARGUE for machine-generated reports , the AutoNuggetizer framework for TREC RAG , the Ragnar\"ok RAG evaluation framework , and nugget-grounded generation pipelines such as GINGER . CoverageBench provides a reusable resource for evaluating retrieval by standardizing topics, nuggets, nugget-level relevance labels, document collections, and baseline rankings across multiple retrieval settings. Crucially for a retrieval-focused benchmark, retrieval-side coverage metrics were shown to correlate strongly with the information coverage of downstream RAG responses , so evaluating coverage at the retrieval stage is a meaningful proxy for end-to-end RAG behavior. A related line of work probes whether RAG systems follow the corpus or fall back on parametric knowledge. Closest to our setting, CRUMQs augments RAG evaluation with unanswerable multi-hop queries, testing whether systems invoke parametric knowledge when the required evidence is absent . Building on the CoverageBench structure, CoverageBench-Negate poses a complementary question of whether systems still cover corpus-supported claims when those claims conflict with parametric knowledge. CRUMQs probes reliance on outside knowledge by removing support, while we probe it by adding contradicting, corpus-supported evidence. Work on factual evaluation states that atomic claims must be explicitly supported, not merely topically related . Yet dense retrievers, rerankers, and LLM-based query expansion tend to reward semantic similarity, fluent reformulation, and learned associations, and can be brittle under subtle, adversarially constructed perturbations . Negation is a particularly hard case. Recent analysis shows that dense retrievers may treat two passages as similar because they share a topic even when one states ``X'' and the other ``not X'' . Negation-sensitive ranking benchmarks such as NevIR find that retrievers struggle to separate a claim from its negation . In a corpus-grounded setting, however, the desired behavior is not to retrieve what is globally likely but what the corpus actually supports . Testing this cleanly requires paired evidence in which the context stays fixed while only the supported claim changes. Negation offers a practical way to construct such pairs, flipping the supported claim while leaving the topic, entities, and much of the document intact. Prior work shows that models struggle when negation changes entailment in controlled NLI settings , precisely the meaning-changing edit we exploit. This mirrors contrast sets, where small label-changing edits test whether a model responds to the changed meaning rather than surface form . We use negation as a controlled augmentation rather than a target in itself: each original nugget and supporting document is paired with a negated nugget and an edited document, with construction details in Section~. We provide baseline results for three first-stage retrievers to illustrate how CoverageBench-Negate can be used both as an augmented coverage benchmark and as a paired diagnostic resource. The benchmark-style evaluation follows CoverageBench directly. We add the edited documents to the collection and the negated nuggets with their qrels, then score top-20 rankings with $ $-nDCG~ and Subtopic Recall ~. Both are computed at rank~20: $ $-nDCG discounts the $j$-th document covering a nugget by $ ^{j-1}$, while StRecall is the fraction of a topic's nuggets covered in the top~20. For each retriever, query, and original--edited document pair, we compare the raw score assigned to the original document, $s_{orig}$, with the raw score assigned to the edited negation-supporting document, $s_{edit}$. We define the score gap as $ _s = s_{edit} - s_{orig}$. A negative value means the retriever prefers the original document, while a positive value means it prefers the negated document. This analysis uses retriever-specific subsets of the paired evidence set where both $s_{orig}$ and $s_{edit}$ are available in the top-1000 retrieved documents. The two scores are compared within the same retriever and query setting, so the sign of $ _s$ gives a direct pairwise preference signal, though the raw magnitudes are comparable only within a retriever. Coverage-evaluation benchmarks quantify whether retrieved evidence covers the different pieces of information needed to satisfy an information need. Existing benchmarks mostly consider factual claims that may already be known to pretrained retrieval components, leaving open whether retrievers find corpus evidence when it contradicts those priors. We study how retrievers behave when their parametric knowledge and the information in a corpus disagrees. To do so, we develop a process to augment a nugget-based coverage benchmark with controlled contradictions. For each nugget, we create a negated counterpart and edit its supporting documents to support that negation while preserving the context. Applying this process to CoverageBench, a benchmark measuring information coverage, we create CoverageBench-Negate, spanning six datasets and roughly 63{,}000 original--edited evidence pairs . We attach rich construction metadata to every pair and additionally provide out-of-family LLM judgments over all pairs and a human audit of a sample. In baseline experiments, BM25 separates original from edited near-twin evidence substantially better than dense retrievers , which stay near chance. We release the benchmark on Hugging Face Datasets together with the construction materials, including the prompts used to create it. Retrieval-augmented generation systems rely heavily on the information they retrieve. If the retrieved documents repeat the same point or leave out an important claim, the final answer can be incomplete even when every document is relevant. Retrieval quality, thus, depends not only on finding relevant documents, but on finding a complete set of distinct evidence to support an answer. Rather than focusing on whether each retrieved document is relevant, coverage evaluation asks whether the retrieved set contains the information needed to answer a query. This information is often represented as : small, atomic claims that a complete answer should cover . Existing coverage benchmarks typically evaluate ordinary historical or world facts that commonly appear in the data used to train language models. Meanwhile, modern retrieval pipelines increasingly build on those same language models. Many dense retrievers, neural rerankers, and LLM-based query expansion methods inherit, directly or indirectly, the world knowledge absorbed during pretraining. Consequently, they may favor the more familiar version of a claim over the evidence that is actually present in the corpus . This raises the question: To study this, we develop a general process for augmenting a nugget-based coverage benchmark with controlled contradictions. The process keeps the original topics fixed, and for each nugget, it constructs a negated counterpart. Then, it edits the supporting documents so they support the negation while preserving the surrounding context. Since the topics, entities, and contexts stay fixed, this paired design isolates the polarity flip from confounds such as topic or entity shift. Applying it to CoverageBench , we create CoverageBench-Negate. It covers six datasets: 284 topics and roughly 63{,}000 edited document pairs, spanning every topic and 2,975 nuggets. Each evidence pair is released with quality metadata so researchers can apply strict or permissive filters depending on the diagnostic setting. The resource consists of the following components: We reuse all 284 CoverageBench topics without modification, and retain the 3,496 nuggets and 116,842 nugget--document support links. A link is a tuple $ $: document $d$ supports nugget $n$ under topic $t$, and 3,234 nuggets have at least one supporting document. Keeping the originals lets the benchmark continue to measure ordinary information coverage alongside the negated coverage. For every nugget $n$, we create one negated nugget $$ that contradicts the original claim while preserving the topic scope, entities, and event context . For each support link, we attempt to create an edited document $$ that supports $$ instead of $n$, changing only the target claim while keeping the context intact. Editing is per link rather than per unique document since one source document can support several nuggets. After pre-filtering and failed edits, the 116,842 links yield 63,372 edited documents . The main artifact is a link-level paired evidence set of 63,372 pairs spanning every topic, and 2,975 original nuggets. Each pair connects an original topic, nugget, and supporting document with a negated nugget, edited document, and construction metadata. At every stage of the pipeline, we record diagnostics , validation outcomes , and categorical labels , so that users can filter the benchmark to their needs. Some checks determine what enters the benchmark; the rest are labels users can filter on . For each nugget, we prompt the LLM to write a negated version such that the original and the negation cannot both be true. Two LLM checks then refine the result. A single validation call scores three criteria: whether the negation reverses the original meaning, reads naturally as a standalone claim, and preserves the relevant entities. A separate mutual-exclusivity call asks whether the original and negated nuggets could both be true, regenerating the negation from the failed attempt up to three times. Separately, an embedding check labels each negation by whether it stays on-topic yet meaningfully different from the original and whether it collides with another gold nugget in the same topic. These checks label quality rather than filter, and no nugget is removed at this stage. Editing operates per nugget--document link . We first remove links whose negated nugget was flagged as colliding with another gold nugget in the same topic. Next, the LLM localizes the verbatim span that supports the original nugget; if it finds none, the link is excluded. The model then rewrites only that span so the document supports the negated nugget instead. Deterministic checks verify the edit's length, the absence of synthetic-edit language, and the removal of the original span. A separate LLM judge confirms that the edited document no longer supports the original nugget, does support the negated nugget, and preserves the surrounding context. An edit that fails any check receives one failure-aware retry . For example, to support the negated nugget ``WBC count often remains elevated even after the infection has cleared,'' the editor rewrites the supporting sentence `` the child's WBC count will return to normal.'' into `` the child's WBC count often remains elevated,'' flipping only the target claim while leaving the rest of the document intact.
