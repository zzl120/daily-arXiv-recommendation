In this work, we introduced a practical framework for cross-domain sequential recommendation that transfers knowledge through learned user representations, without sharing raw interaction data or item embeddings across domains. Our SASRec-based architecture, combined with a lightweight fusing layer and vertical federated fine-tuning of the source encoder, enables effective alignment of source and target user preferences under realistic privacy and deployment constraints. Extensive experiments across five datasets, including two industrial-scale benchmarks and three established academic benchmarks, demonstrate consistent improvements over strong single-domain and cross-domain baselines, for both shared and target-only users, confirming that representation-level transfer not only avoids negative transfer but also benefits the entire user population. Future work includes extending the framework to multi-source and multi-task settings, exploring alternative fusion mechanisms and backbone architectures, and integrating privacy-preserving mechanisms to protect the exchanged data during learning. FLADRec was essentially designed to achieve cross-domain knowledge transfer between different subsidiaries under a shared ecosystem. The federated training process operates in a distributed storage environment with mild privacy restrictions. Thus, the proposed architecture lack a dedicated mechanism to prevent user history reconstruction attacks. However, we obtain a representation-transfer mechanism that naturally operates effectively whether representations are exchanged via secure channels or directly , . To secure the training process where gradients are transferred back to the source domain, stronger privacy guarantees, such as formal differential privacy, can be integrated into the federated training protocol. In this section, we identify and analyze five prevalent pitfalls in benchmarking pipelines for cross‑domain recommendation models, which substantially undermine their practical reliability in industrial settings when compared with single‑domain baselines. As illustrated in our scalability experiments , we took steps to avoid as many of these pitfalls as possible. Large companies such as Amazon and Douban release anonymized user-item datasets split into categories, which researchers repurpose to simulate cross-domain recommendation. However, these datasets originate from a single platform, where a shared environment and shared models have access to all data simultaneously. Recently, more "honest" datasets have been released, notably the MegaMarket--Zvuk pair~ and TenRec~ are collected from genuinely separate platforms and therefore produced by independent models. Most CDR research assumes shared users or items across domains and links their representations accordingly. In practice, overlap is limited, creating non-uniform conditions under which models must handle shared and non-shared users differently. Most researchers in the CDR field run experiments on datasets with unrealistically high or even full user overlap, and insufficiently discuss the disparity in quality improvement between shared and unshared users. Notably, recent work has begun addressing quality fairness between shared and non-shared users~. Many recent CDR papers still rely on small, heavily processed datasets , especially when controlling the user overlap ratio. Replicating most such models in real-world settings is typically computationally infeasible. Since recommender systems research is inherently practical, comparisons against baselines evaluated only on small datasets are of limited value, even when those baselines appear to outperform others in that restricted regime. Sampled metrics have grown popular because they enable fast computation by ranking relevant items against a random subset of negatives rather than the full catalog. However, this approach can distort model comparisons, and several works have proposed corrections for this bias~. Sampling each user's most recently interacted item is a common evaluation practice in recommender systems. It is, however, frequently applied to non-temporal or temporally inconsistent data splits, introducing a risk of temporal leakage~. Nevertheless, temporal leakage multiple domains remains insufficiently investigated to date. Single-domain recommender systems have achieved significant progress through deep learning architectures like Transformers and self-supervised learning techniques~, which effictively capture the dynamic user preferences. For the case of many domains, particularly with partial user overlap, sequential cross-domain recommendation have emerged. These method concentrate on transferring knowledge from source domains to improve recommendation quality in the target domain~ for the next-item prediction task. A common line of work assumes the presence of users shared across domains and transfers knowledge through shared user representations. Models such as CD-SASRec~, DCDIR~, and MIFN~ employ domain-aware encoders or attention mechanisms to fuse sequences from multiple domains. More recent CDSR approaches focus on transferring knowledge at the representation level, by aligning latent user embeddings via contrastive objectives or partially sharing model parameters across domains. C$^2$DSR~ utilized contrastive learning through domain-specific and domain-invariant tasks. ABXI~ uses task-guided LoRA modules to identify domain-invariant user interests and finetune the models in both source and target domains. AutoCDSR~ utilizes self-attention to optimize the cross-domain knowledge transfer and combat the negative transfer caused by imbalanced signals from domains. These methods typically rely on joint training over all domains, including the usage of merged interaction sequences across domains and exploit shared users to bridge domain gaps. The increasing focus on privacy has spurred research into federated recsys, where models are trained across decentralized data sources without exchanging raw user interactions. PPCDR~ proposes a scheme that shares user embeddings protected by local differential privacy technique. However, this method provides learning schemes for domains with full user overlap, which rarely happens in real-world services. FedCDR~ addresses cross-domain recommendation in federated settings but focuses on rating prediction rather than sequential next-item prediction, limiting its applicability to modern sequential recommendation systems. Our approach bridges critical gaps between cross-domain recommendation and privacy-preserving learning. Unlike prior CDSR methods that require sharing raw sequences or item mappings, our framework transfers knowledge exclusively through distilled user representations, never exchanging raw interactions or embedding tables between domains. Unlike methods that assume full user overlap, our architecture naturally accommodates partial overlap through a fusion mechanism that seamlessly converts to single-domain recommendation when source data is missing. Our lightweight adapter enables cross-domain knowledge transfer while maintaining strict domain separation, making it deployable in federated learning scenarios where existing methods fail. Furthermore, our framework uniquely benefits all users, not just those with cross-domain activity. Through joint training with shared parameters, target-domain-only users indirectly benefit from the regularization effect of cross-domain signals, achieving performance gains. This dual benefit represents a practical advance toward deployable cross-domain recommendation systems that respect privacy boundaries while maximizing utility. A key property of the proposed framework is its ability to jointly improve performance for users with and without source-domain interaction histories. For users lacking source-domain data, the model naturally reduces to a standard single-domain sequential recommender, as the fusion step is skipped and the target encoder operates on unmodified target item embeddings. Crucially, however, these users still benefit indirectly from the training procedure. During joint training, the target encoder is exposed to both: fused sequences, where target items are conditioned on transferred source preferences, and unfused sequences, corresponding to users without source histories. As a result, the target encoder learns a representation space that is simultaneously compatible with conditioned and unconditioned inputs. This shared optimization encourages the model to learn more robust and generalizable sequential patterns in the target domain, improving its ability to model user behavior even when no source-domain information is available. From a representation learning standpoint, the presence of fused sequences acts as a form of implicit regularization, guiding the target encoder toward preference-aware dynamics without imposing additional constraints at inference time. Consequently, the framework not only supports the partial-overlap scenario, but can also improve recommendation quality for users observed exclusively in the target domain. Although users' embeddings transferred from the source are valuable to enhance recommendations in the target domain, they are originally encoded to solve a different task . However, these embeddings may not be optimal for the task of cross-domain knowledge transfer. Recent advances in deep sequential models, such as Transformer-based architectures, have significantly improved recommendation quality within a single domain~. However, in many real-world scenarios, user interaction histories in an individual domain are sparse or incomplete, limiting the effectiveness of even the most expressive sequential models. Cross-domain sequential recommendation addresses this limitation by transferring knowledge across domains in which the same users interact with different item spaces, such as e-commerce, media consumption, or online services~. By leveraging behavioral signals from a source domain, CDSR methods seek to improve recommendation performance in a target domain, particularly for users with limited interaction histories. Existing CDSR approaches commonly rely on shared item spaces, joint training on merged interaction data, or pretraining and fine-tuning strategies. While effective in controlled settings, these methods often require strong assumptions that are difficult to satisfy in practice, such as user/item alignment across domains or unrestricted access to raw interaction data~. Moreover, parameter-transfer approaches may suffer from negative transfer or catastrophic forgetting when domain distributions differ substantially~. Practical deployment of CDSR systems, therefore, faces two fundamental challenges. First, interaction data transfer across domains is often prohibited due to storage or data ownership concerns, making centralized modeling of raw interaction sequences from different domains infeasible~. We formalize this setting as a problem~, where domains possess distinct feature spaces but partially overlapping users, and only intermediate representations rather than raw interaction sequences may be exchanged. Second, user overlap between domains is typically partial, and many users may have no interaction history in the source domain at all~. Effective CDSR models must consequently operate under and remain robust to . To address these challenges, we propose FLADRec , a representation-level cross-domain sequential recommendation framework. Our key idea is to transfer cross-domain knowledge , rather than raw interactions or shared item embeddings. In the proposed framework, the source-domain encoder produces a fixed-dimensional user representation from its interaction sequence. To enable effective transfer, we introduce a lightweight fusing mechanism that conditions target-domain sequence modeling on a projected source-domain user representation. The transferred representation is aligned to the target embedding space and injected directly into the target sequence, allowing the target encoder to . This design enforces strict separation between domains while enabling early and flexible integration of source-domain information. Importantly, the proposed framework naturally supports users without source-domain interaction histories. Our main contributions are: We propose FLADRec, a framework for CDSR based purely on representation-level transfer. FLADRec transfers only fixed-dimensional user embeddings, without merging raw interaction sequences across domains, enabling deployment under vertical federated learning constraints. We design an effective conditional fusion mechanism that integrates projected source-domain user representations into target-domain sequence modeling, enabling adaptive preference transfer under strict data separation. We demonstrate the capability of FLADRec to enhance recommendations for all target users, including those with no source-domain history through implicit regularization, even without explicit cross-domain transfer at inference. Cross-domain sequential recommendation aims to improve next-item prediction in a target domain by leveraging user behavior from auxiliary source domains. However, real-world deployment faces two critical challenges: business constraints that isolate interaction data across domains, and user heterogeneity, where many users lack cross-domain activity. This makes existing cross-domain methods difficult to apply. Importantly, when source data is unavailable , the model seamlessly defaults to a robust target-only sequential recommender, retaining benefits from joint training across all users.
