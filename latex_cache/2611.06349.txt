These agents draw on users' past item interactions and feedback to infer preferences that may not be stated in the current request~. Advances in multimodal LLMs support extending these interactions to image--text queries~. For example, a user can ask for clothing with the pattern shown in a reference image. Such image--text queries, already supported by Web shopping interfaces such as Amazon's Alexa for Shopping~, have become a new way to interact with recommendation services and are also explored in recommendation research~. Personalization in this setting therefore requires agents to meet the current image--text requirements while actively accounting for preferences inferred from the user's interaction history~. However, existing benchmarks offer limited evidence on whether agents follow implicit preferences reflected in users’ interaction histories when responding to multimodal queries. Representative personalized recommendation benchmarks evaluate preference-aligned item selection under textual queries~. Multimodal queries make this selection harder: agents must first interpret the requirements conveyed by reference images, and it remains unclear how agents combine preference modeling with this interpretation. Conversely, multimodal benchmarks assess image--text query matching without testing whether recommendations reflect the preferences in real user histories~. Yet multiple items may satisfy the same query while differing in their suitability for a particular user. Assessing personalization in this setting calls for queries whose text, reference images, and combination are grounded in real user behavior, so that the query introduces no requirement that conflicts with this user’s preferences. Even with such queries, randomly selected candidates, commonly used in recommendation evaluation~, may reward coarse query--item matching without adequately testing preference following~. Overall ranking metrics alone also cannot distinguish requirement understanding from preference following. Evaluation therefore needs challenging candidates and separate proxy measures for these two abilities. We introduce the ersonalized ultimodal ommendation mark to evaluate recommendation agents' personalization under image--text queries. Two complementary designs address these evaluation needs: We derive query requirements from consistent behavioral patterns in real user records, with reference images and targets linked to actual interactions. We construct challenging diagnostic candidate pools that pair a query-satisfying target rated highly by the user with two competitor groups: highly rated items that violate the query, and query-satisfying items the user disliked or never interacted with. The latter serve as proxies for preference misalignment. Two separate diagnostic metrics use the target's rankings relative to these groups as proxies for requirement understanding and preference following, respectively. pairs 510 image--text queries with the corresponding users' real histories from Amazon and MovieLens, covering physical products and digital content , respectively. Ranking candidate items therefore requires agents to interpret reference images for requirement understanding and infer user preferences from user histories. Our extensive experiments reveal a personalization imbalance in recommendation agents: {adding multimodal queries to personalization signals weakens preference following, even though the additional information about query requirements improves overall ranking performance.} This imbalance is masked by strong results on random candidate pools, where coarse query matching can often suffice. Specifically, ablations on diagnostic candidate pools show that agents follow preferences well with personalization signals alone, but adding multimodal queries lowers the mean preference-following score across four agents by approximately 4–8 percentage points, depending on the domain and backbone. Comparisons of query representations link this decline to image interpretation, while sampled failure traces show that agents can prioritize query-based cues, such as resemblance to reference images, over personalization signals. Upgrading the backbone alone does not resolve the imbalance: with the agent fixed to ReAct, the tested commercial models follow preferences less well than Gemma-4-31B despite comparable or stronger requirement understanding. These findings indicate that agents' demonstrated ability to use personalization signals is not consistently reflected in their final recommendations, a failure mode relevant to Web platforms serving image--text input.
