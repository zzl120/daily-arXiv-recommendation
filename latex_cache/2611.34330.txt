However, serendipity is increasingly viewed as a user experience shaped by both algorithmic outputs and system features. This study positions recommendations not as the primary goal users pursue but as an environmental affordance that creates opportunities for discovery during other activities. We propose RecField, a spatial movie organization interface where users freely arrange movies into collection zones. For each collection, RecField provides attribute-based recommendation lists labeled according to their recommendation perspectives. A between-subjects experiment examined the effects of recommendations on discoveries made during a foreground task of creating and organizing personal movie profiles. Although recommendations did not significantly affect environment-level perceived serendipity, ratings of encounter-related aspects tended to be higher, with small effect sizes. At the individual-movie level, favorites in the recommendation condition received significantly higher ratings for stimulating the desire to watch and broadening preferences, but not for novelty or unexpectedness. Traditional research on recommender systems has focused primarily on accurately predicting users' ratings and behaviors. However, the limitations of evaluating these systems solely in terms of predictive accuracy have been widely recognized , prompting interest in beyond-accuracy perspectives, such as diversity, novelty, and serendipity . Among these perspectives, serendipity has attracted particular attention as a means of counteracting the narrowing of content exposure associated with accuracy-oriented recommendations. It refers to encounters with items that are both unexpected and useful to users . Most studies on serendipity-oriented recommender systems have focused on algorithms for recommending serendipitous items and on metrics for measuring them . In contrast, argued that serendipity in recommender systems should not be narrowly regarded as an algorithmic performance metric, but as a user experience influenced by a variety of mechanisms operating outside the algorithm. Information behavior research views serendipity not as mere luck but as arising from the interaction between people and their environment; a phenomenon that can be influenced by environmental design to some extent . Serendipitous discoveries are often unplanned, occurring as people pursue goals other than the fortuitous revelation . Recommendations can be designed not as the primary goal that users actively pursue, but as environmental affordances embedded within another ongoing task to offer opportunities for serendipitous encounters. People are known to classify information rigorously and arrange it spatially while its classification remains ambiguous, gradually discovering structure from the arrangement . Moreover, collections such as folders and albums are fundamental means by which individuals manage information and curate their interests . As a user compiles a collection, its organizing criteria implicitly emerge. Presenting related items based on these perspectives can be expected to foster encounters with new items aligned with the user's organizational context. However, most studies on interactive recommender systems and serendipity-fostering user interfaces investigate tools explicitly designed for recommendation or exploration , while research on spatial information organization has primarily addressed arranging information that users already possess. The role of an embedded recommendation feature in shaping users' serendipitous experiences during the foreground task of organizing has not been sufficiently clarified. This study therefore proposes RecField, an interface that enables users to freely place movies on a canvas called the field and organize them into collection zones. When a user forms a collection zone in RecField, the system presents recommendation lists of related movies. These lists are based on the similarity between the collected movies and those defined by attributes such as genre, series, production company, cast, director, and score composer. To isolate the effect of the recommendation feature itself, the recommendation logic was deliberately kept simple and a label was attached to each recommendation list indicating its key feature , for users to understand why the movies were presented. To verify the effect of the recommendation feature in RecField, a between-subjects experiment was conducted that manipulated its presence. Following the experimental design proposed by , this study focused its evaluation on movies that participants discovered and added to their favorites while performing a foreground task of creating and organizing their own movie profiles. Both the serendipity of the entire system environment, based on the scale proposed by , and that of individual movies, based on the questionnaire items of were measured. Furthermore, interaction logs were used to identify the path by which each favorite was added and analyze movies selected separately from those added via other routes. Specifically, the study addresses the following research questions: Does the presence of the recommendation feature in RecField affect the perceived serendipity of the system environment as a whole? Does the presence of the recommendation feature affect the perceived serendipity of the movies that users add to their favorites? Is the serendipity effect of the recommendation feature limited to favorite movies added directly from recommendation results, or does it extend to favorite movies added via other routes? The main contributions of this study are as follows: Recommendations are positioned as environmental affordances that offer opportunities for encounters during organizing activities. Based on this perspective, RecField was designed and implemented as a spatial movie organization interface that presents attribute-labeled recommendation lists for each user-created collection. A between-subjects experiment involving a foreground organizing task was conducted to evaluate the effects of the recommendation on perceived serendipity at two levels: the entire system environment and individual favorite movies. The findings show that the effects of the recommendation feature are not limited to movies added directly from recommendation lists. Similar effects were observed in ratings of movies added through other pathways, suggesting that recommendations can contribute to serendipitous experiences through indirect routes. Finally, this study is positioned in terms of these previous studies . Serendipity has long been discussed as the accidental discovery of something one was not looking for . In library and information science, serendipity and information encountering have been studied as forms of information behavior . These studies treat serendipity not as mere luck but as a phenomenon that arises from interactions between people and their environment that can be cultivated to some extent . Based on interviews, identified strategies that people use to increase their own chances of encountering serendipity. also attempted to directly observe the process by which serendipitous discoveries occur in digital information environments. Regarding the design of environments that support seren-dipity, proposed a framework linking environmental and personal factors. This framework identifies three key affordances that environments should provide: diversifiability , traversability , and sensoriability . developed a scale measuring the characteristics of digital environments that foster serendipity and examined how environmental and personal characteristics affect serendipitous experiences. Other work has discussed design guidelines for serendipity-fostering tools , interaction patterns , and practitioners' motivations when designing for serendipity . Building on these insights, this study positions the recommendation feature as an environmental affordance that offers opportunities for encounters during users' exploration and organization activities, not as mere algorithmic output. In the evaluation, the scale proposed by is also used to assess the environment as a whole. In recommender systems research, the limitations of evaluation focused solely on predictive accuracy have been pointed out . In response, beyond-accuracy objectives such as diversity, novelty, coverage, and serendipity have been discussed . Among these, serendipity has attracted attention as a countermeasure against the content narrowing caused by accuracy-oriented recommendation, commonly known as the filter bubble . Research on serendipity-oriented recommender systems has been summarized in several surveys . Many early studies focused on algorithms for recommending serendipitous items and on metrics for measuring them. For example, methods have been proposed for introducing serendipity into content-based recommendation , for considering diversity, novelty, and serendipity in music recommendation , and for enhancing serendipity by fusing two items that a user likes . Metrics for evaluating the serendipity of recommendation lists have also been proposed . Meanwhile, other studies treat serendipity as something perceived by users and aim to clarify its components and effects through user evaluations. defined serendipity as a combination of relevance, novelty, and unexpectedness. They then surveyed real users of the movie recommendation service MovieLens and showed that serendipitous items are associated with the broadening of user preferences. Through a large-scale user survey, showed that novelty and unexpectedness affect user satisfaction via perceived serendipity. analyzed the effects of item features and users' personal characteristics on perceived serendipity. More recently, there have been studies revisiting the definition of serendipity in recommender systems in light of its original meaning of ``fortunate discovery'' . argued that serendipity in recommender systems should not be narrowly regarded as an algorithmic performance metric, but rather understood as a user experience influenced by a variety of mechanisms operating outside the algorithm. They also classified the mechanisms that foster serendipity and proposed an experimental design for evaluating them. As in , this study takes the position that serendipity is a user experience. The recommendation algorithm is deliberately kept simple and transparent and the effect of embedding the recommendation feature in the interface itself is examined. For evaluating individual items, the questionnaire items of are used. In contrast to conventional recommendation, which unilaterally presents a ranked list, research has sought to support recommendation and exploration through interaction between users and systems. $\\$ surveyed research on user interfaces that foster serendipity and organized their design patterns. The Bohemian Bookshelf by provides multiple entry points into a book collection through several coordinated visualizations, supporting serendipitous book discovery. proposed a system that encourages the rediscovery of songs not listened to for a while by visualizing personal music collections. Other proposals include a browser that highlights information on the web page being viewed based on the user's own past experiences , a faceted browser for Linked Open Data , collaborative exploration of book repositories on a tabletop , and an exploration interface for large-scale video archives . In recommender systems, interfaces that let users slide-adjust recommendations' degrees of serendipity and methods for visualizing serendipitous recommendations have also been investigated. $\\$ Interactive recommender systems emphasize improving the transparency of recommendations and users' controllability over them . TasteWeights by is a hybrid recommender system that enables users to interactively adjust the weights of multiple information sources. showed that visualizing set relationships among recommendations encourages user exploration. Moodplay is an interface that provides recommendations while users explore music in a mood space. proposed a visual interface that goes beyond ranked lists to support user-driven exploration and diversification. Other studies present recommendations by arranging items on a two-dimensional map or in a three-dimensional space . In the movie domain, MovieExplorer enables users to explore movies based on a latent preference space derived from rating data. There has also been an attempt to let MovieLens users adjust the characteristics of recommendation algorithms themselves . The relationship between the degree of user control and users' personal characteristics has also been analyzed . Being able to understand the reasons for recommendations is an important factor in user trust and acceptance . Accordingly, methods that render recommendation results explainable and steerable have also been proposed . $\\$ Designs that group recommendations by category or perspective, rather than presenting them as a single list, have also been investigated. proposed an organization interface that groups recommended items by trade-off perspectives and demonstrated its effectiveness. compared single-list, grid, and multi-list interfaces and examined their effects on choice overload . RecField likewise presents multiple recommendation lists, each with an attribute-based label, for each user-created collection. $\\$ Most of these studies target interfaces whose main purpose is for exploration or to obtain recommendations. In contrast, this study embeds a recommendation feature in an environment where users have a different goal , namely organizing movies by themselves. It further differs in that it examines how the recommendation feature affects the user experience in that environment. How people organize information has been extensively studied. observed how people organize documents on office desks and found that they arrange information spatially as rigorously classified ``files'' but also in loosely classified ``piles.'' He also showed that spatial arrangement serves to remind people of tasks to complete. In research on spatial hypertext, VIKI was proposed to support users in freely arranging information objects on a two-dimensional plane and in letting structure gradually emerge from the arrangement. indicated the problems of imposing formal structure on users at an early stage and argued the importance of incrementally formalizing informal representations. In personal information management, ``collections'' such as folders and albums are seen as fundamental tools for organizing data . A shift from information consumption to curation has also been noted . Furthermore, beyond lookup searches for specific information, studies on exploratory search have emphasized the importance of exploration involving learning and investigation . However, these studies on information organization focus on how users organize information they already possess. Embedding a recommendation feature into the organization process to foster encounters with new items remains underexplored. Based on the above analysis, the positioning of this study is summarized as follows. First, whereas most research on serendipity-oriented recommender systems has focused on improving algorithms, this study, as argued by , regards serendipity as a user experience that is also influenced by other mechanisms. Based on this, the effect of embedding a recommendation feature into a spatial organization interface is examined. Second, whereas most research on interactive recommender systems and serendipity-fostering interfaces has aimed at exploration or obtaining recommendations per se, this study focuses on encounters that occur during the foreground task of organizing movies. Third, in the evaluation, both the perceived serendipity of the entire environment and the same for individual items are measured. Moreover, by analyzing items added directly from recommendation lists separately from those added via other routes, the indirect effects of the recommendation feature are clarified. RecField is a web application that embeds a recommendation feature into an environment where users organize movies spatially. Users organize their movies by freely placing them on a canvas called the field and grouping related movies into collection zones. Once a collection zone is formed, recommendation lists are presented for each collection based on the attributes of the movies it contains. Differently expressed, in RecField, recommendations are not the primary goal that users actively pursue but are positioned as affordances that offer opportunities for encounters during the foreground task of organizing. RecField adopts a simple recommendation logic that emphasizes transparency so that users can clearly understand why each item was recommended in response to their actions. For example, when a user forms a collection of movies directed by Steven Spielberg, his movies are presented as a recommendation list; when a user forms a collection of movies from the Harry Potter series, these movies are presented. Each recommendation list is given a label indicating its perspective. This enables users not only to accept recommendations as they are, but also to reconsider the perspective of their own collection or switch to a different perspective. Collection-based recommendation is performed through the following steps: All items are clustered in advance from multiple perspectives . At the same time, each extracted cluster is given a label representing its content. When a user forms a collection, all clusters containing every movie in the collection are extracted as candidate clusters. For each candidate cluster, the movies not already included in the collection are used as recommendation candidates . For each candidate cluster, the recommendation candidates are presented as a recommendation list for the collection. This section describes the recommendation logic. Participants were recruited through the following procedure. On Lancers}, a Japanese crowdsourcing service, a pre-screening and recruitment questionnaire was posted. Responses from 500 people were obtained. The pre-screening questionnaire collected information regarding demographic attributes such as age and gender, as well as experience with PCs and recommendation services. For the user study, the 500 respondents were asked to complete a questionnaire about their movie-watching frequency and habits. Responses from 400 of them were obtained. From the 400 respondents, 210 respondents who watched movies ``at least one day a month'' and had watched at least one movie in the past year were selected. From the 210 respondents, 80 respondents were randomly selected. The 80 selected respondents were randomly assigned to two groups of 40 each and asked to participate in the task described in Section~. In total, $N = 57$ participants completed the task: $N_{A} = 29$ in Group A and $N_{B} = 28$ in Group B. Of the 57 participants, 43 were men, and 14 were women, aged 26--71 years . Even when restricted to movies added via routes other than recommendation lists, Group A gave significantly higher ratings than Group B for ``Motivational Novelty'' and ``Preference Broadening'' . ``Viewing experience'' was also significantly lower in Group A . These effect sizes were nearly identical to those obtained when comparing all of Group A with Group B . Therefore, the between-group differences observed in RQ2 were not driven by the 15 movies added directly from recommendation lists, but appeared to a similar degree in favorites that users added themselves via other routes. In summary, the answer to RQ3 is as follows. Favorites added directly from recommendation lists accounted for only 14\ In contrast, the between-group differences in F3 and F9 were observed to a similar degree even when restricted to favorites added via routes other than recommendations. That is, the serendipity effect of the recommendation feature was not limited to movies added directly from recommendation lists but extended to movies added via other routes. This result suggests that, rather than directly delivering ``completely unexpected movies,'' the recommendation feature prompted users to search for and re-evaluate related movies, thereby indirectly stimulating their desire to watch and broadening their preferences. No significant difference in the perceived serendipity of the system environment as a whole was found between conditions with and without the recommendation feature. Given that the median was 4 for many items in both groups, RecField's basic interaction of creating zones and spatially placing and organizing movies appears, in itself, to have been accepted to a certain degree as an environment that fosters serendipity, regardless of the recommendation feature. Nevertheless, the direction of the effect sizes showed a consistent pattern. Group A rated items related to higher, such as ``Unexpected Encounters'' and ``Attention to Items of Interest'' , whereas Group B rated items related to slightly higher, such as ``Ease of Organization'' and ``Ease of Exploration'' . This result suggests that, while the recommendation feature provides users with new opportunities, it may be in a partial trade-off with users' sense of organizing and exploring the field according to their own intentions. In the ratings of favorite movies, Group A gave significantly higher ratings for ``Motivational Novelty'' and ``Preference Broadening'' , and reported significantly less ``Viewing Experience'' . In contrast, no significant differences were found for ``Awareness Novelty'' or unexpectedness . Whereas conceptualize serendipity as a combination of relevance, novelty, and unexpectedness, what the recommendation feature enhanced in this experiment was not ``unexpected discovery'' in the strict sense, but rather aspects obtained as of serendipity, namely stimulating the desire to watch and broadening preferences. The low unexpectedness ratings in both groups can be attributed to the fact that the foreground task focused on organizing known movies, so participants mainly added known movies to their favorites. Differently expressed, rather than facilitating encounters with movies entirely new to users, the recommendation feature can be interpreted as having led users to re-evaluate known movies, or movies close to their existing interests, in the sense of ``I want to watch this again'' or ``my preferences have broadened.'' Favorites added directly from recommendation lists accounted for only 14\ In contrast, even when restricted to favorites added via routes other than recommendation lists, the differences between Groups A and B in F3 and F9 were observed with nearly the same effect sizes as in the comparison using all of Group A. This result suggests that the serendipity effect of the recommendation feature arose through the route of directly accepting recommended movies, and through an in which the presentation of recommendations prompted users to recall and search for related movies and to re-evaluate movies on the field. In addition, movies added from recommendation lists had slightly higher mean ratings for ``Awareness Novelty'' and ``Recommendation Unexpectedness'' , but slightly lower ratings for ``Discovery Unexpectedness'' and ``Preference Unexpectedness'' . This suggests that, owing to RecField's design of presenting recommendations based on the movies in a zone, recommended movies were , which may be one reason why direct acceptance of recommendations was limited. This study has the following limitations. First, among the affordances provided by RecField, we manipulated only the presence of the recommendation feature. The effects of other affordances were not examined. Besides recommendations, RecField offers several features that can serve as opportunities for encounters, such as free placement of movies on the field, creation and labeling of collection zones, the search panel, and the overview map. Because both groups had access to these features in this experiment, whether the median of 4 for many items in both groups in RQ1 was due to the spatial organization environment itself or to individual features could not be determined. Moreover, as indicated by Group B's slightly higher ratings for ease of organization and exploration in RQ1, the recommendation feature may interact with other interface elements; thus, caution is needed when attributing observed effects to the recommendation feature alone. Second, for the recommendation algorithm, a simple method that emphasizes transparency was adopted so that users can easily understand the reasons for recommendations. The effects of different recommendation algorithms were not examined. RecField identifies attribute-based clusters that contain all movies in the collection and presents the remaining movies in each cluster as a recommendation list, without explicitly considering preference estimation accuracy, diversity, or unexpectedness. The absence of significant differences in unexpectedness ratings in RQ2---and the tendency in RQ3 for movies added from recommendation lists to be unknown yet still within users' preferred types---may stem from these algorithmic characteristics. Consequently, it remains unclear whether similar effects would emerge under alternative recommendation strategies. Third, sample size and the unit of analysis also impose limitations. With approximately 28 participants per group, this experiment does not have sufficient statistical power to detect small effects. In RQ1 in particular, although the direction of the effect sizes showed a consistent pattern, no statistically significant differences were found. Verification with larger samples is needed to determine whether these tendencies reflect the effect of the recommendation feature. Furthermore, the item-level analyses in RQ2 and RQ3 treated individual movie ratings as independent observations, although multiple movies were evaluated by the same participant. This within-participant dependency may affect the statistical inference. Therefore, the results of these analyses should be interpreted with caution. Future work includes the following. First, the effects of affordances other than the recommendation feature on serendipity should be examined. For example, by manipulating the presence of spatial placement and collection zones and comparing the interface with a list-based organization interface, the contribution of the spatial organization environment itself can be clarified. It would also be possible to position each feature regarding diversifiability, traversability, and sensoriability as identified by , and to examine their interactions with the recommendation feature. Second, the effects of different recommendation methods should be examined. In addition to the current similarity-based recommendation method, future work will implement recommendation methods that incorporate diversity and unexpectedness and compare their effects on serendipity ratings. In particular, the investigation will examine whether presenting movies that are moderately distant from the zone context, while maintaining transparency through labels indicating the recommendation perspective, can facilitate preference broadening and unexpected discoveries. Third, the relationship between users' organizing behavior, such as creating and labeling zones, and serendipity ratings should be analyzed. The interaction logs from this experiment indicate a tendency for Group B to perform more operations such as searching and editing zone labels. Clarifying how the presence of the recommendation feature affects users' organizing behavior and how this relates to serendipitous discoveries is an important direction for future work. Fourth, experiments will be conducted with larger numbers of participants and the tendencies observed in this study will be verified through analyses treating participants as random effects. Recording the sequence of user behaviors from exposure to recommendations to adding movies to favorites will help clarify the indirect process of searching and re-evaluation triggered by recommendations. Long-term usage studies will be conducted to examine how continued use of the recommendation feature affects the broadening of user preferences and actual viewing behavior. Finally, the applicability of the proposed approach to domains other than movies, such as books, music, and academic papers, will be examined. This study positions recommendations not as the primary goal users actively pursue, but as an environmental affordance for beneficial encounters while another objective is being sought. To facilitate the process, RecField, a spatial movie organization interface, was proposed and investigated. In RecField, when users place movies on the field and organize them into collection zones, recommendation lists labeled with their perspectives are presented based on the similarity between the collection and attribute-based movie clusters. Through a between-subjects experiment that manipulated the presence of the recommendation feature, the serendipity perceived during the foreground task of organizing movies was evaluated at two levels: the system environment as a whole and individual favorite movies. The experiment yielded the following findings. First, no significant difference was found in the perceived serendipity of the system environment as a whole between conditions with and without the recommendation feature . However, tendencies with small effect sizes were observed: i) the recommendation group---Group A--- rated items related to opportunities for encounters higher, such as ``Unexpected Encounters'' and ``Attention to Items of Interest,'' whereas ii) the no-recommendation group---Group B--- rated items related to user-driven operations slightly higher, such as ``Ease of Organization'' and ``Ease of Exploration.'' Second, for individual movies added to favorites, the recommendation group gave significantly higher ratings for stimulating the desire to watch and for broadening preferences, and a higher proportion of unwatched movies were added . In contrast, no significant differences were found in novelty or unexpectedness, indicating that the recommendation primarily prompted the re-evaluation of known movies, or movies close to existing interests, rather than unexpected discoveries. Third, favorites added directly from recommendation lists accounted for only 14\ These results suggest that recommendations embedded in an organizing interface may contribute to serendipitous experiences by leading users to directly accept recommended items and through an indirect route in which the presentation of recommendations prompts users' own searching and re-evaluation. This highlights the limitations of evaluating the value of recommendation features solely by direct metrics such as click-through or acceptance rates. It also demonstrates the usefulness of evaluating serendipity-fostering systems at both the environment and item level. Nevertheless, this study was based on whether or not the recommendation feature was present. It did not examine the effects of other affordances, such as spatial placement and collection zones. In addition, because a simple, transparency-oriented method was used for recommendation, the effects of different recommendation methods remain unclear. Furthermore, a sample size of approximately 28 participants per group is insufficient to detect small effects, and the tendencies observed in RQ1 have not been statistically confirmed. Future work will further clarify how recommendations embedded in organizing activities foster serendipitous discoveries by examining the effects of affordances other than recommendations. The proposed approach will be compared with recommendation methods that consider diversity and unexpectedness, and the relationship between users' organizing behavior and serendipity ratings will be analyzed.
