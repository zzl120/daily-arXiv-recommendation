 {inputenc} {fontenc} {natbib} {itemsep=0.2ex, topsep=0.5ex} {itemsep=0.2ex, topsep=0.5ex} {0.1em} } Setlist identification is the task of automatically determining which songs are performed during a live concert recording by matching segments of the concert audio against a database of known reference tracks. This is a specific application of , where the goal is to recognize a song despite the significant acoustic differences between a query song and its original version. Nowon focuses on research related to version identification and copyright infringement detection in AI-generated music. The production use case at Nowon imposes challenges that go beyond standard VI benchmarks: Concert recordings range from professional recordings to smartphone captures in noisy venues, introducing reverberation, crowd noise, clipping, and low-frequency rumble. Live performances often differ substantially from studio versions through key changes, tempo variations, extended solos, mashups, medleys, and shortened or rearranged sections. A single concert can last 1--3 hours, and the reference database may contain millions of candidate songs, requiring efficient retrieval. Unlike query-by-example retrieval where a clean query is provided to identify a single song, setlist identification must process the full concert timeline producing detections over time. This report provides an objective evaluation of three version identification systems on the ASID benchmark~, comparing their effectiveness under these conditions. Music version identification has been studied extensively in MIR. Early approaches used hand-crafted chroma features with sequence alignment ~. Deep embedding models then enabled scalable nearest-neighbor retrieval. MOVE~ and Re-MOVE~ learn compact embeddings from cremaPCP chroma; CQTNet~, ByteCover~, and CoverHunter~ explore alternative architectures and losses on standard cover-song benchmarks such as SHS100K~ and Da-TACOS~. More recently, CLEWS~ uses contrastive learning on segment-level embeddings with distance-reduction strategies for variable-length matching. The specific problem of setlist identification --- matching concert audio against references over a continuous timeline---has received less attention. An early study by ~ explicitly formulated the task as automatic setlist identification and song segmentation for full-length concert videos. Their approach sequentially identifies songs from a database of studio recordings and estimates their boundaries, and was evaluated on a dataset of 20 full concerts and 1,152 studio tracks. More recently, ~, revisit the task and introduce the dataset and benchmark. The method from ~ uses a sliding window over the concert audio and matches each window against the full reference database. Temporal postprocessing then merges consecutive detections of the same song and resolves overlaps by keeping the highest-confidence match. Optionally, an SVM trained on the development set filters remaining false positives. ~ evaluates Qmax~, 2DFTM~, and Re-MOVE~, with Re-MOVE obtaining the best performance. To our knowledge, ASID is the largest publicly available benchmarks specifically designed for setlist identification under the noisy, variable-quality conditions typical of live concerts. The ASID dataset was not available at the time of conducting this work. Therefore, we had to collect the original audio and regenerate the features to reproduce the results. However, some of the original concerts were not available anymore. Although several systems above outperform Re-MOVE on track-level cover-song benchmarks, they are rarely validated on long, noisy concerts. We therefore restrict this study to three systems that are most relevant to setlist identification: ~~, the strongest baseline in the original ASID benchmark~ and the closest published prior work on live-concert matching; ~~, a recent state-of-the-art VI embedding model; and ~, our internal system. Our goal is not an exhaustive comparison of all VI systems, but a fair comparison under the ASID protocol against the established baseline and a current SOTA embedding method. Re-MOVE~ is the strongest system in the original ASID paper. It operates on cremaPCP chroma features , produces fixed-dimensional embeddings via a metric-learning model, and scores query--reference pairs using cosine distance. We use the original authors' code and pre-trained model. CLEWS~ uses a contrastive learning framework producing segment-level embeddings. Scoring uses normalized squared Euclidean distance between all query--reference shingle pairs, reduced via bpwr-5 or bpwr-10. We use the publicly released checkpoint from Zenodo with the official inference code. CLEWS operates on raw audio and produces multiple embeddings of 1024 dimensions that represent each song. This raises a potential limitation on the evaluation protocol used in the previous ASID paper, and the need for a specific metric that allows to capture such difference. {image1.png} {image2.png} The most striking result is the ${ }12$ pp DAP improvement of all NAVE and CLEWS systems over Re-MOVE. This gap likely stems from three factors: CLEWS and NAVE models operate on Constant-Q Transform spectrogram representations rather than hand-crafted cremaPCP chroma, capturing richer timbral and spectral information. Rather than pooling an entire 120\,s window into a single embedding, CLEWS and NAVE produce sequences of shingle embeddings , enabling finer-grained temporal matching that is more robust to partial overlaps and arrangement changes. NAVE embeddings are 256-dimensional, a $4 $ reduction compared to CLEWS's 1024-dimensional shingles. This reduces storage and retrieval costs while slightly improving identification accuracy. The recognition scorer and the pooled scorer achieve similar DAP but differ in precision: Recognition: 280 FP vs.\ Pooled: 346 FP , a 24\ The DP alignment penalizes matches that lack temporal consistency, acting as an implicit false-positive filter. Pooled achieves slightly higher DAP by being more permissive, catching a few extra annotations at the cost of noise. The results confirm that audio quality significantly affects identification accuracy. AQ-C recordings are the hardest , consistent with the noise, reverberation, and spectral distortion typical of crowd-recorded audio. This validates that our evaluation covers realistic deployment conditions. In production systems, embedding size matters for indexing large catalogs. Here we provide a comparison of the storage efficiency between the different systems. Assuming float32 segment embeddings with a 20\,s window and 5\,s hop for both CLEWS and NAVE pipelines, a 5-minute reference yields 57 shingles. Under that setup, storing one million songs requires approximately for CLEWS versus for NAVE ---a $4 $ reduction with no loss in DAP and a slight gain in DLP . By comparison, Re-MOVE stores a single 256-D vector per track , but that compactness comes with the ${ }$12\,pp DAP gap observed on ASID. In production, the NAVE footprint also reduces memory for nearest-neighbor search and lowers I/O when streaming embeddings for long concerts, while retaining segment-level matching that Re-MOVE's single-vector representation does not provide. While this is the largest public setlist-ID benchmark, it may not capture the full diversity of production conditions. 3 eval concerts lacked audio at evaluation time, reducing the effective eval set to 62 concerts. To isolate the effect of the VI system, CLEWS and NAVE are evaluated using the same sliding-window and temporal postprocessing protocol. We do not train the original ASID SVM for these systems because its features and score distributions were designed around Re-MOVE. Some reference songs were unavailable or had extraction failures, potentially affecting recall for specific concerts. After looking closer to the output of the recognition systems, we see that the metrics used in the original ASID paper may not fully capture the temporal precision of the recognition between the boundaries. NAVE system already reach ${ }0.90$ DAP on ASID. At this level, small DAP differences seem hard to interpret and may reflect dataset limitations, more than clear model gains. We may be approaching a ceiling of the current ASID dataset/metrics. This evaluation demonstrates that NAVE embedding models substantially outperform Re-MOVE on setlist identification, achieving ${ }0.90$ DAP vs.\ ${ }0.78$ on the ASID benchmark. Nowon's NAVE recognition model with 256 dimensions improves the public CLEWS checkpoint in detection accuracy while being substantially smaller than CLEWS's 1024-dimensional representation. Music/non-music gating provides a modest additional improvement, and could be integrated as a lightweight preprocessing step in production. Through qualitative analysis, we identified improvement from music/non-music gating as well as some potential limitations of the metrics used in the original ASID paper. Additionally, the preprocessing step provides an advantage for real-world long concert recordings, as it can reduce computational costs by avoiding expensive processing of short and long gaps between music performances.
