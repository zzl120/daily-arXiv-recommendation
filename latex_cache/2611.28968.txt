{4.3\ {20\ {IEEEtran} {hyperref} {} {{ }} {Proposition} {draftwatermark} {C^{R}} } Hospitals often update a deployed risk model by adding a data source, such as laboratory results, that only some patients have. Work on safe model updates assumes fixed inputs and so ignores patients who lack the new data. Better overall accuracy does not guarantee that an update keeps doing what the old model did well, and new errors on cases the old model got right can hurt users, such as clinicians, who have learned to work with it~. For risk stratification, \"Otle et al.~ defined $ $: the probability that the updated model orders a pair of patients with different outcomes correctly, given that the original model did. Adding a differentiable version of $ $ to the training loss made their updates more compatible at little cost in AUROC. That work, like most studies of update stability~, keeps the model's inputs fixed. Many real updates do not. They add a data source that exists only for patients for whom someone ordered it. Ordering follows clinical suspicion and local practice~, and it can differ across patient groups~. Adding data this way creates an asymmetry . If the updated model swaps two patients who both have the new data, the swap may reflect new evidence. If it swaps two patients who both lack it, it cannot reflect new measurements for either of them. For a linear update, their score difference depends on re-estimated weights on the original features. The availability indicator can still affect those weights during training, even though it has the same value for both patients at evaluation. Aggregate $ $ mixes these cases together. This matters in practice. An emergency department can use risk scores to decide whom to look at first, for example by reviewing the highest-risk visits on a list. A patient who has had no laboratory tests gets no new measurements from an update that adds laboratory results, but still competes for a place on that list with patients who now score higher. If the update pushes such patients down, clinicians see their relative risk change with nothing to explain it, and a seriously ill patient who has not yet been tested can drop off the list. We ask three questions. When an update adds data that only some patients have, are the patients without it re-ranked more than ordinary retraining would re-rank them? Do they lose their place on alert lists? And can that be prevented without giving up what the update gains? We compare with retraining on the old inputs, because retraining alone already reshuffles some patients. At two hospitals, an update that adds useful data left patients without that data worse off in two ways: with linear updates their order was reshuffled without new measurements, and with every model they slipped down alert lists behind patients who were tested, even as the update caught more patients overall. Whether that trade is acceptable is a clinical and ethical question to ask before deployment, all the more because tests are ordered unevenly across groups. We suggest that teams report flips and fixes among patients without the added data and their alert-list catch rates, overall and by group, next to the usual aggregate metrics. These results add to earlier work. A compatibility loss makes updates more compatible at little cost in AUROC~, but compatibility over pairs did not protect patients' place on an alert list. Retraining can affect groups unevenly~; adding a feature creates a new group, those without it, affected in a predictable direction. Whether a test was done predicts outcomes~, and the update used that signal: better calibrated for untested patients, it still ranked patients partly by whether they had been tested. An early alert may matter most for patients not yet worked up, but that needs prospective study. Two results surprised us. The alert loss came from the availability indicator in Experiment~3 but from the lab values in Experiment~2, so dropping the indicator need not suffice. And boosted trees mostly removed the extra reshuffling but not the alert loss. The two-threshold policy needs someone to choose each group's share of alerts. We used the retrain's share, which keeps things as they were, including any unfairness in how alerts were shared before. That choice belongs to the clinical team, and the gap we still saw for one group at MC-MED shows it should be checked within groups too. Next steps are to repeat this analysis for deep fusion models and outside the US, to set the shares in a principled way, for example with conformal risk control~ or exposure-based rules~, and to ask clinicians how they react when a patient's risk changes without any new measurement. This is a retrospective study of simulated updates, so lower catch rates mean missed alerts, not shown clinical harm. It covers two US academic centers, and at both the added data that mattered was laboratory results. Our main updates are linear, and Prop.~ covers only that case. Boosted trees reproduced the alert loss but little of the extra reshuffling, and we did not try deep multimodal fusion models. When a risk model gains a data source that only some patients have, the patients without it do not stay put. In simulated updates at two hospitals, linear updates re-ranked them more than retraining did when the new data helped prediction, and with every model we tried they lost their place on alert lists to patients who were tested. A pre-specified replication at a second hospital found a loss of almost the same size. Fixes that act on the model helped only partly; separate alert thresholds for patients with and without the new data undid most of the loss at almost no cost. Splitting compatibility by data availability, and reporting it with error correction and alert-list effects, is a simple way to catch these changes before an update goes live. *{Data and Code Availability} MC-MED is available to credentialed users through PhysioNet~ under a data use agreement, and so are MIMIC-IV-ED and MIMIC-IV~.
