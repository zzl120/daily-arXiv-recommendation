See Table~ and ~ for more qualitative examples. } Modern AI systems increasingly rely on dense embeddings not only for semantic retrieval, but across agentic pipelines where retrieved documents, memories, tools, and multimodal content can shape system behavior. Dual encoders are especially well suited to this role because candidate representations can be precomputed and retrieved efficiently at scale~. A query representation may retrieve documents for retrieval-augmented generation~, match multimodal content~, recover episodic memory~, or identify relevant tools~. In these settings, the query vector is therefore more than a semantic representation: its geometry determines which evidence, memories, and capabilities are brought into the model’s context, with direct consequences for subsequent behavior. This design relies on a close relationship between representation and meaning. Learned embedding spaces, however, need not preserve human semantic judgments. Semantically unrelated texts can collide in representation space~, and visually mismatched adversarial queries can produce retrieval results similar to those of a target image~. Other work manipulates queries toward known retrieval targets~, or modifies the corpus, memory, or cache being searched~. We study a more information-restricted setting: can a visibly nonsemantic artifact act as an operational proxy for a natural query, preserving its retrieval behavior without observing the candidate bank or the entity retrieved by that query? We call such nonsemantic proxies . Given a natural query, we construct an EON using a simple gradient-free greedy search that modifies a nonsemantic artifact to make its embedding as close as possible to the natural query representation. The optimizer never observes candidate identities or embeddings, relevance labels, rankings, retrieval scores, or guardrail decisions. It therefore does not know which intent, tool, document, memory, response, or image the natural query retrieves; its only objective is to reproduce the natural query representation. Despite these severe constraints, optimized EONs frequently inherit the retrieval outcome of their source queries. We observe substantial natural-query outcome preservation across intent routing, tool selection, full-corpus document retrieval, episodic agent memory, text-to-image retrieval, and image-to-image retrieval, spanning multiple independently trained dense embedding families. Notably, this preservation also appears on LongMemEval and MemBench~, where each query is evaluated against a different memory pool. This rules out adaptation to a single fixed retrieval index and strengthens the corpus-blind interpretation. Matched random noise controls rarely preserve natural selections, confirming that these artifacts are non-trivial, and systematically increasing query-EON cosine similarity yields higher candidate preservation. This representational gap creates an immediate vulnerability at the boundary between query moderation and retrieval. Because EONs look like harmless nonsemantic noise, they can easily slip past query guardrails designed to catch overt malicious intent~. Our guardrail evaluations reveal that this is precisely what happens, with independent safety classifiers frequently permitting EONs, often at rates comparable to matched random strings, while reliably intercepting the corresponding natural malicious requests. The result is a striking security loophole: an attacker can bypass front-line moderation with an artifact that appears entirely benign, yet still preserve the safety-sensitive retrieval outcome of the blocked query. We call this phenomenon , as our findings demonstrate that dense dual encoders across modern retrieval architectures are operationally blind to the linguistic validity of an incoming query. Because these representations mediate retrieval across modern semantic and agentic pipelines, the mismatch can propagate directly into subsequent system behavior~. Geometrically, query-level representation matching constrains score changes over unseen candidates, allowing retrieval outcomes to remain stable without access to the candidate bank. Dense retrieval maps queries and candidates into a shared representation space, enabling large candidate collections to be searched efficiently using vector similarity~. This abstraction has expanded beyond conventional document search. Multimodal encoders such as CLIP support retrieval across text and images~, while retrieval-augmented generation uses external retrieval to supply foundation models with information beyond their parametric knowledge~. Modern agents extend the same idea to action selection: retrieval is used to identify relevant tools from large inventories~ and to recover information from external memory~. Retrieval can therefore determine not only what information is returned, but also what information or capability becomes available to subsequent model reasoning. A common design uses a single vector for each query and candidate, allowing candidate representations to be precomputed and searched efficiently. Late-interaction methods such as ColBERTv2 instead retain token-level representations and defer part of the matching computation until retrieval time~. This distinction is relevant to our setting because the blindspot phenomenon targets the query representation itself: when a single vector mediates the retrieval decision, reproducing that vector may reproduce behavior over many possible candidate banks. Neural representation spaces need not align perfectly with human semantic judgments. demonstrated semantic collisions in which unrelated texts are mapped to similar representations, establishing that embedding proximity can be manipulated independently of apparent meaning. Related work has exploited the query side of retrieval directly. In image retrieval, construct visually unrelated adversarial queries that produce retrieval results similar to those of a target image, while subsequent work studies query-efficient black-box attacks against image-retrieval systems~. Textual RAG systems have likewise been attacked by modifying queries so that a chosen document enters the retrieved context~. These works make clear that query-side adversarial manipulation itself is not new. Our setting differs in what is optimized and what information is available. EONs are not optimized to retrieve, suppress, or rerank any known candidate. Instead, they are optimized only to reproduce the representation of a natural query and are evaluated afterward against a candidate bank that was absent from the optimization loop. The resulting question is whether a nonsemantic proxy can inherit the retrieval behavior of an arbitrary meaningful query without knowing what that query retrieves. A large body of recent work instead attacks the state searched by the retriever. Adversarial passages can be injected into dense-retrieval corpora so that they rank highly for many queries~, with later work developing increasingly effective black-box and gradient-based corpus-poisoning strategies~. In retrieval-mediated agents, AgentPoison inserts poisoned demonstrations into long-term memory or RAG knowledge bases and uses optimized triggers to cause those demonstrations to be retrieved during agent execution~. Semantic caching creates a related attack surface: crafted query--response entries can poison embedding-based caches and subsequently affect other users whose queries are considered sufficiently similar~. These attacks demonstrate that retrieval components can become security-critical once their outputs feed foundation models or agents. They also share an important difference from our setting: the attacker manipulates the corpus, memory, cache, or a target-specific retrieval objective. In our threat model, the retrieval state remains unchanged and hidden; only a proxy for the natural query is constructed. Modern safety systems commonly place dedicated classifiers around language-model interactions to identify harmful user requests or unsafe model responses~. At the same time, retrieval can materially change the safety behavior of a language-model system: recent evaluations show that adding RAG can alter a model's safety profile even when the underlying model and retrieved documents are individually considered safe~. AgentPoison and related work further show that retrieved memories or knowledge can influence later planning and actions~. Our work concerns the boundary between these two mechanisms. A semantic guardrail judges the query it can read, while a dense retriever acts on the query representation. EONs test whether these two views can separate: a query may lose its recognizable semantic content while retaining enough of the original representation to preserve safety-sensitive routing or retrieval. We therefore study not only whether nonsemantic proxies preserve retrieval, but also when that preservation creates a gap between semantic moderation and embedding-based selection. Let $q$ denote a natural query and $q^*$ its nonsemantic proxy, with $Q$ and $Q^*$ denoting their normalized embeddings. We use as an umbrella term for both text and image proxies. For a candidate proxy $q'$ with normalized embedding $Q'$, EON construction optimizes only \[ q^* _{q' X } Q^ Q', \] where $ X $ is the allowed artifact space. Here, $Q$ is always the embedding of the individual natural evaluation query itself. EONs are never optimized toward a class or intent centroid, gold answer, tool description, paired item, retrieved candidate, or other task-specific target. The setting is strictly : optimization observes only the natural-query representation and representations of proposed EONs. It receives no candidate identities or embeddings, labels or relevance judgments, rankings, retrieval scores, retrieved items, or guardrail decisions. The EON is frozen before any candidate-dependent evaluation, whether the eventual candidate bank is fixed or query-specific. Text and image EONs share the same representation-matching objective but use different artifact spaces. The default text EON contains 64 fixed, space-delimited visible ASCII atoms and is initialized as repeated colons. The legal atom bank is encoder-specific but query-independent and contains only short atoms that round-trip through the evaluated query tokenizer as single non-special tokens. Whitespace is fixed, and a lexical no-copy constraint prevents direct reuse of meaningful terms from the source query. At each proposal, one atom is replaced and the change is retained only if it strictly increases $Q^ Q^*$. Image EONs begin from a blank white canvas. Each proposal resamples a single scalar image channel, and the change is retained only if it strictly increases $Q^ Q^*$. Once a channel has been changed by an accepted proposal, it is not edited again. The final optimized image is used for retrieval evaluation. Both procedures are gradient-free and use query--EON similarity as their only optimization signal. The default optimization budget is 50,000 mutation proposals per query. Exact artifact constraints, tokenizer filtering, no-copy enforcement, update rules, and pseudocode are provided in Appendix~. We identify , a representational failure mode in which a natural query can be replaced by visibly nonsemantic Embedding-Optimized Noise while preserving retrieval behavior over candidate banks never observed during optimization. Constructed solely by matching the natural query representation, EONs preserve intent and tool routing, document and query-specific memory retrieval, and cross-modal and image retrieval across multiple dense embedding families. The effect is query- and encoder-specific: it strengthens with optimization, is absent for unoptimized noise, degrades when optimized structure is disrupted, and transfers poorly across independently trained encoders. For safety-sensitive queries, this creates a consequential mismatch between surface semantics and embedding-mediated behavior: the same frozen EON can pass independent guardrails that reject the natural query while preserving its retrieval outcome. Our analysis provides a geometric explanation for this behavior: query--EON similarity places a corpus-blind bound on score changes for unseen candidates, allowing retrieval decisions to remain stable without access to the candidate bank. At the same time, late interaction and independent cross-encoder reranking substantially weaken preservation, identifying clear architectural boundaries and mitigation directions. Overall, semantic recognizability and representational actionability can diverge: a query may cease to be meaningfully interpretable while remaining operationally meaningful to a retriever. Retrieval systems, particularly in safety-sensitive settings, should therefore not treat embedding similarity alone as evidence that a query is semantically well formed or safe. Semantic retrieval increasingly mediates which documents, memories, tools, and multimodal content become available to downstream AI systems. A mismatch between query-side safety checks and retrieval behavior can therefore have consequences beyond retrieval accuracy: a proxy that passes a semantic guardrail may still surface the same safety-sensitive content or capability as the blocked natural query. Our experiments establish this failure at the retrieval boundary; its downstream consequences depend on the access controls, generator, and agent policy of the deployed system. For example, in systems where semantic retrieval selects a memory record, document, or tool before downstream reasoning, preserving that retrieval under a guardrail-passing proxy could expose the same resource to subsequent system components even though the original query was rejected. The remainder of this section clarifies the interpretation and scope of these results, including optimization cost, candidate-bank independence, safety evaluation, and architectural boundaries. The default 50k proposal budget is intended to provide a strong and consistent operating point, not to imply that 50k proposals are required for the effect to appear. Tables~ and~ show that substantial Preservation@$k$ is already present at 2k and 10k checkpoints in many routing, safety-sensitive, and visual settings, and generally increases as query--EON alignment improves. The 50k setting therefore emphasizes the full strength of the phenomenon, while the lower-budget checkpoints expose its cost--performance trade-off. The corresponding wall-clock costs are also modest relative to the scale of the evaluation. On NVIDIA H200 hardware, the throughput-scaled 2k equivalent for optimizing 500 text queries is approximately 1.6--5.1 minutes across the principal text encoders, while directly measured 50k runs take approximately 40--128 minutes . These costs reflect only local query-encoder optimization and do not require candidate-bank queries, retrieval feedback, or guardrail interaction. Once an EON is constructed, it is frozen and can be evaluated against any compatible candidate bank. Gold@$k$ and Preservation@$k$ measure different properties. Gold@$k$ asks whether retrieval is benchmark-correct, whereas Preservation@$k$ asks whether the EON reproduces the outcome selected by the natural query. For benign retrieval, this is the natural top-ranked candidate; for the primary safety-sensitive evaluation, it is the corresponding annotated harm category. Preservation therefore need not imply benchmark correctness, and a benchmark-correct EON need not reproduce the natural query's exact behavior. The evaluated tasks vary substantially in candidate structure. Routing uses complete intent or tool bank, document retrieval searches full collections, visual retrieval searches galleries, and memory benchmarks use query-specific memory pools. The latter are especially informative as the candidate bank itself changes across queries. Together with the absence of candidate identities, embeddings, rankings, or retrieval scores during optimization, these settings distinguish query-representation matching from candidate-aware retrieval optimization. The primary safety experiments evaluate retrieval at the level of annotated harm categories. Gold@$k$ measures whether an EON retrieves items matching the query's annotated category, whereas Preservation@$k$ measures whether the category selected by the natural query remains represented among the EON's top-$k$ retrievals. EON construction has no access to the safety taxonomy or candidate bank. The result should therefore be interpreted as preservation of safety-sensitive retrieval behavior at the category level, not as evidence that the EON surface itself expresses the corresponding harmful semantics. Bypass-ASR isolates the conjunction of two events: the natural query is rejected while the same frozen EON is permitted and preserves its evaluated retrieval outcome. Random legal noise shows that semantic guardrail acceptance alone is insufficient, because unoptimized artifacts are often permitted but do not retain query-specific retrieval behavior. At the same time, the present short-atom construction is not invisible to every filter that a retrieval system could apply. A simple supervised gibberish detector rejects all evaluated text EONs and matched random artifacts while rejecting only $1.0\ queries , reducing the corresponding text Bypass-ASR to zero. While specialized lexical filters reliably flag non-standard queries, they address the surface form of the present attack rather than the underlying representational mismatch. This distinction matters because dense encoders can admit semantic collisions , motivating complementary defenses at the retrieval level in addition to query screening. Notably, the same retrieval-preservation phenomenon also appears in the image setting, where lexical query screening is not applicable. Frozen EONs transfer poorly across independently trained encoders, indicating that the effect is closely tied to the representation geometry against which the proxy is optimized. The strongest practical setting is therefore one in which the deployed query encoder is known, publicly available, or otherwise reproducible. Importantly, once such access is available, optimization remains entirely local: constructing a proxy for a new query does not require probing the deployed candidate bank or observing retrieval outcomes. The effect is strongest for dense single-vector retrieval. ColBERTv2 late interaction substantially weakens preservation, and independent cross-encoder reranking weakens it further. These controls are consistent with the proposed mechanism: corpus-blind representation matching is most consequential when a single query vector largely determines candidate selection, while explicit query--candidate interaction provides a natural architectural boundary. The principal limitation is encoder specificity. Because cross-encoder transfer is weak, a reliable EON generally requires access to, or knowledge of, the query encoder used by the target retrieval system. This restricts the threat model for opaque proprietary encoders. Conversely, public and reproducible embedding models make the optimization setting considerably more realistic, since EON construction can then be performed locally without access to the deployed candidate bank, retrieval scores, or guardrail feedback. We do not evaluate whether proxies optimized against several commonly deployed encoders could be precomputed and tested opportunistically against an unknown system. Such a strategy could partially relax the exact-model assumption when the space of plausible encoders is small, but its effectiveness is presently unknown and should not be inferred from our transfer results. The current text EONs are also visibly nonsemantic. The present construction is designed to isolate the representational failure mode rather than optimize surface-form stealth. As shown in Table~, a dedicated lexical/surface-form detector can reject the present short-atom construction with low rejection of the evaluated natural queries. We therefore do not claim that EONs evade specialized gibberish, lexical-validity, or representation-OOD filters. Whether an optimizer can preserve retrieval while simultaneously satisfying such surface-form constraints is a separate question. Finally, our safety experiments establish failure at the retrieval boundary: a nonsemantic proxy can pass semantic moderation while retaining a safety-sensitive retrieval outcome. We do not claim that every preserved retrieval necessarily produces a harmful final system action. End-to-end effects will depend on the generator, agent policy, retrieved content, and additional controls applied after retrieval.
