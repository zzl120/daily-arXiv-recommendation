{29} {11.5} {88.7} {10.7} Generative search engines such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity answer a question with a written text and a handful of links. For many users, these links have become the entry point to the Web, and the engines thereby decide which Web pages, and which brands, are seen. A fast-growing industry of advises publishers on how to earn these citations, most often by adding FAQ blocks, structured data, summary boxes and author information to their pages. Whether such advice works is largely untested outside controlled settings, and observational comparisons are easily misled: practices are concentrated on popular websites, which are cited more for reasons that have nothing to do with the practice. In this paper, we study what shapes citation in generative search engines, using two 90-day collections of daily answers to commercial questions in contrasting markets: a consumer market dominated by a few large brands and a niche market of small business-software vendors . We address three research questions. which properties of a page's content go with more citations, when pages are compared within the same website? how much of the variation in citations do page properties explain, and what is the rest? how do the engine's retrieval and the model's own knowledge shape which pages are cited and which brands are named? To answer them, we combine four approaches. A within-website design with a five-test robustness rule separates the effect of a page from that of its website. An API replay of every question exposes what the collection cannot show: the searches each engine writes, the ranked list of pages it retrieves, and which of them it cites. A probe of the engines' models without search measures what they already know. Finally, controlled experiments vary one factor at a time in the input the model reads: the order of the sources, the names of brands and publishers, and the set of alternatives. The results can be summarized in the title's three terms. matters in one way: pages whose title and paragraphs match the questions people ask are cited more, whereas FAQ blocks, structured data, summary boxes, author boxes and page speed appear effective only across websites. Page properties, however, explain little of the variation between pages of a website ( \ citation counts are highly repeatable. The reason lies in : every page ChatGPT cites is one its own search retrieved, and retrieval explains \ rewrite each question into new sub-queries on every answer, rank pages by how well they match these queries in meaning, and cite mostly from the top of their own ranking, which is not that of Google or Bing. Finally, the shapes both steps and the answer itself: ChatGPT writes brands it knows into its searches, prefers websites it knows, and names brands without any supporting page ( \ products with identical facts. Pages are thus cited when they are searched; brands are also named when they are remembered. A second market of small vendors replicates these findings and shows where they depend on the market: there, the models barely know the brands, and a stable position exists only when search finds evidence about them. Our contributions are as follows: Two longitudinal datasets of generative search answers in contrasting markets, joined with page content, links and replay logs, released with all code . As generative engines become a gateway to the Web, which pages and brands are visible depends on retrieval and model knowledge that publishers cannot observe and that change from week to week. Early work evaluated whether the citations of generative search engines support their statements, and found that many do not~. Measurement studies have since described which sources the engines cite: audits found commercial and geographic biases in the sources of answers on public topics~, citations of news that concentrate on few outlets~, a strong preference for third-party media over brand-owned content~, and sources that differ substantially between Google Search, its AI Overviews and Gemini~. Others related page characteristics to how strongly a cited page is used in the answer~, and showed that visibility varies across repeated queries and must be measured as a distribution~. These studies observe the answers. In contrast, we hold the website fixed when comparing pages, follow the same questions daily for 90 days, and replay them through the engines' APIs to observe the retrieval step itself, the searches, the ranked pages and the selection among them, which the answers do not reveal. Generative engine optimization was introduced as rewriting a page so that it is more visible in answers generated from a given set of sources~. Related work showed that injected text can promote a product in the rankings of conversational search engines~ and of LLM recommendations~, and a recent benchmark found that most such methods are ineffective or harmful, whereas conventional search engine optimization remains effective~. These evaluations fix the set of sources and measure the answer step. Our results complement them in two ways: we test the commonly recommended page practices observationally, within websites, on production engines; and we show that most of the variation in citations arises before the answer, in retrieval, which a fixed source set excludes. Our observational comparison also relates to work on search engine optimization that compares optimized with non-optimized pages~; we find that the engines' retrieval is not a proxy for classic search rankings. Generative search engines are systems of retrieval-augmented generation~ that rewrite the user's input into search queries~. Language models use long inputs unevenly~, and when they judge candidates, the order in which the candidates are presented changes the verdict~. They also prefer well-known brands: global over local brands~, and incumbents over equivalent products~. Our controlled experiments measure these effects on the models behind two production engines, with real questions and sources, and relate them to what the replay shows: the position effect inflates ChatGPT's preference for its top-ranked sources, and brand names reorder its rankings in line with what the model believes about them. Four conclusions follow. First, write pages for the questions people ask: a title and a paragraph that answer a question in its own terms are the only page properties that pass every test, most strongly on third-party pages ( \ they decide which questions a page is cited for. Second, being retrieved matters more than anything on the page. The engines search with their own rewritten queries, rank pages by how well they match these queries in meaning, and do not return Google's or Bing's pages, so a good rank in classic search is not a proxy; at equal rank, concise pages from the original source win over roundups and forums. Third, the practices most often recommended for AI visibility are not associated with more citations within a website, and at equal rank they go with slightly fewer. Fourth, citations must be monitored over time and per question: the cited set changes over weeks and a brand's standing is specific to the question. Our results trace a chain from the model's prior to citation: a brand in the prior is written into the engine's searches ( \ against \ are preferred at equal rank , and a cited page about the brand leads to the brand being named; the prior also acts without any page. We therefore hypothesize that, if a brand's representation in a model's prior could be changed, an effective strategy would combine placing accurate associations between the brand and the relevant questions into the prior with making the brand's own and third-party pages retrievable for the brand-named queries this produces. Two conditions follow from our data: what is imprinted matters, not only the name (a name can lower a brand's ranking on identical facts); and the prior alone yields mentions without citations, since every cited page is retrieved. We stress that this is a hypothesis: our study establishes associations along the chain, not the effect of changing a prior, and known brands also differ in size, coverage and links. Testing it requires intervention studies, for example probing priors again after model updates. Studies of generative engine optimization have mostly evaluated content changes on a fixed set of retrieved sources~. Such evaluations measure only the answer step, whereas most of the variation we observe arises in retrieval; they should also randomize the order of the sources and report the brands involved, since position and names change the answer on identical content. Observational comparisons should hold the website fixed, as every recommended practice appears effective across websites and none within them, and should be longitudinal, as citations drift. The results are associations: the within-website design removes everything constant within a website, but not unmeasured differences between its pages, and page links were measured after the citations.
