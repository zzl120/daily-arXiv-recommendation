We present a production skill router over 34,396 skills and a large-scale study of skill retrieval using limited real supervision and synthetic data. We found that the synthetic-data fine-tuning improves in-distribution retrieval but it causes catastrophic forgetting on real and out-of-distribution data. We evaluate several forgetting mitigation fine-tuning approaches inspired by continual learning, including embedding-anchor regularization, Learning without Forgetting , Elastic Weight Consolidation , and L2-initialization. The results show that these approaches not only retain the performance on OOD skills retrieval but also improve the retrieval on synthetic in-distribution skills by 13.98\ Modern LLM-driven agents increasingly rely on : short, agent-readable procedures retrieved at run time and injected into the agent's context to solve specific tasks~. These tasks span scientific , engineering , financial , software and many other domains, and they require specific skills for end-to-end completion; e.g., a fraud-detection task may depend on fuzzy matching, database search, PDF reading, and Excel analysis. As skill registries grow, retrieving the right skills for a task becomes hard. The common information retrieval practice is to use sparse retrieval algorithms, such as BM25 and dense vector retrieval models to match on lexical and semantic similarity ~. This practice is also enhanced with synthetic supervision from an LLM to fine-tune a model for improving retrieval performance~. We follow this paradigm and conduct a systematic empirical study of skill retrieval over a repository of skills~. We use tasks from SkillsBench and Terminal-Bench~2 and execute approximately 1K trials across 109 tasks using hybrid retrieval pipelines in Harbor environment~. We further construct two synthetic training tracks: Track~A creates anchor-skill tasks with retrieval-derived positives and hard negatives, while Track~B combines real data, paraphrase generation, and multi-skill task synthesis. Using these datasets, we evaluate compact retrieval models, such as under varying fine-tuning regimes. Our experiments show that synthetic supervision improves in-distribution retrieval performance but can also induce substantial forgetting. In the most aggressive fine-tuning configuration, OOD recall drops from $0.850$ to $0.650$, demonstrating that gains on synthetic training data do not necessarily translate to robust deployment behavior. This raises an important deployment question that has received limited attention: Catastrophic forgetting is well studied in continual learning, where approaches such as Learning without Forgetting and Elastic Weight Consolidation preserve pretrained behaviour~. However, it remains unclear how these preservation strategies behave in the context of synthetic-data-driven skill retrieval for LLM agents, especially at realistic deployment scale. Therefore, we study several forgetting mitigation regularization approaches, including embedding-anchor regularization, LwF, EWC, and L2-initialization, under a common fine-tuning recipe . Our results show that these fine-tuning regularization approaches not only retain the behavior of the frozen model on OOD or real tasks for LoRA adapters~, but also improve performance on the synthetic data compared to aggressive LoRA fine-tuning. Our contributions are threefold. We present a systematic study of skill retrieval for agents at a realistic scale, covering skills and 109 verifier-scored tasks, and identify synthetic-data-induced forgetting as a deployment failure mode. We introduce a unified benchmark that combines real and synthetic supervision under a shared evaluation protocol. We evaluate multiple forgetting-mitigation approaches, including embedding anchors, LwF, EWC, and L2-initialization, and show that conservative regularized fine-tuning preserves real-world and out-of-distribution behavior while improving in-distribution gains, providing a practical recipe for deployed skill-retrieval systems. Agent systems increasingly rely on retrieved procedures, tools, or skills at run time~, and generating queries or pseudo-labels with LLMs is a standard recipe for low-supervision retrieval~. While previous work on skill router training reports gains from synthetic data , our findings show a nuanced picture: synthetic supervision is beneficial when applied to lightweight components but can harm performance when used to fine-tune encoders or rerankers if the synthetic task fails to capture the true multi-positive structure of downstream evaluation. Catastrophic forgetting has been widely studied in continual learning methods, such as: EWC regularizes parameters against the Fisher-information-weighted posterior of the previous task~, learning without forgetting regularizes outputs via knowledge distillation~, L2-init keeps parameters close to initialization , and analyzing the extent of catastrophic forgetting in neural ranking models~. We present an empirical study of synthetic data induced forgetting in large-scale skill retrieval for LLM agents. We compare preservation-style regularization--including LwF, EWC, L2-init, and embedding anchoring--under a shared conservative LoRA recipe. We studied skill retrieval for LLM agents on the -skill scale using a unified benchmark that combines real tasks and synthetic supervision. Our experiments show that naive fine-tuning can degrade real-task retrieval and induce catastrophic forgetting on out-of-distribution queries. Our results also show that the use of forgetting mitigation regularizers with LoRA based fine-tuning not only improves in-distribution performance but also preserves the out-of-distribution performance. According to our intervention-based experiments, performance regression in aggressive updates can be characterized as stability-plasticity failure consistent with representational or ranking-function drift. We therefore cannot rule out the possibility that some negative results--especially for encoder LoRA fine-tuning--would change at substantially larger synthetic-data scale or under broader positive-skill coverage. We do mitigate this concern by showing results on external large benchmarks and how forgetting mitigation methods can preserve the existing behaviour while improving results on a new training distribution. We do not study multilingual skill retrieval, multimodal inputs, or non-code agent settings.
