Over time, RSs have evolved from relying solely on user–item interactions to incorporating auxiliary content signals such as product images and titles, aiming to alleviate issues such as data sparsity and cold start . Such multimodal features serve as complementary signals to enhance representation learning . In contrast, the emergence of large language model -based RSs fundamentally shifts this paradigm. Recent models generally formulate recommendation as a language-driven task, where user behavior, item information, and task objectives are expressed in natural language prompts, and personalized recommendations are produced through language understanding, reasoning, and generation . As a result, textual content is no longer a side feature, but becomes the primary interface through which the model performs recommendation decisions . However, this paradigm shift also broadens the attack surface, raising new security and robustness challenges . RSs have long been recognized as highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, particularly item promotion attacks that aim to increase the exposure of target items , as illustrated in Fig.~, thereby fostering unfair competition among providers and eroding users’ trust in the platform ecosystem. Such attacks align closely with real-world threats observed on platforms like Amazon, eBay, and Sony . Existing attacks are predominantly realized through data poisoning, where malicious actors, typically profit-driven item providers, inject well-crafted structured interactions into the training process of RSs to promote their target items . In response, existing defenses focus mainly on identifying and filtering such anomalous interactions . However, current attacks against LLM-based RSs often operate at inference time through prompt perturbations in the open-ended language space , as shown by the example in Fig.~, limiting the effectiveness of those existing defenses. Consequently, systematic detection methods tailored to LLM-based RSs remain largely underexplored. [width=0.9 ]{intro_fig.pdf} Existing detection approaches for LLMs , which are primarily designed to recognize adversarial prompts that induce unsafe or misaligned outputs , are not well-suited to the characteristics of RS attacks. Moreover, these methods typically operate in a static and one-off manner, lacking mechanisms to adapt to evolving attack strategies . This limitation becomes more pronounced as recent attacks increasingly leverage LLMs to generate more effective, human-like, and stealthy promotional content . As a result, these detectors often exhibit degenerate behaviors, either overwhelmingly favoring benign predictions or achieving high detection rates at the cost of excessive false alarms . Beyond detection performance, explainability is essential for real-world deployment . However, most existing detectors operate as black boxes and provide detection decisions without exposing a human-understandable decision process. This highlights the urgent need for adaptive and explainable detectors for LLM-based RSs. To address the above challenges, in this paper, we propose GuardAgent, a novel LLM-powered agentic detection framework designed to safeguard LLM-based RSs against promotion-driven adversarial content manipulations. We formulate the framework as a cognitive agentic loop consisting of Perception, Action, Reflection, and Memory, as illustrated in Fig.~. Specifically, GuardAgent maintains a dynamic memory mechanism, allowing it to continually interact with and learn from its environment, and to evolve its decision policies through self-reflection on past detection failures and erroneous reasoning traces, thereby adapting to previously unseen benign and attack patterns. To improve the explainability, GuardAgent distills reasoning into memory-augmented decision policies and explains its detection outcomes via explicit policies and reasoning traces, enabling security teams to audit decisions, diagnose potential failures, and respond effectively to emerging threats. We propose GuardAgent to recognize adversarial content manipulations in recommendations and provide explainable detection outcomes through explicit textual policies and reasoning traces. The reflection stage aims to refine symbolic decision policies by analyzing reasoning errors arising from incorrect detections, rather than relying on reward-driven optimization as commonly adopted in reinforcement learning-based agents , which may reduce explainability and controllability in security-sensitive environments. To this end, GuardAgent introduces to support robust and generalizable policy refinement. Unlike most episodic reflection methods that perform updates based on isolated failures, GuardAgent learns decision policies over a batch of failure cases accumulated across multiple interactive detection episodes. Each case consists of a benign--malicious content pair exhibiting false alarms and missed detections, together with the associated mistaken reasoning traces. The batch-wise reflection improves the stability and generalizability of policy updates by mitigating overfitting to individual failures and reducing sensitivity to noisy error cases. To capture both individual errors and systematic limitations, we adopt a two-level contrastive analysis paradigm for policy refinement: 1) is conducted within each benign--malicious failure pair. By jointly revisiting the detection state $s_t$, the predicted action $a_t$, and the associated reasoning traces $r_t$, reflection identifies misleading patterns in benign content that triggered false alarms, as well as overlooked cues in adversarial content that failed to indicate malicious intent based on $y_t$. This localized contrast highlights relative pattern differences responsible for incorrect decisions, enabling precise correction of decision boundaries. 2) is performed across multiple failure pairs within the batch. Patterns that consistently appear in omitted adversarial content are strengthened, while patterns frequently observed in benign content are suppressed or qualified. Through this consolidation update, the robustness and generalizability to unseen attacks are enhanced. The agent finally distills the above analysis into refinement signals in the form of revised or newly derived decision policies, where revision is prioritized to avoid memory explosion and ensure stable adaptation. Before entering the cognitive loop, GuardAgent initializes its memory $_0$ with a small set of pre-defined detection policies induced by prompting LLMs. To enhance their generality, the prompts explicitly incorporate attack goals, encouraging the model to synthesize decision policies that capture common promotion-oriented manipulation patterns, such as lexical noise, exaggerated claims, and clickbait urgency . During the loop, GuardAgent maintains a memory module that integrates various abstraction levels of traceable knowledge derived from its detection experiences. Specifically, the memory consists of three complementary components: 1) episodic evidence, 2) explicit reasoning traces, and 3) textual decision policies, collectively supporting the distinction between benign and malicious content. The hierarchy ensures high-level policies are experience-grounded and auditable, enhancing accountability by enabling each policy to be traced back to its underlying traces and evidences, while simultaneously providing a basis for trustworthy adaptivity to previously unseen threats. Consistent with our threat model in Section~, all attacks manipulate the textual metadata of target items to promote them in recommendation results. Their brief descriptions are provided below, while a more detailed discussion of these attacks is provided in Section~. DeepwordBug and PuncAttack manipulate texts by introducing typos and inserting punctuation, respectively. ChatGPTAttack increases the stealthiness and effectiveness of TrivialInsertion by rewriting the whole text content via GPTs. Early studies on RS security mainly focus on poisoning attacks on conventional RSs , where malicious user–item interactions are injected to bias recommendations . However, such attacks are less effective or only partially applicable to LLM-based RSs , which increasingly rely on textual item metadata for prompt construction and recommendation generation . Consequently, the attack surface shifts toward language-driven manipulation of item metadata or system prompts . Compared to system-level perturbation , which generally requires privileged access or even overall compromise, modifying textual item content is more practical and stealthy , as item providers can directly manage their own metadata . Therefore, we focus on detecting item-level adversarial perturbations in textual metadata used to promote target items in LLM-based RSs. {.35 } {amazon_rm.pdf} {.35 } {yelp_rm.pdf} {guardagent_prompts.pdf} Current defense mechanisms in RSs have mainly focused on poisoning attacks . Existing studies in this area can be broadly categorized into robust training and detection-based defenses, where the former enhances model robustness against contaminated data , while the latter identifies and filters poisoned samples before training . Compared with robust training, detectors provide greater flexibility and interpretability, making them more suitable for real-world deployment where malicious data must be explicitly identified and removed . Such detectors often employ supervised or unsupervised anomaly detection, clustering, or confidence-based scoring to distinguish poisoned samples from benign ones. Similarly, recent studies on image-side defenses have shifted attention toward detecting forged or adversarially manipulated product images , highlighting the growing importance of multimodal security in RSs. Beyond traditional RS architectures, recent works have begun to study prompt injection attacks against LLM-integrated applications, proposing both prevention-based defenses and detection-based defenses . However, systematic evaluations indicate that existing defenses either suffer from limited robustness or incur significant utility degradation, and none are tailored to recommendation scenarios. To sum up, detecting malicious textual manipulations in LLM-based RSs remains largely unexplored. Despite the growing integration of LLMs into recommendation pipelines , existing defenses are still primarily designed for conventional collaborative or visually-aware architectures , leaving a significant gap in safeguarding LLM-driven systems against adversarial textual manipulations. Although recent studies have attempted to explore trigger-based poisoning detection in LLM-based RSs , these methods mainly focus on detecting explicit and unnatural trigger tokens , while more stealthy and natural-looking semantic manipulations embedded in textual metadata, such as fluent promotional rewrites and instruction-like perturbations shown in Table~, remain largely unexplored. In this work, we address these gaps by proposing an agentic detection framework that continuously learns, reasons, and updates its decision policies to detect evolving adversarial textual attacks in dynamic LLM-based recommendation environments . In this paper, we present GuardAgent to safeguard LLM-based RSs against item promotion attacks. By formulating detection as a cognitive agentic loop, GuardAgent robustly adapts to evolving attack patterns while providing faithful detection outcomes supported by explainable reasoning traces. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art baselines, validating GuardAgent’s strong generalization ability under practical adversarial settings. Overall, GuardAgent takes a step toward autonomous and explainable security agents for protecting next-generation LLM-empowered RSs in dynamic threat environments. Future work will further improve explanation faithfulness, robustness under weaker supervision, and deployment scalability. We will explore causal inference as a potential approach toward these goals. In addition, ensemble learning can be considered to enhance generalization performance. While our study necessarily evaluates existing adversarial manipulation techniques, the attacks considered in this work are based on previously published methods and are used solely for controlled evaluation. A potential risk is that descriptions of adversarial patterns could facilitate misuse; however, our work does not introduce new attack capabilities and instead focuses on detection and mitigation. In practical deployment, false positives may incorrectly flag benign item content and affect legitimate providers. We therefore emphasize false-alarm control and recommend human review for high-impact decisions. The datasets used in our experiments are publicly available benchmark datasets and do not introduce additional collection of sensitive personal information.
