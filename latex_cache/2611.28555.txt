Exactness and space succinctness come with the extra benefit that no parameter tuning is needed. LSR models encode queries and documents as sparse vectors over the vocabulary of a language model~. Relevance is the inner product, so retrieval is top-$k$ maximum inner-product search over sparse vectors, and the natural data structure is the inverted index, whose posting lists can be compressed with the techniques surveyed by Pibiri and Venturini~. Learned weights break the assumptions of classic query processing: queries have tens of terms, weights are large on frequent terms, and documents are expanded with terms they do not contain. Mackenzie et al.\ call them ``wacky weights'', on which ``few documents can actually be skipped if exact query evaluation is desired''~. Document-at-a-time processing advances one cursor per query term in doc-id order and scores one document at a time. Dynamic pruning skips the documents that cannot enter the top $k$, using per-term upper bounds. MaxScore~ splits the terms into ones, read in full, and ones, whose total contribution is too small to bring a document into the top $k$ on its own. The non-essential terms are read only for the few documents that may still enter it. WAND~ pivots on the prefix sums of the bounds, and Block-Max WAND~ adds per-block maxima. Block-Max MaxScore~ runs MaxScore inside doc-id intervals, with tighter bounds for each interval. Dimopoulos et al.~ already compute these bounds over fixed ranges of doc-ids whose size is a power of two, as does . Anytime ranking visits ranges of doc-ids in decreasing order of their bound~. ~ partitions the reordered doc-id space into blocks of a few documents, stores 8-bit block maxima per term and, per block, inverted lists of the block's postings, and scores the blocks by decreasing upper bound until the threshold exceeds the next bound. Each block is scored term at a time, into one accumulator per document of the block. ~ groups blocks into superblocks pruned before their blocks, and ~ visits the most promising superblocks with 4-bit maxima and a forward index. In their rank-safe settings they are an order of magnitude faster than the MaxScore of ~ on SPLADE, but their indexes are several times larger than the data. ~, a Rust re-implementation of DS2I~ about as fast as , offers the same document-at-a-time algorithms, MaxScore and Block-Max MaxScore among them. ~ keeps the top entries of each posting list, clusters them into blocks summarized by quantized max-vectors, skips the blocks whose summary score is below a fraction of the threshold, and scores the others with a forward index. Graph indexes such as HNSW~, implemented for sparse vectors in ~ and in the winners of the BigANN'23 sparse track~, are the other family. Both trade recall for speed through parameters chosen by a grid search on each collection~. Term-at-a-time processing adds one posting list at a time into an accumulator per document. DAAT came to dominate research~. On large memory-resident indexes, the cache misses of TAAT increase drastically, which Fontoura et al.\ attribute to ``random access over the large array of accumulators''~. The research literature studies TAAT with accumulators over the whole collection or over a pruned subset of it~. Cache-sized windows of doc-ids appear in DAAT, but only to hold a bitmap of live blocks~. To our knowledge, no published study measures TAAT over cache-sized windows of global posting lists, with or without pruning, against DAAT, block-based or approximate LSR indexes. Classic TAAT keeps one accumulator per document: each posting list is read sequentially, but its updates land at random positions of an array as large as the collection, and on large indexes the cache misses grow sharply~. and avoid these misses by evaluating the query on one small window of doc-ids at a time, whose accumulators stay in cache. Both use what we call : the window exists only at query time, and for each term the search reads postings until the next one falls outside the window. The search must thus find where the window ends in the list of each term and bring every doc-id to its position within it. Soft windowing is usually paired with a partitioning of the posting lists by cardinality, for example into chunks of a fixed number of postings, none of which corresponds to a window. Our index uses instead what we call : the windows are fixed, aligned intervals of the doc-id space, the same for every term, and they are also the blocks in which every posting list is stored. The postings that a term has in a window are thus known in advance, and the search does not have to find where the window ends. A posting does not store its block either, only its position within it, which is already its slot in the accumulator. In exhaustive search, this keeps the work per posting simple for the CPU: no comparison with the window and no shift, only its contribution added to its slot. This already speeds up exhaustive search, and hard windows will be crucial for dynamic pruning . In TAAT over windows, the window is the range of doc-ids covered by the array of accumulators, and it is convenient to make it coincide with the blocks into which the doc-id universe is partitioned. The universe is cut into blocks of $B$ consecutive doc-ids, the same for all the terms, and the array has one slot per doc-id of a block. In this way, a posting stores only its offset within its block, since the list is stored block by block; its doc-id is $ B + $, and its offset is directly the index of its slot. The width $B$ thus fixes the size of the accumulator, and we choose it for the accumulator to stay in the L1 cache. We fix $B = 2^{13}$, the fastest value in our experiments: an accumulator of 32-bit floats takes 32\,KiB and fits in the 48\,KiB L1 data cache of our machine, and an offset fits in 16 bits. A query is evaluated one block at a time, in ascending order, visiting only the blocks in which its terms have postings. Within a block, the query terms are taken one after the other, and the contribution $q_t d_t$ of each of their postings is added to its slot, where $q_t$ and $d_t$ are the weights of term $t$ in the query and in the document. Once all the terms are done, the accumulator holds the exact score of every document of the block. A last pass over the array of accumulators then moves these scores to a heap that keeps the best $k$ documents seen so far, and prepares the accumulator for the next block. The threshold of the heap is $- $ until the heap is full, and then the lowest score it holds. This last pass reads every slot of every visited block, so it must cost little. In its simplest form, it offers each slot to the heap, which accepts it only if it exceeds the threshold, and it resets the slot to zero as it reads it, so the accumulator needs no separate clearing. No slot is tested against zero: documents with a zero score are discarded when the results are collected. Every slot still costs a comparison with the threshold, and SIMD instructions make it cheaper. An AVX2 register holds 8 scores of 32 bits, so four SIMD comparisons check 32 slots at once, with a single branch. Only when this branch is taken does the scan look for the groups of 8 that hold a score above the threshold, and hand their slots to the heap. Once the heap is full the branch is almost never taken, so it is well predicted. Each slot is still reset in the same pass. The representation of a posting list consists of three parts: the blocks in which the term has postings, the offsets of its postings within each of these blocks, and their weights. It is inspired by the elegant representation of Elias and Fano~, from which quasi-succinct indices~, partitioned Elias-Fano~ and Roaring bitmaps~ also drew inspiration: every value is divided into a high part, which selects a bucket of the universe, and a low part, its position within the bucket. In the high part is the block, implicit in the structure of the list, and the low part is the offset. Figure~ shows the layout on one term. We first describe the layout that stores the offsets uncompressed, which is the fastest, and then its compressed variant. To know which blocks to visit, each term has a bitvector with one bit per block, set when the term has postings in the block, and the bitwise OR of the bitvectors of the query terms gives the blocks to visit. The bitvectors take negligible space: with CoCondenser, about 4\,MiB in all, 0.1\ the index of . Each block of a posting list is stored as the offsets of its postings, sorted, in 16 bits each, and their weights in the same order. During a query, the blocks are visited in ascending order and each posting list is read from left to right, so every offset that is read belongs to the block being visited. The bitvector of the term tells whether its list has postings in that block, and a small array of stores where every block of a list begins, so its length is the distance between its start and the next one. Each term thus reads its list with a that only moves forward, and reaching the next block requires no search. Adding a block of a list to the accumulator is a tight loop over its offsets and weights, with no branches on the data. In Figure~, the cursor of $t$ reads the four postings of block 0, adding the contribution of weight 83 to slot 147, skips block 1, and reads the two postings of block 2 and the three of block 5; block 3 is a different case, which we discuss below. The contribution of a posting comes from its weight, stored as an 8-bit integer , a 16-bit float or a 32-bit float . With 8 bits, each posting list has its own affine quantization $d_t m_t + r\,s_t$, where $r$ is the stored integer. The query weight is folded into the two parameters of the list once per query, $q_t d_t + r\, $, so each posting costs a single multiply-add. With 32-bit weights the stored weights are the original ones, so the search returns the true top-$k$, and with 8-bit weights it loses less than 1\ 16-bit weights lose about 0.1\ 2--5\ . The offsets and weights described so far are the of a block of a list. Storing the offsets becomes costly when the block is dense. The of a block of a list is the fraction of the doc-ids of the block in which the term occurs. A dense block is therefore stored in : one weight for every doc-id of the block, 0 for the documents without the term, and no offsets. The weight zero is free to mark the absent documents, because 8-bit weights are quantized to the levels $1, ,255$ and a 16- or 32-bit weight equal to zero contributes nothing; a dense block is thus added to the accumulator with one sequential, vectorized pass. In Figure~, block 3 is stored in dense format. A block is stored in dense format when its density makes this format no larger than offsets and weights: 1/2 with 16-bit weights and 2/3 with 32-bit ones. With 8-bit weights the threshold is 1/4 instead of 1/3; between the two, time and space change by less than 1\ Dhulipala et al.~, in the faster version of Mackenzie et al.~, which we improve in Section~. In the sparse format, every offset still takes 16 bits. ~ compresses a sorted sequence by cutting the gaps between consecutive values into groups, usually of 128, and storing each group with the number of bits of its largest gap. The standard method is recursive graph bisection ~. BP splits the documents into two halves and refines the split with . In each round it estimates, for every document, how much the log-gap cost $ _t _i _2 $ would decrease if the document moved to the other half, and swaps the pairs of documents with the largest gains. After up to 20 rounds it recurses on both halves, down to nodes of 16 documents. It takes into account only the lists of at least 4096 documents, and every round reads all their postings. Its fastest public implementation, the crate~, takes 1.7 minutes on CoCondenser on 20 threads, plus half a minute to prepare its input, while the rest of the build takes seconds. We rewrote BP around three ideas. Log-gap is the cost of an ideal gap coder, while pays, for each group of 8 gaps, the width of the largest one, and a header for each block of a list. Moving documents inside a window of a few hundred doc-ids changes the gaps but rarely the widths, so the index barely notices, while log-gap keeps rewarding such moves. Refining the order inside small windows thus costs full passes over the data and gains almost nothing. We therefore choose every parameter of the bisection by the exact size of the index it produces, computed from the posting lists in a few seconds. With this measure, the recursion stops at nodes of 256 documents instead of 16, which saves four levels without enlarging the index. The blocks of suggest a second choice: a node larger than a block of $B=2^{13}$ documents is split at the multiple of $B$ nearest to its middle. Every block is then one subtree of the recursion, and its documents come from one group of similar documents. Swap rounds converge slowly, and the split itself matters less than it seems. In our experiments, splits that differ in a large share of their documents give indexes of similar size, as long as each half gathers documents that share terms. We therefore run the swap rounds, at most four, only on a of the node, 5\ documents and at least 512, chosen by a hash of the doc-id so that the sample does not depend on the number of threads. The documents that clearly belong to one half end up at its far end, and the undecided ones meet at the cut, so the two children start from an order in which documents that share terms are already close. The pass that scores the documents is bound not by memory but by the chain of floating-point additions that sums the gains of the terms of a document. We compute the gain of each term once per round, as a 16-bit integer, so the gains of a document are summed in four independent integer accumulators, and the pass becomes about twice as fast. The recall of an approximate index is measured against the exact top-$k$ of a set of queries, the ground truth, which also serves to measure the effectiveness of a model. The ground truth is usually computed by exhaustive search, with a multithreaded brute-force scan such as that of vectorium~, the library under and . The tool computes it on all cores with an index, which gives exact scores. The index is not reordered, because at this number of queries reordering costs more than the search time it saves. On the four datasets , the tool takes 15--30\,s at $k=1000$, reading the input and building the index included, and 11--25\,s at $k=10$ with . The brute force of vectorium takes 9--25 minutes, so our tool is 27--52$ $ faster at $k=1000$. Standard IR techniques, combined and engineered for modern CPUs, give a fast exact index with no parameters for learned sparse retrieval. In , term-at-a-time evaluation runs over blocks of doc-ids whose accumulators fit in the L1 cache, so that scoring a posting is a multiply-add into a slot already in cache. Block-Max MaxScore inside these blocks makes faster than at every $k$, and the fastest exact index: an order of magnitude faster than and , and several times smaller than the other block-based indexes. Our blocks and pruning do not depend on learned weights, so they also apply to classic term weights, such as BM25, which are outside the scope of this paper.
