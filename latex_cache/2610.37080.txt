A retrieval pipeline answers a query $q$ with a ranked list $L= $ of evidence items. An item is a text chunk or a screenshot tile, and each item $e_i$ comes from a webpage $ $. The reader receives the top $k$ items, at a cost of $B= _{i k}|e_i|$ tokens under the reader's tokenizer or image processor. We call $C= $ the context, because its elements were sized for indexing. builds a context C' = ), |U | |e_k|, where $U $ is a text block assembled from units of the pages behind $L$. The context keeps the pipeline's best $k-1$ items, which carry continuous prose and the pipeline's ranking signal, and spends the last slot on units. The cost stays at most $B$. The layer is training-free and reads only $q$, $L$, and the HTML of pages $ $. The ranked list is a noisy pointer to passages and a reliable pointer to pages . takes the top $m$ items of $L$ and collects their distinct pages in rank order, = , , ), with $m=10$ by default. Several top items often share a page, so $ $ holds at most $m$ pages. The layer then fetches the HTML of each anchor page from the page store, which is the crawl snapshot that the index was built from. Units are query-independent, so a deployment can cache them per page. A unit is the smallest piece of a page that states a fact once its context is attached. Each unit $u$ has a context $c $ , optional labels $h $ , and a body $b $. Its text is $c $ followed by $b $, with each row value paired with its label, and is self-contained: a reader, or a reranker, can interpret it without the rest of the page. walks the DOM of an anchor page in document order after removing scripts, styles, navigation, and citation superscripts, tracking the section headings above the current position. The walk emits two kinds of units, about 150 per Wikipedia page. Each paragraph, list item, definition term or value, and figure caption becomes one unit. Its context $c $ is the path given by the heading stack. Each row of a table or an infobox becomes one unit. The difficulty is to attach every cell to the right label. Web tables use merged cells, multi-row headers, title rows, and group rows, and the cells of a row in DOM order do not line up with the header cells above them. On the anchor pages of our experiments, pairing cells with the first header row in DOM order assigns a wrong header to 32.5\ Every cell is copied into all grid positions that its and cover, so each grid position holds the cell that a reader of the rendered table would see there. The caption and the leading rows that span the full width become the table title and join $c $. The following rows that consist of header cells form $h $. For multi-row headers, the labels of a column are joined top-down, for example . A full-width row inside the body labels the rows below it and joins their $c $ until the next group row. In a table without column headers whose rows start with a header cell , the first cell is the key. inherit the title and headers of the table that wraps them. Line breaks and list items inside a cell are kept as separators, preserving multi-valued cells . The anchor pages of a question hold about a thousand units, and one slot holds a few dozen. Selection runs in two stages. A lexical prefilter keeps the $N=100$ units whose completed text shares the most content words with the question, which costs no model call. A cross-encoder then scores each remaining pair $ )$; we reuse the pipeline's own text reranker, BGE-reranker-v2-m3, so the layer adds no model to the deployment. Scoring the completed text matters: the context states which match, season, or entity a row belongs to, and the bare row does not reveal it . The packer fills the budget $|e_k|$ of the displaced item. Completion serves the scorer, which judges each unit in isolation; the reader sees the selected units together and needs each context only once, so groups units by context instead of repeating the same path and headers on every row. It visits units in descending score order and admits a unit if its cost fits the remaining budget, where the cost of a unit is its body plus, for the first unit of a group, the group's context line and header line. After selection it renders each group once: the context path, then the column headers, then the member rows as value lines in page order. Groups appear in the order of their best unit. The result reads like an excerpt of the original table , and it fits about 14 lines into a slot that holds about 11 units when each one repeats its context. The packer measures cost with the reader's tokenizer, so the unit block never exceeds the displaced item. In our runs the mean reader prompt with is within 12 tokens of the baseline prompt. The anchor pages are the pages of the top reranked chunks, and the unit block replaces the last chunk. Because units come from the page HTML, they include content that the pipeline's extractor dropped before indexing. The block works as text or as a rendered image . Retrieval-augmented generation grounds a language model in an external corpus~, and the Web is the corpus that matters most in practice: search assistants, deep-research agents, and browsing models all answer from retrieved webpages~. A webpage is a structured document. Its facts sit in infoboxes, tables, lists, and captions as often as in prose, and each fact takes its meaning from the surrounding structure: a number in a scorecard means nothing without its column header, its row label, and the section that names the match. Carrying this structure from page source to reader is a Web-specific challenge for RAG and agentic search, which prior work largely overlooks by retrieving from clean passage corpora that strip tables, infoboxes, and lists~. The standard web RAG pipeline discards this structure early: an extractor such as Trafilatura~ flattens each page to text, a chunker cuts the text into fixed windows, and the reader receives the top few chunks after retrieval and reranking. HTML-centric methods keep pruned markup in the reader's context~. Vision-centric methods skip parsing and index rendered pages as screenshot tiles~; PixelRAG scales this design to the full English Wikipedia and reports that screenshots beat text across six benchmarks~. We review prior work along the pipeline, from indexing to reading. Web RAG systems commonly reduce a page to main-content text before indexing~. Learned extractors~ preserve more structure at a much higher corpus-processing cost, and HtmlRAG~ keeps pruned HTML in the reader's context. Table QA serializes a given table for a language model~ or retrieves tables from a table corpus~, treating each table as standalone. shares the premise that page structure carries information but converts it into explicit context on small units, with no markup tokens or trained models, and selects table rows, merged cells resolved from the HTML, under a budget shared with the surrounding prose. DSE~, ColPali~, VisRAG~, and M3DocRAG~ retrieve rendered pages with vision-language encoders, and PixelRAG~ scales the approach to a full Wikipedia snapshot, motivated by the rich layout information present in webpages. A screenshot tile is a fixed window like a chunk, so complements this line: it uses the top-ranked tiles only to find which pages to open, and builds its units from their HTML . Proposition-level indexing~, small-to-big retrieval~, RAPTOR~, late chunking~, contextual retrieval~, and structure-aware parsers~ all adjust what a chunk is when the corpus is indexed. They pay their cost for every page, and most operate on extracted text, which has already lost the structured evidence. acts at query time on the HTML of the pages a query retrieves and is independent of the index granularity. Query and document expansion~ and cross-encoder reranking~ address earlier stages, and shows that their gains add to ours. Post-retrieval refiners such as RECOMP~ and LongLLMLingua~ shorten retrieved passages to reduce costs and distractions. instead selects complete structured units from the pages behind them and is orthogonal to such refiners.
