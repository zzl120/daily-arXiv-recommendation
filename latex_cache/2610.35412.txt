Existing retrieval methods face two limitations. Diffusion- and similarity-based methods expand retrieval along fixed edge weights or by similarity to the question, without resolving its implicit entity references, allowing relevance errors to accumulate across hops. In contrast, exploration-based methods let an LLM reason about the next hop, but require a separate generation at each hop, leading to high inference costs. We observe that an LLM can resolve implicit entity references from retrieved passages and a multi-hop question before generating any tokens. Further analysis identifies a small set of attention heads, termed addressing heads, that capture this capability across multiple hops in a single forward pass. Building on this finding, we propose , which runs Personalized PageRank on a description-labelled hypergraph, using addressing heads to determine the restart distribution and transition weights, thereby guiding multi-hop retrieval without any generation at query time. On MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and HotpotQA, outperforms the strongest baseline by 3.5 points of Recall@5 and 7.4 points of All@5 on average, with gains that grow with the number of hops. It answers more than twice as fast and builds its index at half the cost. Retrieval-Augmented Generation grounds Large Language Models in external knowledge by retrieving passages from a corpus and conditioning the answer on them~. Retrieval-Augmented Generation enables LLMs to leverage the vast and continuously evolving knowledge available on the Web, supporting more accurate and up-to-date question answering. Standard RAG retrieves passages based on their relevance to the original question, working well for single-hop questions but struggling with multi-hop questions~. In multi-hop questions, some essential evidence may not be directly relevant to the original question but is instead connected through intermediate entities. We call such an intermediate entity a bridge entity, and the question often refers to it only implicitly. For example, to answer , a system must find that the author is Pope John~XXIII and then retrieve his biography, although the question never mentions him. A retriever that compares passages with the question as a whole finds the passage about Princeps Pastorum but rarely the biography, so the second piece of evidence is often missed~. Graph-based RAG organizes passages into a graph and retrieves evidence along its edges, enabling the discovery of evidence passages connected to initially retrieved passages through intermediate entities. While existing systems construct graphs using different representations~, their multi-hop retrieval strategies generally fall into two categories . Diffusion- and similarity-based methods expand retrieval with a fixed procedure: HippoRAG~2~ and LinearRAG~ spread relevance over the graph with Personalized PageRank ~, and PropRAG~ searches paths of propositions by their similarity to the question. Exploration-based methods such as IRCoT~ and Self-Ask~ rely on LLM generation for two tasks: identifying intermediate entities to guide retrieval and inferring and following relationships among entities . These methods iteratively retrieve passages and generate reasoning based on previously retrieved evidence, while exploring entity relationships through textual contexts. {figures/1.pdf} follows the question without generating.} Both strategies have limitations. ~ At each hop, these methods expand retrieval without identifying the entity that the question refers to. For example, PPR propagates relevance scores using fixed edge weights, regardless of which connections are relevant to the question. As shown in Figure~ , the question asks where the author of Princeps Pastorum died but does not explicitly name John~XXIII. While John~XXIII is the relevant bridge entity, PPR also propagates relevance scores to other connected entities that are irrelevant to the question. These irrelevant entities can further propagate scores to their neighbors, causing retrieval errors to accumulate across hops. Similarity-based methods rank passages by their relevance to the original question, often missing essential evidence at later hops that is connected through intermediate entities. ~ First, semantic understanding requires step-by-step LLM generation, where each hop relies on previously retrieved passages and generated reasoning. As the retrieved evidence and reasoning accumulate, the input context grows longer, increasing the computational cost with each hop. Second, relational exploration typically operates over flat textual contexts without explicitly modeling structural relationships among entities, as in graphs. For questions involving complex entity relationships, LLMs must recover and combine relationships scattered across multiple passages. Compared with explicit graph structures, this increases the risk of missing important relationships and may require additional LLM reasoning, further increasing computational costs. These limitations raise a key question: Our strategy is to decouple semantic guidance from structural exploration, which exploration-based methods typically handle together through LLM generation. Instead of relying on LLMs to generate reasoning at every hop, we use LLMs to provide question-aware semantic signals and graph-based computation to explore structural relationships. The first challenge is whether we can leverage LLMs' semantic understanding capabilities without relying on LLM generation. We revisit how LLMs resolve implicit entity references. Interestingly, an LLM resolves implicit entity references while reading the passages and before generating any token~. This resolution is concentrated in a handful of attention heads, which we call . When an LLM reads retrieved passages followed by a multi-hop question, these heads move information from the mentions of each described entity into the words that describe it. For , this information comes from the mention of John~XXIII in the passage about Princeps Pastorum. One forward pass does this for the bridges of several hops, each at the words of its own description. The heads can be identified without labels, and ablating them halves the LLM's accuracy on two-hop questions whose bridge is described. In one forward pass, the addressing heads thus score how strongly the question refers to each entity in the passages and to each of its mentions.\\ The next challenge is how to use these semantic signals to explore relationships among entities without relying on LLM-generated reasoning. To this end, we represent passages and their shared entities in a description-labelled hypergraph and use the addressing scores to steer Personalized PageRank . The hypergraph explicitly captures structural connections among passages through shared entities, while the addressing scores guide the walk toward entities and mentions relevant to the question. In this way, multi-hop relational exploration is performed through graph-based computation rather than step-by-step LLM generation. , short for }graph retrieval via in-context }ing, implements this strategy through two components: an offline description-labelled hypergraph and online attention-steered PageRank. Our contributions are summarized as follows: We show that an LLM reading retrieved passages with a multi-hop question identifies the entity that each description refers to through a handful of addressing heads. These heads can be identified without labels, and the LLM depends on them when a bridge is described. They cover several hops in one forward pass. We propose , a retriever that runs Personalized PageRank on a description-labelled hypergraph and takes both its restart distribution and its transition weights from the addressing heads. On three multi-hop benchmarks, outperforms the strongest of nine baselines, PropRAG, by 3.5 points of Recall@5 and 7.4 points of All@5 on average. Its gains grow with the number of hops, to 6.3 points at four hops on MuSiQue. It generates no token at query time, answers more than twice as fast, and builds its index at half the cost. Graph-based RAG systems differ in what their index stores. Hierarchical indexes store summaries: RAPTOR~ recursively clusters and summarizes passages into a tree, and Microsoft's GraphRAG~ summarizes the communities of an LLM-extracted entity graph. Knowledge-graph indexes store relations between entities. HippoRAG~ and HippoRAG~2~ extract them as open information extraction triples, and LightRAG~ writes LLM-generated descriptions on entities and edges. G-Retriever~ and GFM-RAG~ run graph neural networks over such graphs. Other indexes store propositions~ or groups of related facts~, and HopRAG~ connects passages with LLM-generated questions. Relation-free indexes record only which entities co-occur: LinearRAG~ links passages and sentences through the entities they mention, and HGRAG~ treats entities as vertices and passages as hyperedges. Multi-hop retrieval methods also differ in what decides each hop. In diffusion-based methods, relevance spreads along the edges of the graph from the entities and passages that match the question. HippoRAG, HippoRAG~2, and LinearRAG use Personalized PageRank~, and HGRAG uses hypergraph diffusion. HippoRAG~2 filters the seed facts with an LLM, and GFM-RAG learns query-dependent propagation from labelled questions. PropRAG~ searches proposition paths with a beam search that scores each step by embedding similarity, without an LLM. In exploration-based methods, an LLM decides each hop. IRCoT~ and Self-Ask~ interleave retrieval with generated reasoning or follow-up questions, and Think-on-Graph~ and HopRAG let the LLM choose the next node. ChainRAG~ rewrites each sub-question with the entity found at the previous hop, and BridgeRAG~ ranks later-hop candidates with an LLM judge conditioned on the bridge evidence. EfficientRAG~ trains small models to replace the LLM in this loop. Attention heads have been used to retrieve information from the context. Retrieval heads copy information from long inputs~. ICR~, QRHead~, and BlockRank~ score the passages of a prompt by the attention that the query pays to them. LOCOS~ selects heads by their logit contribution rather than their attention and finds heads that retrieve non-literal matches. The attention of a question-answering reader has also been distilled into a retriever~. Interpretability studies describe how models resolve references in context: through binding IDs~, through lookback mechanisms that retrieve information bound at an earlier position~, or through a mixture of positional and lexical mechanisms~. Other studies trace how a model resolves the bridge entity of a two-hop question~. An LLM that reads retrieved passages together with a multi-hop question identifies the entity that each description in the question refers to before it generates anything. Exploration-based retrieval, however, waits for the LLM to generate every hop. Since a Transformer moves information between positions only through attention, this identification relies on attention heads~. Given a corpus $$ of passages and a question $q$, a retriever ranks the passages so that the evidence for $q$ appears among the first $k$. For a multi-hop question, part of this evidence is about entities to which $q$ refers only through descriptions. A description-labelled hypergraph $= $ has the passages as its vertices and a set $$ of hyperedges, one per entity, each containing the passages that mention its entity. A pair $ $ with $p e$ is an ; it has a weight $w >0$ and a label $D $: the descriptions by which passage $p$ identifies entity $e$ without naming it. A relation is thus stored as the label of the incidence between the passage that states it and the entity that it concerns, rather than as an edge between two entities. An LLM reads every passage once and writes its . For each entity that the passage mentions, an entry gives the canonical name of the entity, its mentions in the passage, and one to three descriptions. Each description identifies the entity through a fact stated in the passage and is phrased the way a question would refer to it. Canonical names and their aliases group the entries of all passages into hyperedges. Each hyperedge also contains every passage that names its entity, and the descriptions label its incidences. An incidence has the weight $w =|e|^{-1/2}$, so that the walk moves less probability through an entity that many passages mention. The LLM also writes the facts that the descriptions leave out and a question that the passage answers. Together with the passage's own sentences and one card per entity, which lists the entity's name with its descriptions, these form the of the passage. A knowledge graph makes entities its nodes and extracted relations its edges. $$ inverts these roles: passages are the vertices, entities the hyperedges, and what a passage says about an entity stays on their incidence, in the words of the passage. This placement serves both stages. Offline, a label is phrased the way a question refers to an entity. The initial restart can therefore match a description and bring the passage that states the relation into the reader's context. Online, the reader identifies the entity from the text of that passage at query time . Since two passages are connected only through an entity, the reader's scores re-weight every transition out of a passage that it has read.
