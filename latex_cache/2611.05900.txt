Personalized retrieval-augmented generation requires extracting and applying user preferences from extended interaction histories. This task is challenging because preference evidence is scattered across past conversations and remains bound to specific temporal and situational contexts. Consequently, isolated fragments rarely provide complete support for a response, while preferences expressed under different conditions are easily conflated. To address these limitations, we propose Weaver, a framework that incrementally weaves memory-conditioned retrieval trajectories with compositional preference reasoning for personalized responses. Weaver operates on a Dual-Layer Retrieval and Reasoning Graph that couples an upper Intent Dependency Graph with a lower Memory-Conditioned Retrieval Trajectory Graph. Dependency Information Gap Tracking represents the information needed to answer the current request and its prerequisite dependencies in the upper graph. Memory-Conditioned Incremental Retrieval uses established premises and clues from retrieved conversations to locate missing evidence, recording the resulting retrieval trajectories in the lower graph. Scope-Aware Compositional Reasoning jointly interprets the accumulated evidence to determine which preferences apply to the request. It identifies complementary and alternative support and distinguishes conflicting claims within the same temporal and situational context. These conclusions iteratively update task premises and remaining information gaps, guiding subsequent retrieval to establish a coherent contextual basis for the final response. Experiments on personalized RAG benchmarks, particularly PersonaMem, demonstrate that Weaver outperforms leading baselines in both evidence completeness and answer quality. These details distinguish the interpretation of a memory, the support for a conclusion, and the completion of the current request. Historical interpretation retains original text together with its source reference, speaker role, and available temporal information. These records allow a conclusion to be traced to the passages that support it. A user statement, an assistant suggestion, and an account of a past experience have different evidential roles. In particular, assistant advice does not establish that the user adopted a preference, and message order alone does not identify the date of a described event. The subject of an experience and its applicable situation must be resolved before it supports a preference for the current request. The structured reading format distinguishes observations that support a candidate value, contradict existing support, provide partial information, remain unknown, or are irrelevant to the local task. These categories describe an interpretation of evidence, not a single task-status sequence. A partial observation can expose a useful search clue without establishing the task output. An observation of support likewise does not by itself establish that the original request has been answered. A support group $s= $ records a complete candidate conclusion, its original memories, its actual premises, and its applicability conditions. The members of $M_s$ may be complementary: their joint interpretation supports $f$, even when none suffices alone. Separate complete groups provide alternative support under Eq.~. Partial observations are not automatically combined into a valid conclusion; their relationship and joint sufficiency must be assessed. When a premise changes, groups that depend on it are reassessed before their conclusions support further retrieval. This reassessment follows the dependency order and can affect downstream conclusions. Independent valid groups can preserve a conclusion when another group loses support. Retrieval-discovery edges remain distinct from these premise dependencies: changing a clue used to locate a passage does not invalidate a conclusion independently supported by that passage. Contradictory evidence can undermine an existing conclusion without establishing its opposite. A replacement conclusion requires its own applicable support. Similarly, two different values need not conflict when they concern disjoint situations. Reinterpreting a historical summary can change its relevance to the current request without asserting that the underlying past events were false. Original sources are retained when a derived conclusion is revised. Algorithms~-- alternate interpretation and evidence acquisition. Available evidence is interpreted before further retrieval. Revised conclusions and local gaps update the relevant tasks and executable frontier; a newly established premise may enable a previously blocked subquestion. Pausing a task preserves its evidence and retrieval records, allowing later findings to reactivate it. Queries made under changed premises retain distinct contexts rather than being treated as identical executions. Intermediate conclusions remain linked to their original sources, so later composition can inspect the evidence behind a premise rather than treating an unsupported summary as an additional observation. An unsuccessful query does not by itself show that all relevant memories have been exhausted. Likewise, an empty frontier is not a certificate of completion. The original request requires sufficient applicable support for its necessary outputs and no unresolved relevant conflict. Its dual-layer graph tracks dependent information needs and guides incremental retrieval using premises and clues from user histories. Scope-Aware Compositional Reasoning jointly interprets the retrieved evidence and updates task conclusions and information gaps, guiding subsequent retrieval. Algorithms~-- summarize this cycle. After initialization, available evidence is interpreted before another query is issued. Gap tracking identifies an executable need, retrieval adds evidence, and composition updates conclusions and local gaps. These updates return to gap tracking, allowing subquestion results to support the original request. The response requirements are met only when the required outputs have sufficient applicable support and no unresolved conflict; an empty retrieval frontier alone does not establish completion. In the upper graph, Weaver uses an LLM to decompose the current request into dependent subquestions and tracks their unresolved information needs as evidence accumulates. Given a request $q$ and its known context $ _q$, Weaver prompts an LLM to identify the information needed for a personalized response and decompose it into subquestions. As predecessor conclusions become available, this expression determines which information remains necessary for downstream reasoning.
