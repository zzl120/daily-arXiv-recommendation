From the service provider's point of view, the value of a recommendation also depends on portfolio performance: the risk-adjusted return the recommended assets deliver, which drives user engagement and fee revenue. The two goals are rarely achieved jointly, because the recommendations that best match user behavior concentrate on a few popular assets whose returns are highly correlated, which caps the risk-adjusted return a provider can offer. We propose Decorrelated-Satellite Expert Re-ranking , which retains the most relevant recommendations from an expert-following list and replaces the rest with return-decorrelated alternatives. The number of replaced slots is a single integer~$m$ that the provider sets, so the relevance--performance trade-off becomes an explicit, auditable choice rather than a property hidden inside a learned model. In experiments on the public FAR-Trans retail-brokerage dataset, under the primary protocol, replacing a few slots improves risk-adjusted return at a small relevance cost, and the gain survives realistic trading costs at typical retail portfolio sizes. Minimizing correlation would lower basket volatility, but in our data the least-correlated assets are more volatile and higher-return, so their volatility offsets that reduction while their return drives the gain, not lower volatility. The resulting trade-off is asymmetric, with the relevance cost consistent across market phases while the gain is phase-dependent. Banks, brokers, and robo-advisors deploy it as a routine service, and researchers study it as a recommender-systems task over transaction histories, with a public multi-algorithm benchmark~ and methods built on real brokerage data~. It guides retail investors who must choose among thousands of assets under a flood of information. Overwhelmed, many settle for a handful of popular names and hold poorly diversified portfolios. FAR is set apart from ordinary recommendation since a useful recommendation must succeed on two distinct axes. The first is : how well the ranked list matches the assets a user actually goes on to buy , which sustains the engagement and retention a provider ultimately depends on~. The second is : the return and risk-adjusted return of the recommended list when held as an equal-weight basket. That benchmark of eleven FAR algorithms found no method superior on both axes~. Optimizing purely for relevance leaves returns near the market average, while optimizing purely for return ignores what users will actually adopt~. A strong, interpretable baseline is expert following~, which ranks assets by what high-performing investors hold and fits no parameters. Its appeal is that this single signal speaks to both axes at once. On the performance side, the past returns of top investors tend to persist~, so the experts' holdings remain competitive in risk-adjusted terms~. On the relevance side, experts are themselves retail investors, so their most-held assets overlap with what typical users go on to buy. The expert-following list's weakness is . Because expert consensus concentrates on a few popular, heavily traded assets, the top-$k$ list has a high average pairwise return correlation. Its assets move together, diversify poorly, and are exposed to common shocks~, which caps the list's risk-adjusted return. Lowering that correlation creates what we call a diversification budget. A less correlated basket is less volatile than its individual assets, so there is room to admit higher-volatility assets without raising the basket's overall risk. In our data this budget is spent on return, not on lower volatility, since the least-correlated assets are higher-return . This motivates raising the risk-adjusted return of an expert-following list by replacing only its lowest-ranked items with decorrelated assets, without materially sacrificing relevance. We propose Decorrelated-Satellite Expert Re-ranking , a plug-in re-ranker that controls the trade-off between the two objectives. It keeps the expert signal as the recommendation's relevance backbone while explicitly decorrelating its constituents . We frame it as a transparent control and characterize it on the curated, liquid universe a provider actually operates in. Concretely, it retains the top $k-m$ expert items as a relevance core and fills the remaining $m$ satellite slots with the assets whose mean correlation to those already chosen is lowest. For instance, at $k{=}10$ and $m{=}2$ it keeps the eight strongest expert picks and replaces the last two slots with the least-correlated available assets. The single integer $m$ is the provider's only control, set rather than learned. DSER's criterion is the assets' full return-correlation structure, not the diagonal variance penalty of the nearest re-ranker RURA~, which DSER outperforms on the combined score . As a recommendation task~, FAR divides precisely along the axis this paper targets. Relevance-oriented methods predict what a user will buy, including news- and sentiment-based learning-to-rank~, attention- and personality-aware recommenders~, and collaborative filtering for assets~. Return-oriented methods instead optimize financial outcomes, such as large-scale portfolio-optimization recommendation~. A benchmark on FAR-Trans shows that transaction-based relevance is only a proxy for portfolio performance and that no method dominates on both axes~. We adopt FAR-Trans, the first public dataset with an eleven-algorithm benchmark, and address this tension directly. A related family builds the risk objective into the model itself, on standard architectures such as pairwise ranking , graph collaborative filtering , and temporal graph networks over the transaction stream~. The most direct precursor, PfoTGNRec~, couples a temporal graph recommender with mean-variance-aware sampling on FAR-Trans. MVECF~ folds mean-variance efficiency into the collaborative-filtering loss. These models embed diversification in learned weights and expose no explicit control over it. In contrast, DSER externalizes the trade-off as a thin, model-agnostic re-ranking layer that in principle sits on top of any such ranker. {figures/overall_pipeline.pdf} Holding weakly correlated assets is the foundation of Modern Portfolio Theory~, and the Sharpe ratio~ is the standard measure of the risk-adjusted return that theory targets. Estimates of a full covariance matrix are unstable in high dimensions, motivating shrinkage estimators~. The equal-weight portfolio is famously hard to beat once estimation error is accounted for~, which is why our baskets are equal-weighted. Re-ranking for diversity is itself well studied in general recommendation, including maximal marginal relevance ~, topic diversification~, and determinantal point processes~. Yet these notions of diversity are content- or category-based rather than based on return covariance. In finance, the closest work is RURA~, which re-ranks upstream candidates by a risk-aware utility with a variance-based penalty and a single diversification knob~$ $. Our contribution is the financial diversity criterion, not the re-ranking framework. Sections~ and~ show that our integer knob sacrifices nothing relative to a $ $-swept MMR in the operating region providers use, and that the return-correlation criterion outperforms a variance-only rule, including a reimplementation of RURA following its published description. This is consistent with the low-correlation tail carrying a return premium that a variance-only rule forgoes. Following the trades of top-performing investors is a strong, interpretable, and competitive baseline on FAR-Trans~. Its weakness is precisely crowding. The expert consensus concentrates on a few popular assets, so the top-$k$ basket is internally highly correlated. DSER relaxes this structure while keeping the expert signal as the relevance backbone. One caution is specific to this setting. Stale prices and return smoothing~ depress a thinly traded asset's measured variance and correlation, mechanically inflating its realized Sharpe ratio. This threatens any correlation-based re-ranker, which may ``discover'' spuriously decorrelated stale assets. We therefore restrict the recommendable universe to a curated, liquid asset pool, as prior work does~. We then report the after-cost capacity of the satellites under a liquidity-based impact model . At each decision time, a provider must issue a size-$k$ ranked list that balances the two objectives from Section~. Relevance is scored against each user's subsequent purchases, and portfolio performance is the risk-adjusted return of the list held as an equal-weight basket. DSER takes the provider's expert-following ranking as input and re-ranks it into this list. We design DSER around the provider's problem rather than a model class. DSER does not fit a predictive model, although its correlation estimates and design choices remain sample-dependent. We scope every claim to a curated, liquid universe of exchange-listed equities and to the expert-following backbone we test, and do not claim transfer to uncurated or multi-asset universes, where stale prices and illiquidity make correlations unreliable. Retention and revenue are proxied by risk-adjusted return, not measured. That return is a basket-level provider measure, not any single user's realized return. Our relevance metrics are offline and observational. They score each list against purchases users made without seeing it, so they cannot show whether the re-ranked list would change what users buy, which needs an online test. Because the top $k{-}m$ items are identical to the expert list, the relevance change comes entirely from the $m$ replaced slots. nDCG and HR credit an item only if the user organically bought it, so thinly traded satellites are structurally penalized , making the measured relevance cost plausibly an upper bound. The satellite return premium may partly reflect a neglected-stock effect. DSER beats uniform random breadth by $+0.09$ SR at $m{=}4$ , but its minimum-correlation rule also picks thinner names, so this gap does not separate the correlation effect from size and liquidity factors, a separation that would need factor data beyond FAR-Trans. The expert-following list that best balances relevance and portfolio performance exhibits internal crowding. DSER, a transparent, training-free re-ranker, keeps the expert core and fills $m$ satellite slots with the least-correlated assets, turning the relevance--performance trade-off into a single provider-controlled knob. Under the primary overlapping-window protocol, $m{=}4$ increases SR from 1.794 to 2.119 while reducing nDCG from 0.391 to 0.362. The $m{=}2$ gain survives realistic trading costs at retail scale but erodes as portfolios grow. Across $6$ non-overlapping windows, the Sharpe change is positive in $4$, while the relevance change is negative in all $6$. The knob's cost is thus consistent and its benefit phase-dependent, so DSER gives the provider an explicit, auditable operating point on the relevance--performance trade-off. Future work includes validation on a second brokerage dataset, extension to multi-asset universes, personalization of the satellites to each user's holdings, and evaluating diversification at the level of each user's resulting portfolio.
