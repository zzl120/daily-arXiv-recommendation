Over the past decade, CF has moved from classical matrix-factorization-style systems to graph-based formulations that view the user-item interaction matrix as a bipartite graph~. Unlike matrix factorization, which captures only single-step user-item associations, the bipartite graph lets evidence travel along multi-hop paths such as $u_1 \! \! i_1 \! \! u_2 \! \! i_2$, recommending $i_2$ to $u_1$ through a user $u_2$ who shares an item with $u_1$ even when the two share no other item~. The standard graph-based CF pipeline, however, considers interactions only: a user-item edge exists when the user interacted with the item, and is otherwise absent. Many production platforms also collect feedback, such as 1-star Amazon reviews~, hate-button clicks in KuaiRand~, user-to-user distrust in Epinions~, and very-low watch-ratio videos in KuaiRec~. Unlike an absent edge in the user-item interaction graph, which may simply mean the user has not yet seen the item, this signal reveals what the user actively dislikes. A line of recommenders has emerged to use both signs: SiReN~ and PANE-GNN~ split the bipartite graph by sign and fuse the halves through learned attention, NFARec~ models signed feedback sequences with a Transformer Hawkes process, and SIGformer~ and DFGNN~ fold signed spectral information into a Transformer and a dual-frequency GNN, respectively. More recent signed models unify the dual encoders into a single encoder or apply a Gegenbauer-basis spectral convolution to signed bipartite graphs . These methods deliver clear accuracy gains from the additional signal, but each is gradient-based and considerably more expensive than the closed-form spectral methods that our training-free framework, , builds on. A complementary line of work treats each user's interaction row as a signal on the item-item similarity graph and produces recommendations by passing it through a low-pass filter that suppresses noisy high-frequency components while retaining smooth low-frequency ones. The pipeline is closed-form and , yet it matches or beats learned graph neural network recommenders at a fraction of the cost. GF-CF~ showed that this signal processing view recovers the accuracy of GCN-based CF; later work made it faster or more flexible by replacing the eigendecomposition with polynomial filters or by adding a sharpening step that emphasizes top items . Closed-form positive-only methods such as EASE~ and PSGE~ likewise avoid gradient training, while SpectralCF~, JGCF~, and GDE~ vary the polynomial basis or reweight spectral features. SpectralCF~ first reformulated CF as spectral graph filtering with a learned first-order polynomial, and GF-CF~ reframed the problem as graph signal processing and showed that most CF methods are special cases of filtering on the item-item similarity graph. EASE~ is a canonical training-free positive-only closed-form baseline obtained by a single matrix inversion, and PSGE~ likewise bypasses gradient descent by leveraging the top eigenvectors of the normalized interaction matrix directly. Turbo-CF~ replaced the costly eigendecomposition with polynomial approximations in the item-item similarity matrix. BSPM~ introduced a blurring-sharpening ODE that generalizes GF-CF. ChebyCF~ achieved state-of-the-art accuracy via Chebyshev-interpolated filter design. PolyCF~ proposed learnable polynomial filters, and further spectral-CF refinements include JGCF~ and GDE~ . All of these methods share a common form: they express the predicted preference as $}_u = F \,_u$, where $F$ is a scalar filter applied to an item-item operator $$ and $_u$ is the user's positive interaction row. This shared structure is what makes a single sign-aware framework applicable across backbones. All operate on positive-only interactions; none incorporates negative feedback. SiReN~ splits the interaction graph by sign, encoding $^+$ with a GNN and $^-$ with an MLP, then fusing via attention. PANE-GNN~ extends this with dual interest/disinterest embeddings and contrastive denoising. NFARec~ combines a Transformer-Hawkes encoder with feedback-aware hypergraph convolution. SIGformer~ uses sign-aware spectral and path encodings in a Transformer, achieving the best accuracy but at $8$--$155 $ higher wall-clock time than training-free spectral methods. Most directly related to our design, DFGNN~ applies a learned low-pass filter to $^+$ and a high-pass filter to $^-$; is the training-free, backbone-agnostic counterpart, which replaces per-sign filter shapes with a single filter on a signed input signal and signed operator. LSGRec~ argues that dual-encoder methods sever high-order heterogeneous paths across signed links, and proposes a unified single-encoder alternative; our signed-operator component $^{ }$ addresses the same concern from the training-free side. GegenNet~ applies a Gegenbauer polynomial basis to signed bipartite graphs for link sign prediction, a contemporaneous spectral-signed reference that targets a different task than top-$K$ recommendation. The signed Laplacian we use is well-posed: its eigenvalues lie in $ $ with a total-variation interpretation~. All published sign-aware methods are learned models; no prior work combines signed Laplacians with training-free spectral filtering, and no prior framework is generic across spectral backbones. We discovered a latent bug in the upstream BSPM~ implementation that produces near-zero accuracy on datasets containing zero-degree items. BSPM constructs the normalized item degree matrix $_I^{-1/2}$ from the positive training adjacency matrix and then computes its inverse $_I^{1/2}$ via element-wise reciprocal. When an item has zero degree in the positive training set , the code correctly sets $_I^{-1/2}$ to zero at that position, but then computes $1/0 = $ for the inverse. These infinite values propagate through BSPM's ideal low-pass function and corrupt the top-$K$ ranking for every user. The three datasets on which BSPM was originally evaluated have no zero-degree items after standard preprocessing: every item appears in at least one positive training interaction. The bug manifests only on datasets where some items appear exclusively in negative feedback, a property shared by all five of our sign-aware benchmarks, which contain 7--306 zero-degree items in the positive-only training split. Without the fix, BSPM produces Recall@20 below $0.001$ on three of five datasets ; KuaiRec is severely degraded at $0.002$ and Amazon-Music happens to have no zero-degree items and is only modestly affected . We replace the naive reciprocal with a zero-preserving inverse that sets $ _{ii} = 0$ whenever $ _{ii} = 0$, matching the convention already used for $_I^{-1/2}$ itself. This four-line patch ensures that zero-degree items contribute zero to the ideal low-pass output rather than corrupting it with infinite values.
