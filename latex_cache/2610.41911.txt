Unlike a printed guide, the location-based recommender systems behind these online platforms learn from the visits they steer, forming a human--AI feedback loop that affects both people and places~. For people, a crucial outcome at stake is : how unevenly an individual's time at venues spreads across the income groups~. Low experienced income segregation means a person's daily places mix incomes, creating chances for cross-income ties, which predict upward income mobility~. For places, a key outcome at stake is : the share of venues that receive no visits from simulated residents. The feedback loop can concentrate visits on already popular venues~, and a venue without visits is then no longer recommended. Venue disappearance matters for small businesses that depend on online visibility to attract customers~, as well as for the neighbourhoods they serve. As the feedback loop unfolds and the recommender system keeps retraining, both outcomes can build up over time, and their burden can fall unevenly across income groups and neighbourhoods. These effects of the feedback loop are distributional, long-term consequences of algorithmic deployment on the Web. They extend beyond the screen into residents' lived experiences of the city and bear on the fairness of the urban environments that online platforms increasingly help to shape. Understanding and mitigating them is therefore integral to building a responsible Web~. Yet these algorithms are rarely evaluated for their long-term effects on communities, including how they shape segregation across income groups and the distribution of visits across local businesses. Standard offline accuracy measures assess how well an algorithm predicts past behaviour, leaving these collective outcomes largely unexamined. This test evaluates the recommender system once, using data describing visits that were not shaped by its recommendations. Therefore, it cannot capture experienced income segregation or venue disappearance that may emerge as the feedback loop unfolds~. Although regulation has begun to require platforms to assess the systemic risks of their recommender systems~, methods for assessing such long-term effects are still lacking. Prior work has mainly studied human--AI feedback loops for online items such as songs, movies and products~, which any number of users can consume without affecting one another. Urban venues, by contrast, differ from online items in at least three respects: People choose venues near their current location and at times that fit their daily routines; recommendations therefore shape choices within a small set of locally accessible options. A restaurant can serve only a limited number of customers at a time; when recommendations send many people to the same venue, some must wait or go elsewhere. Visitors to a venue share the same physical space; as a result, a recommendation to an individual also changes whom the other visitors meet. To the best of our knowledge, no existing study can capture these three factors together. Audits find that review sites and LLM assistants expose venues unevenly across neighbourhoods~, but they do not measure how exposure changes as recommendations and user behaviour feed back on one another. Simulations can reproduce the feedback loop, but they simplify the setting: some use synthetic users~, others focus on online content or retail goods~. The closest urban simulation study measures how venue concentration changes throughout the feedback loop but does not account for venue capacity nor measure experienced income segregation~. Taken together, these gaps leave three research questions unanswered: How does retraining within the feedback loop affect experienced income segregation and venue disappearance? How do different recommendation algorithms shape experienced income segregation and venue disappearance? Can an algorithmic intervention reduce venue disappearance and experienced income segregation within the feedback loop? In recent years, mobility data have enabled the study of experienced segregation, measured in the places people visit every day rather than where they live~. Various studies relate experienced segregation to city size~, disaster shocks~, or venue characteristics~, but usually measure it in the movements people actually made. As experienced income segregation is associated with individuals' mobility patterns~, and location-based recommender systems increasingly steer where people go~, these systems may change whom people meet. Research on recommender systems has examined how unevenly platforms expose venues across neighbourhoods~ and how recommendations narrow or concentrate what users consume~, but not their effects on experienced income segregation. It has thus overlooked a critical social consequence that accumulates over time and shapes residents' lived experience of the city. We address this gap by simulating three years of weekly retraining on real mobility data, and measuring how each recommender system changes whom residents meet across incomes. A recommender system that retrains on the interactions it has shaped forms a feedback loop. For each user, such loops narrow the set of items the user is exposed to over time~ and amplify the user's preferences toward recommended content~. Across users, they homogenise behaviour without increasing utility~, an effect distinct from the narrowing of each user's consumption~, and they amplify popularity bias through a rich-get-richer effect~. Their net effect on diversity depends on the algorithm and the setting: simulations find that the diversity of each user's consumption can rise while aggregate diversity falls~, whereas a randomised field experiment on Spotify found the reverse~. Nearly all of this evidence concerns online items such as songs, movies and products, which any number of users can consume without affecting one another. Urban venues differ in three respects: choices are bounded by spatio-temporal constraints~, each venue has finite capacity, and, as venues are shared physical spaces, recommendations produce spillover effects on non-recipients~ by altering the composition of co-present visitors. Closest to our work, Mauro et al.~ simulate weekly retraining of location-based recommender systems in New York and Tokyo, but the simulation does not model venue capacity, and check-ins, being voluntary, capture only a small share of residents and their visits. We address these limitations by simulating residents on their GPS-recorded daily schedules in four large U.S. metropolitan areas over three years of weekly retraining under spatio-temporal and capacity constraints, while retaining the observed visits of all other individuals at each venue, so that recommendations to simulated residents also affect co-present non-recipients. In our simulation, residents follow their observed daily schedules, and a location-based recommender system suggests where they eat on each food trip . We restrict our simulation to food trips for two reasons. First, trips that platforms rarely recommend, such as those to home or to work, would mix their effects with those of the recommendations. Second, restaurant visits mix people from different neighbourhoods and are about half as segregated as where people live~, making restaurants an appropriate setting to measure the effect of recommender systems on complex social systems. The simulation captures the three factors that distinguish urban venues from online items: residents keep their observed schedules of all trips and travel distances, each venue has a finite capacity, and a recommendation to one resident also changes whom the other visitors meet. We use anonymised GPS mobility data provided by Cuebiq, which record people's visits to venues from October 2016 to March 2017. In each metropolitan area, we simulate residents who made at least 200 recorded visits, visited at least 10 distinct food venues, and visited food venues throughout the six-month observation period. Following the methodology in , we infer each resident's home census block group as the one where they are most often seen between 10 pm and 6 am, rank census block groups within each metropolitan area by their median household income from the American Community Survey five-year estimates~, and assign each resident the income quartile of their home block group. This provides a neighborhood-level proxy for income rather than a measure of individual income. We draw equal numbers of residents from the four income quartiles, 4,000 in Boston, San Francisco and Detroit and 8,000 in Chicago. During the first four months, the training window, these residents visited between 8,946 and 21,852 food venues per metropolitan area. Over the observed six months, their visits cover 95.5--99.4\ Each resident keeps the dates and durations of their observed trips, and we simulate only which venue each food trip goes to. To cover about three years, each resident's schedule repeats every six months. The other people in the mobility data, between 65,525 and 290,977 per metropolitan area, are not simulated. We keep their real visits unchanged, and they count among each venue's visitors when we measure experienced income segregation . We develop a simulation of the feedback loop between residents and location-based recommender systems, drawing on previous work~ while incorporating large-scale mobility data and realistic constraints on travel behavior and venue capacity. For each food trip, the recommender system suggests venues within reach, and the resident either follows the suggestions or keeps their observed visit. To keep each trip within a realistic travel distance, we draw a search radius for each trip from the distances that residents travelled to venues they had not visited before during the training window. The recommendations are the $K$ venues that the algorithm ranks highest among those that lie within this radius of the resident's last venue and still have capacity. If no such venue lies within the radius, we draw a new radius. To account for finite capacity, we give each venue a weekly capacity of $c$ times the residents' observed visits to it in the corresponding week of the observed six months. Once a venue's visits in a simulated week reach this capacity, it is no longer recommended until the next week. Setting $c>1$ allows for busy weeks and for customers willing to wait, while keeping each venue's capacity finite. We model the probability that a resident follows the recommendations through an adoption rate $a$~; otherwise, the resident goes to the venue of their observed visit. A resident who follows the recommendations picks one of the $K$ venues with probability proportional to its min--max scaled score. At the end of each simulated week, the recommender system is retrained on the residents' trajectories of the previous four months, including the visits that followed its own recommendations, which closes the feedback loop. The simulation runs for about three years. A location-based recommender system affects both the people who follow its suggestions and the venues they visit. For people, we measure experienced income segregation, and for venues, venue disappearance. Both outcomes are restricted to the training venues, the food venues that residents visited during the initial four-month training window. We also report offline accuracy, the measure platforms typically use to evaluate recommender systems. Each simulated week, we compute income segregation and venue disappearance using visits from the preceding three months. We report the average of these weekly measures over the final six months of each simulation, when the feedback loop has been running for more than two and a half years. For people, the outcome is whom they share places with across incomes. People form ties across incomes in the places they share, and such ties are among the strongest predictors of upward income mobility~. Mobility data show that whom people meet depends on where they spend their day as well as on where they live~. By steering where people eat, a recommender system therefore also shapes whom they can meet across incomes, and with it their chances of moving up the income ladder. Location-based recommender systems that continually learn from the visits they steer can reshape both forms of opportunity, yet offline accuracy alone does not capture these collective effects. Our simulations lead to three conclusions. First, offline evaluation is not a neutral test of how these systems affect people and places. It tests each algorithm only once, on visits that people made before the recommender system was in use, overlooking the effects of the human--AI feedback loop~. For places, most of the venue disappearance arises after deployment, through retraining, which offline evaluation never observes. For people, offline accuracy rewards the same returns to familiar places that keep people segregated, and it ranks first the algorithm under which people are most segregated in the feedback loop. Offline evaluation also rewards only predicting where people went before, and it therefore penalizes tail-boost, the re-ranking that lowers both segregation and venue disappearance. Second, the effects of the feedback loop fall unevenly across a city. For places, the feedback loop amplifies popularity bias across neighbourhoods, and less active, lower-income neighbourhoods lose the most venues. Venues without visits drop out of the data on which the recommender system is retrained, and their small businesses lose the platform they rely on to attract customers~. Across all four metropolitan areas, UR-POI further increases segregation among the lowest-income quartile, the group already most segregated in the offline data. Third, no algorithm is best on offline accuracy, experienced income segregation, and venue disappearance at once, and the last two can be measured only inside the feedback loop. Our results suggest that Web platforms and auditors should simulate the feedback loop with real mobility data before an algorithm is deployed, and report experienced income segregation and venue disappearance alongside offline accuracy~. With tail-boost, platforms can lower both outcomes without replacing their algorithm, at a modest cost in offline accuracy and without sacrificing residents' comfortableness. Our results can inform targeted requests for platforms to mitigate or compensate for the effects we observe. In this respect, our methodology aligns with the EU Digital Services Act~, which requires very large online platforms to assess and mitigate systemic risks to society, including risks to fundamental rights and public well-being. Our study has three limitations that point to future work. First, the simulation keeps the human side of the feedback loop simple. Residents keep the dates and durations of their observed trips, and the main setting assumes an adoption rate of 1, at which every food trip follows the recommendations. Even so, in Boston the order of the algorithms by segregation holds at every adoption rate and venue capacity we tested . Second, the mobility data are anonymized to protect privacy and carry no personal incomes, and each resident's income quartile is therefore that of their home block group. Finally, simulating the feedback loop is computationally intensive~. We therefore tested classic algorithms with LightGCN as a neural reference, leaving heavier sequential algorithms and LLM assistants, which already expose venues unevenly across neighbourhoods~, for future work. In conclusion, recommendation research has long evaluated each recommendation as an exchange between one user and one item. In cities, every followed recommendation also changes whom other people meet and which venues stay visited, and these collective effects accumulate with each retraining. Before deployment, we can estimate them only through simulations that realistically model where people can go, how many people each venue can serve, and whom they share it with. With such simulations, platforms and auditors can anticipate how recommender systems on Web platforms reshape the social fabric and the local economy of cities. {} Our anonymised, privacy-enhanced GPS mobility data are provided by Cuebiq and come from users who opted in to share their location under frameworks compliant with the GDPR and the CCPA. To preserve this protection, we locate each resident's home and income only at the level of the census block group and report only aggregates over residents, venues and census tracts, never individual trajectories or named venues. Platforms could use our simulation to select the most profitable algorithm regardless of its effects on segregation and venue disappearance.
