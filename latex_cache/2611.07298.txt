Dense retrieval models encode queries and documents into fixed-dimensional representations and compare them in a shared embedding space to enable highly efficient semantic matching. In practice, these models typically obtain sequence-level embeddings through pooling or token-selection strategies, such as , mean, or last-token pooling~. Although these strategies provide a simple way to aggregate contextualized token representations, they also create a severe compression bottleneck, as heterogeneous token-level information is collapsed into a single retrieval vector, often causing fine-grained semantic details to be lost. Prior work has recognized this bottleneck in dense passage retrieval and proposed specialized pre-training strategies to improve the quality of the compressed representation~. However, while these methods enhance the general representation space, they do not inherently resolve the internal routing of noisy or uninformative signals during the actual encoding phase. Recent studies on Transformer attention dynamics further suggest that not all token-level information contributes equally to the final semantic representation. In particular, the attention sink phenomenon shows that certain tokens can attract disproportionately large attention scores despite carrying limited semantic content~. Driven by the softmax operation that forces attention allocation~, such behavior can help stabilize autoregressive generation, but it inadvertently introduces representational interference in embedding models, where the pooled vector is strictly expected to reflect the pure semantic content of the input. Related observations have also been made in vision Transformers, where learnable register tokens are introduced to absorb high-norm outlier information and reduce non-semantic artifacts in feature maps~. These studies directly motivate our use of trainable prefix tokens to provide a dedicated structural pathway that absorbs residual or non-semantic information during retrieval encoding, yielding cleaner and more discriminative sequence representations. Embedding models have become a fundamental component of modern retrieval systems, where queries and passages are mapped into a shared vector space for efficient semantic matching~. Their effectiveness heavily depends on the ability to compress variable-length text into fixed-dimensional representations while preserving the critical semantic information relevant to retrieval across diverse domains. This compression is typically performed through pooling or token-selection strategies~, effectively forcing the model to funnel rich contextual dynamics into a narrow informational bottleneck. However, this process can also introduce representational interference: token-level information that is not directly useful for semantic matching, such as recurrent positional patterns, special-token artifacts, or other non-semantic cues, may be absorbed into the embedding representation and distort its true semantic content~. When this noise accumulates, it fundamentally limits the discriminative power of the resulting embeddings in downstream retrieval tasks. This issue becomes particularly important in light of the attention sink phenomenon, where certain positions attract disproportionately large attention despite carrying limited semantic content~. Since the final retrieval representation is obtained by pooling or selecting from contextualized token representations, such artificial attention bias can cause non-semantic information to become deeply entangled with content-bearing tokens~, thereby diluting the overall sequence representation. Standard retrieval fine-tuning addresses this issue only indirectly: contrastive objectives improve query--passage alignment at the macro embedding level, but they do not explicitly control how residual token-level information is represented or routed before pooling. Consequently, conventional retrieval optimization may still leave residual token-level information entangled with the embedding representation, leaving a gap in how we optimize dense retrievers. In this work, we focus on how such residual information is handled during retrieval encoding by proposing a simple yet highly effective architectural intervention. We use trainable tokens as input prefixes during embedding model training. These prefix tokens participate in contextualization before the sequence is compressed into a single embedding, acting as dedicated semantic buffers. By absorbing attention associated with uninformative signals, they help reduce the influence of residual token-level information that would otherwise be mixed into the embedding representation. Crucially, the method does not require modifying the core model architecture, introducing auxiliary losses, or changing the retrieval objective, making it a highly scalable plug-and-play solution.
