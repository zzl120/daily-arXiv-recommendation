It represents user behavioral histories as sequences of discrete semantic tokens and reformulates candidate item retrieval as autoregressive generation via beam search over a codebook space. Recent studies demonstrate that GR substantially enhances the modeling of complex behavioral semantics and user preferences, driving its widespread adoption in industrial recommender systems~. However, in industrial recommender systems, GR often faces a severe online inference efficiency bottleneck caused by extremely long user behavioral sequences~. To retrieve a list of candidate items, a generative recommender decodes multiple independent beams in parallel. At each decoding step, every beam computes full attention between its current query and the entire behavioral history~, causing the decoding cost to scale linearly with history length. In practice, scaling the history length by $4 $ leads to a $2.46 $ increase in inference latency. This prohibitive overhead makes it challenging for GR to meet the stringent latency constraints of large-scale recommender systems with long user interaction histories. Existing approaches to reducing the decoding cost of full attention over long behavioral histories fall into two main categories: This approach aggregates historical interactions via predefined rules or lightweight models prior to decoding, thereby shortening the history accessed by the decoder~. However, because compression is decoupled from decoding, it struggles to capture attention patterns that vary across decoding steps ). Consequently, critical semantic information required at specific steps may be irreversibly lost. This method addresses the above limitation by dynamically retrieving the top-$k$ most relevant historical tokens based on the current query at each decoding step~. While this approach adapts to step-wise attention patterns and preserves step-specific critical semantics, its efficiency gains remain limited under beam search. Specifically, because each beam independently selects top-$k$ tokens at every step, it induces frequent non-contiguous key-value cache accesses~, severely degrading inference efficiency . Although different beams can share the KV cache of the historical sequence, this cache must still be repeatedly read from memory at every decoding step. Coupled with multi-step decoding and beam search, these factors establish attention over long histories as a primary inference bottleneck in GR. Static history compression mitigates decoding costs by merging tokens or aggregating features prior to decoding, condensing the full history representations into a shorter sequence. Representative methods such as LONGER~ and Q-Former~ follow this paradigm, which can be formulated as: }=f_{} ^{M d}, M<N, where $f_{} $ denotes the compression function and $M$ is the compressed sequence length. During decoding, $}$ replaces the full history $$ as the context for attention at every step, reducing both the attention computation and KV memory access overhead. However, because compression is performed once before decoding and remains static across decoding states, it cannot adapt to evolving information needs across steps and risks discarding fine-grained semantics required by later tokens. Once lost, this information cannot be recovered during subsequent decoding, ultimately degrading recommendation quality. To prevent premature information loss, dynamic top-$k$ sparse attention leverages the current decoding query at each step to retrieve the top-$k$ most relevant historical tokens for attention. While this strategy adapts to step-specific information needs, it requires each beam to independently perform token selection at every decoding step, introducing substantial selection overhead. Moreover, it necessitates retaining the full-history KV cache in memory. Because the selected tokens are typically scattered across non-contiguous memory locations, gathering their KV states incurs irregular memory access and high data movement overhead. Consequently, the theoretical FLOPs reduction from attending to fewer tokens rarely translates into actual wall-clock inference speedup. Unlike general natural language generation, where attention patterns often shift dynamically across tokens, GR exhibits two distinctive attention properties during autoregressive decoding: and . First, attention becomes progressively focused on a small subset of critical historical tokens, motivating a progressively shrinking token budget as decoding proceeds. Second, critical tokens maintain consistent importance rankings across steps, and critical tokens identified early continue to capture most of the attention mass in subsequent steps, justifying the reuse of early rankings. However, conventional dynamic top-$k$ sparse attention methods overlook these GR-specific properties; by repeatedly retrieving over the full history and maintaining a static token budget at every step, they incur substantial redundant computation. In contrast, our key insight is that the model can establish a shared importance ranking of historical tokens early in decoding and progressively prune the tokens participating in attention. A typical paradigm maps items to SIDs composed of discrete codewords, and then autoregressively generates target identifiers conditioned on user context~. Subsequent studies align item representations with the generation process by enriching codewords with content semantics and collaborative signals~, or by jointly optimizing tokenizers and recommenders toward recommendation objectives~. However, as interaction histories accumulate, accurately capturing long-term preferences while maintaining efficient autoregressive inference remains a pressing bottleneck. Traditional discriminative recommenders leverage target candidates as queries to retrieve or aggregate relevant historical behaviors~, or reduce computational overhead via feature compression, sparse access, and external memory~. Lacking explicit target candidates during inference, generative recommenders must preserve diverse user interests while constraining history access throughout autoregressive search. Consequently, one line of work focuses on history pre-compression and state maintenance. For instance, CAUSE aggregates long-term behaviors by category alongside recent interactions within HSTU~. To achieve internal denoising and multi-granular fusion, RASTP prunes uninformative history tokens at intermediate Transformer layers based on semantic saliency and attention centrality~; GRACE integrates block compression, query-aware block selection, coarse journey tokens, and recent sliding windows via gating mechanisms~. For streaming updates, Rec2PM jointly encodes existing preference memory and incoming behavior segments, recurrently maintaining memory tokens for subsequent predictions~. Another line of research retrieves historical behaviors on demand using intermediate decoding signals, such as partially generated prefixes or current-step queries. DualGR predicts a coarse SID to retrieve behaviors from the corresponding bucket for fine-grained decoding~; similarly, GLASS summarizes long-term interests for first-codeword prediction, and then uses this codeword to retrieve relevant history and gate it into subsequent decoding steps~. GEMs dynamically selects top-$k$ mid-term KV entries via a lightweight query-based indexer, combining them with recent interactions and pre-compressed lifelong representations~. While these methods mitigate computational costs through compression and single-step retrieval, they largely overlook the multi-step nature of autoregressive decoding when modeling long-sequence user histories in GR---specifically, how to efficiently share and reuse historical context across decoding steps and candidate beams. Research on large language models has established several sequence-modeling paradigms to accelerate long-context inference. Common strategies include compressing long contexts into shorter text or summary vectors~; recurrently updating hidden states via linear attention or state-space models to bypass full-history traversal~; and reducing attention complexity through local windows and content-aware token or block selection~. As a mainstream paradigm, top-$k$ dynamic sparse attention substantially reduces attention FLOPs, yet online history scoring and selection often emerge as new latency bottlenecks~. To eliminate redundant selection overhead, recent studies explore index and state reuse across multiple dimensions. For example, IndexCache and ChunkKV share retrieval indices across layers~---a strategy adopted in frontier models such as GLM-5.2~ and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash~---while other works share selected context across decoding steps to amortize selection costs~. This reuse philosophy has recently been extended to generative recommendation: OneLA shares historical recurrent states of linear attention across beams to accelerate wide-beam search~. Nevertheless, for standard softmax attention mechanisms that remain widely adopted, designing efficient long-sequence modeling tailored to the specific decoding structure of generative recommendation remains largely underexplored. In this paper, we present PFA, an efficient sparse decoding framework for long-sequence generative recommendation. Specifically, we identify two key properties of historical attention during autoregressive decoding: progressive attention focusing and cross-step ranking consistency. Motivated by these observations, PFA retains full attention for initial preference exploration and constructs a shared token importance ranking through cross-beam weighted aggregation. By reusing this ranking and geometrically reducing the token budget in subsequent steps, PFA preserves critical historical semantics while minimizing redundant token selection overhead and KV cache access. Furthermore, we incorporate a step-aware KV projection to capture the distinct semantic requirements across different decoding steps. Extensive offline experiments on real-world datasets and online A/B testing in a commercial advertising system demonstrate that PFA simultaneously improves recommendation quality and accelerates inference, validating its practical value in supporting longer user histories. In future work, we aim to explore adaptive token budget allocation to better accommodate diverse user histories and dynamic decoding states.
