In particular, numeric Document Identifiers are generally more efficient because they are shorter and generally fixed-length sequences over a small vocabulary, requiring fewer decoding steps during beam search. However, they tend to generalize poorly to unseen documents and must be learned during indexing-stage training. In this paper, we propose a numeric DocID design for generative retrieval that enables better adaptation to unseen documents without retraining. Experiments on three benchmarks show that our DocID design substantially improves unseen-document retrieval while maintaining comparable performance on seen documents. It achieves over 20 percentage points higher Hit@10 on unseen documents and up to 7$ $ lower inference latency than existing numeric DocID designs on NQ320K and TriviaQA, while performing only 3 percentage points below weight-update methods on MS MARCO without additional training. These results show that an appropriate DocID design can heavily reduce retraining costs when adapting generative retrievers to new documents. {image/pareto_latency_dyn40v2_hit10.pdf} Generative Retrieval is a retrieval paradigm in which an input query text is directly matched to a document identifier using a language model, typically a sequence-to-sequence model. Unlike conventional dense retrieval methods, which require a vector database or Approximate Nearest Neighbour index to store document embeddings and perform nearest-neighbor search at inference time, generative retrieval retrieves documents by generating their DocID directly . This design can offer inference and memory efficiency advantages . Two different DocID designs exist for generative retrieval systems: numeric DocIDs and textual DocIDs. Both have their own strengths: Numeric DocIDs offer computational advantages, as they reduce the complexity of beam-search decoding . In contrast, textual DocIDs can exploit the semantic priors encoded in pretrained language models and are able to give more interpretable document identifiers . One of the major challenges in current generative retrieval research is adapting to the retrieval of previously unseen documents. Generative retrieval models typically require an indexing stage during training, where the model learns to associate documents with their corresponding DocIDs. As a result, the model tends to be biased toward DocIDs seen during training. This lack of generalization is especially pronounced for numeric DocIDs , but also textual DocIDs lead to substantially lower effectiveness on unseen than on seen documents . While incremental training methods adapt numeric DocIDs to new documents , updating model parameters remains computationally expensive. Recent model-editing approaches update only a small subset of parameters while achieving performance comparable to full retraining . However, editing the model for newly added documents still requires computation with each update, making frequent updates costly. To address the poor adaptation of numeric DocIDs and the cost of updating model weights, we propose mputed it-Factorized dentifiers for enerative etrieval . CoBIGR represents each document with a sign-projection binary code of its document vector and factorizes the retrieval score over its identifier bits, which inherit the model's knowledge of the document rather than a memorized embedding learned from indexing training. Additionally, the proposed bit-factorized IDs are flat and their score is permutation-invariant. This property enables multi-tree beam search during inference, which substantially improves effectiveness on unseen documents, while scoring with one forward pass keeps decoding efficient . Our contributions are: We propose a bit-factorized numeric DocID: its bits derive meaning from the language model, so unseen DocIDs can still be retrievable. We study direct DocID adaptation to dynamic corpora without weight updates, addressing an underexplored gap in costly generative retrieval training. Our experiments show that our approach achieves comparable performance on seen documents to prior DocID designs while substantially improving performance on unseen documents, with 40--60 percentage points higher Hit@1 and Hit@100 than other numeric DocIDs. During training, the DocID serves as the output target for both document-to-DocID and query-to-DocID training. For text-based identifiers, titles , URLs , document substrings , and term sets are commonly used as identifiers. In contrast, numeric identifiers are often derived through using clustering methods such as k-means or through representation-based quantization methods such as Product Quantization , Residual Quantization and RQ-VAE . These IDs are semantically structured. Although the numeric identifiers produce compact beam trees and enable more efficient decoding, they often suffer from limited generalization. Hashing has been widely used for efficient retrieval by providing compact representations and approximate similarity measures based on Hamming distance . Recently, FLASH uses hash codes as the first step of retrieval: the generated code seeds a Hamming-radius search and is reconstructed into a continuous embedding for reranking. Existing work primarily focuses on retraining the retrieval model when new documents are arrived: DSI++ reduces catastrophic forgetting by optimizing for flatter loss landscapes and incorporating pseudo-queries in continual training. IncDSI perform a constraint optimization to adapt to dynamic corpus, this avoids the full re-training of the decoder. However, the method is restricted to atomic document identifiers. DOME further generalizes document insertion by optimizing edit vectors with soft-label guidance, enabling effective adaptation under structured document identifier schemes. Unlike prior retraining-based approaches, we study the zero-shot adaptation of newly arrived documents into structured numeric identifier spaces, which, to the best of our knowledge, has remained underexplored. Training combines two objectives: an step that learns to bind each document $d$ to its DocID $_d$, and a step that learns to map a query to the DocIDs of its relevant documents. In practice, the document collection $D$ changes over time. We therefore consider newly arrived documents after the model is trained. Both training stages operate on bits, but we perform inference token by token: each token represents an $m$-bit segment of $_d$ as an integer in $[0,2^m)$. Thus, each DocID has $T$ tokens and $k=Tm$ bits in total. This reduces the number of pruning decisions, as $m$ bits are used for each decision. Since no token score depends on previously decoded tokens, no autoregressive decoder is needed. In generative retrieval, a DocID is decoded as a sequence of tokens, each step producing a softmax over the possible token values, with a prefix tree restricting the output to valid DocIDs. Our add-on is to bit-factorize each token value into an $m$-bit pattern. Let the DocID of document $d$ be $c_1, ,c_T$, with $c_t \{0, ,2^m-1\}$. Beam search scores DocIDs token by token on the partial sum $ _{t' t} p_{t'} $ and prunes prefixes as it goes. One potential problem is that pruning may discard a relevant DocID early, even if its remaining bits match the query well. Because our tokens are independently scored through bit factorization, any bit permutation, followed by regrouping into tokens, generates a valid prefix tree. We therefore build $h$ trees using different bit permutations, run beam search on each tree, and rank the union of their candidates by log-probability. Since different bit orders prune based on different bits, using multiple trees can recover documents that a single tree would miss. We find that existing numeric DocID designs perform well on seen documents but degrade substantially on unseen documents, while our approach maintains comparable performance on seen documents and improves on unseen documents by a large margin over previous methods, and remains close to methods that update model weights. This suggests that our DocID design for incorporating new documents without retraining is promising, especially given the high computational cost of retraining generative retrievers. In future work, we plan to apply factorization to hierarchical DocIDs to investigate whether it can further improve recall at high ranks. *{Limitations} In our token design, no prefix is more informative than another, so early decoding prunes on limited information. Multi-tree beam search reduces this effect, but Hit@100 on seen documents remains slightly below some baselines. In future work, we plan to combine bit factorization with residual quantization such as RQ-VAE, whose coarse-to-fine codes may make early levels more informative and reduce pruning errors.
