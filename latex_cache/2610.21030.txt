However, additional reasoning may introduce over-generalized or semantically noisy abstractions that weaken retrieval specificity. Existing methods largely supervise reasoning through final retrieval outcomes, providing limited guidance over the refinement process itself. We propose AURA, a framework that directly supervises intermediate reasoning states according to how well they separate relevant documents from hard distractors. AURA further trains a lightweight utility estimator over prefix-conditioned embeddings to adapt reasoning depth at inference time. On the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT benchmark, AURA-4B achieves 34.7 average nDCG@10 while using 2.8 reasoning steps and 96 generated tokens per query. Under matched deployment conditions, adaptive stopping reduces latency from 4.6 to 0.76 seconds relative to full-trajectory reasoning. Results on MS MARCO further suggest that this specialization need not substantially degrade conventional in-domain retrieval. Recent dense retrieval systems increasingly incorporate reasoning through query rewriting, decomposition, reranking, and reasoning-conditioned embeddings~, motivated by the observation that semantic refinement can improve retrieval for complex queries. {images/utility_dual_final.pdf} However, existing reasoning-based retrieval systems generally treat additional reasoning as beneficial, applying it without distinguishing whether further refinement helps or hurts retrieval quality. In practice, retrieval-oriented reasoning often exhibits diminishing returns. While some refinements sharpen ---the separation between relevant documents and hard distractors---by identifying useful constraints or disambiguating entities, others introduce semantic drift or overly abstract reformulations that weaken retrieval discrimination~. As a result, longer reasoning trajectories do not always improve retrieval quality, and can even harm retrieval discrimination, increase inference latency, and introduce unnecessary computational cost. To understand how retrieval utility evolves during reasoning, we analyze retrieval specificity across intermediate reasoning steps on BRIGHT. As shown in Figure~, retrieval specificity improves on average during early reasoning steps, suggesting that additional refinement can help construct more discriminative retrieval representations. However, this improvement is highly heterogeneous: variability increases rapidly with reasoning depth, indicating that different queries benefit from substantially different amounts of reasoning. The fraction of queries that have already passed their optimal reasoning step increases and exceeds 50\ {images/concept.pdf} . While effective for evaluating end-to-end success, these coarse signals provide little guidance over the reasoning process. More importantly, current retrieval systems cannot predict whether additional reasoning will improve retrieval before observing retrieval outcomes, and thus can only allocate computation reactively: they expand reasoning trajectories regardless of whether further refinement is likely to help. In this work, we formulate retrieval-oriented reasoning as a utility-guided computation allocation problem. Rather than treating reasoning as an unconditional token expansion, we evaluate whether each intermediate refinement improves the model's ability to distinguish relevant documents from challenging distractors . Under this perspective, useful reasoning is determined not by trajectory length or textual sophistication, but by its measurable effect on retrieval discrimination. Building on this perspective, we propose .} , a framework for learning retrieval-oriented reasoning through utility supervision over intermediate reasoning states. During training, AURA uses relevance labels and retrieval candidates to estimate whether each refinement step improves retrieval discrimination, encouraging reasoning trajectories that sharpen retrieval specificity while avoiding semantically noisy refinements. Since retrieval specificity is unavailable at inference time, AURA additionally trains a lightweight utility estimator to predict the retrieval utility of each current reasoning state. Monitoring this estimate online allows AURA to stop when state utility ceases to improve, without exhaustively expanding the reasoning trajectory. We evaluate AURA on the 12 reasoning-intensive retrieval domains in BRIGHT~ and on MS MARCO~ as a conventional in-domain retrieval setting. AURA outperforms the compared reasoning-conditioned retrieval methods on BRIGHT. On MS MARCO, it remains comparable to a strong reasoning-trained retriever, suggesting no substantial in-domain degradation when extensive reasoning is unnecessary. Controlled analyses further show that the gains cannot be explained by denser intermediate contrastive supervision or trajectory filtering alone, and that retrieval specificity is strongly aligned with corpus-level nDCG@10. Our contributions are summarized as follows: We formulate reasoning-conditioned retrieval as an adaptive refinement process and show that reasoning utility is highly heterogeneous. We propose , a framework that supervises intermediate reasoning states according to their retrieval specificity, and trains a lightweight utility estimator to dynamically allocate reasoning during inference. We demonstrate improvements over the compared methods across 12 reasoning-intensive domains, together with a substantially better performance--cost trade-off under matched inference conditions. Modern dense retrieval commonly relies on bi-encoder architectures that map queries and documents into a shared embedding space, where retrieval is performed through vector similarity~. Early embedding approaches such as Sentence-BERT~ demonstrated the effectiveness of contrastive representation learning for semantic retrieval, followed by stronger instruction-tuned and large embedding models including INSTRUCTOR~, E5~, and recent LLM-based embedding systems~. Recent studies further explore using LLM hidden states directly as embeddings~ or leveraging in-context learning to improve representation quality~. Beyond supervised retrieval data, dense retrievers have also been trained using unsupervised contrastive objectives and synthetic supervision~. Despite strong performance, most embedding models remain fundamentally single-pass encoders that construct representations without explicit semantic refinement or intermediate reasoning. Recent work increasingly incorporates reasoning into retrieval systems through query rewriting, decomposition, iterative retrieval, and reasoning-augmented reranking~. For example, ReasonIR~ synthesizes reasoning trajectories and hard negatives to train retrieval models on reasoning-intensive benchmarks~. More recently, reasoning has been incorporated directly into embedding formation: multimodal methods such as RITE~, LREM~, and UME-R1~ generate reasoning before constructing retrieval representations, with further gains from inference-time scaling through sampling multiple reasoning trajectories~. However, reasoning is not uniformly beneficial for retrieval. Query expansion has long been known to suffer from query drift~, and recent work shows that LLM-based reasoning can hurt dense retrieval due to semantic drift~. Similar observations have emerged for chain-of-thought prompting more broadly~. Despite these findings, existing methods either supervise reasoning only through final retrieval outcomes or select among complete trajectories via best-of-$k$, providing no direct supervision over intermediate refinement states nor adaptive reasoning allocation at inference time. Two findings are notable. First, uniform-weight supervision already improves substantially over final-state-only , confirming that denser contrastive training is beneficial. However, AURA outperforms uniform weighting by a further 1.1 points, demonstrating that utility-aware differentiation among intermediate states provides supervision that cannot be achieved by simply increasing the number of contrastive training positions. Second, monotonic labels underperform uniform weighting , indicating that the assumption of monotonically increasing reasoning quality is actively harmful---consistent with the over-reasoning phenomenon motivating this work. Random labels degrade close to final-state-only, confirming that the pairwise ranking loss requires accurate ordering signal to function effectively. Retaining trajectories in which no intermediate state improves over the initial query changes nDCG@10 only marginally, from 34.7 to 34.6. Filtering therefore provides a small benefit by removing trajectories without a positive within-trajectory ranking signal, but does not explain AURA's overall improvement. In contrast, removing gradient isolation causes a substantial drop to 29.8. Retrieval gradients propagated through earlier prefixes therefore interfere with stable joint generation--retrieval optimization, supporting the gradient-isolation design. At inference time, ground-truth retrieval specificity is unavailable, which motivates utility estimation. The utility estimator $V_ $ achieves 34.7, 0.3 points below the oracle upper bound , where the oracle selects the reasoning state with the highest true $S $ using ground-truth relevance labels. Random state selection drops to 32.3. This narrow aggregate performance gap indicates that $V_ $ provides an effective approximation of retrieval utility without access to relevance labels. We compare AURA with an LLM-confidence rule based on the mean token entropy of the current reasoning step, tuning both the threshold and stopping direction on development data. Its strongest variant obtains 32.8 nDCG@10 at 3.2 average steps, compared with AURA's 34.7 at 2.8 steps. Thus, generation confidence alone does not reliably identify the reasoning state that is most discriminative for retrieval. Early stopping consistently outperforms using the full reasoning trajectory , validating that over-reasoning degrades retrieval quality. Among stopping strategies, patience $p{=}1$ performs best; greedy peak stopping is comparable , while increasing patience to $p{=}3$ allows too many low-utility steps through. Without early stopping, AURA generates 332 tokens per query, takes 4.6 seconds, and achieves 33.0 nDCG@10. The utility estimator itself adds approximately 1 millisecond per query. Because AURA estimates retrieval utility at intermediate reasoning states, it naturally enables inference-time scaling by allocating additional reasoning computation only when further refinement is predicted to improve retrieval quality. We evaluate two search strategies under increasing inference budgets. : sample $k$ complete reasoning trajectories independently and select the trajectory with the highest estimated utility. : generate multiple candidate refinements at each reasoning step and continue from the one with the highest predicted utility. Figure~ summarizes the performance-compute tradeoff on BRIGHT. Increasing search budget improves retrieval performance for both strategies, suggesting that additional reasoning can translate into stronger retrieval representations when guided by utility estimation. However, greedy expansion consistently achieves better compute efficiency than trajectory-level Best-of-$k$ search. Performance improves from 34.7 to 37.2 , compared to 36.7 under Best-of-$k$ on the same search scale. These results suggest that retrieval-oriented reasoning benefits more from early utility-guided decisions than from selecting among completed reasoning trajectories. We presented AURA, a utility-guided framework that supervises intermediate reasoning states and stops generation when predicted retrieval utility ceases to improve. AURA achieves the strongest average BRIGHT performance among the compared methods while substantially reducing reasoning cost, without an external inference-time reasoner. Controlled ablations and full-corpus analysis show that the gains arise from utility-aware supervision rather than denser supervision or trajectory filtering, and that retrieval specificity closely tracks nDCG@10. Results on MS MARCO suggest limited in-domain degradation, while utility-guided greedy expansion further improves the performance--compute trade-off. *{Limitations} Despite its efficiency gains, AURA remains slower than single-pass retrieval because reasoning is generated autoregressively. Its utility labels are constructed once from a warmed-up checkpoint and may become less accurate as the embedding space evolves during subsequent optimization.
