This data-constrained regime differs from the abundant-data setting assumed by standard scaling laws, making it unclear how the best attainable retrieval performance changes with model size and unique fine-tuning data. We study this question through the minimum achievable evaluation loss , defined as the lowest evaluation loss reached during training under fixed model-size and data-budget constraints. We first show that learning-rate choices strongly affect optimization trajectories but have little effect on the final attainable MAEL: after rescaling the optimization-step axis, learning curves collapse across learning rates, and CKA analysis shows that checkpoints reaching MAEL learn highly similar representations. We then fine-tune 35 BERT-family dense retrievers on MS MARCO subsets and find that MAEL is accurately described by a simple multiplicative power law over non-embedding parameters and unique samples, outperforming commonly used additive scaling formulations. We further derive a scaling law for the training step at which MAEL is reached and show that optimal weight decay follows an approximately invariant EMA timescale, yielding lower MAEL and stronger retrieval performance on both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks. Finally, combining loss and step scaling suggests that, in our observed regime, increasing pretrained model size is approximately 1.46 times as fine-tuning-compute-efficient as increasing unique in-domain data for reducing MAEL. { } {Corresponding author } Large language model scaling laws have shown that increasing model size and training data consistently leads to improved performance~. Similar observations have also been made in the information retrieval literature, where prior work demonstrates that scaling laws continue to hold for dense retrieval models~. Unlike large language models, which are typically pretrained on massive amounts of unlabeled text, dense retrieval models are generally obtained by fine-tuning pretrained language models on downstream retrieval tasks~. However, high-quality supervised retrieval datasets, such as those constructed through manual annotation or collected from user interaction logs~, are relatively scarce and are several orders of magnitude smaller than pretraining corpora. As a result, repeatedly reusing the same training data over multiple epochs has become standard practice in the fine-tuning paradigm~. Existing scaling laws, however, have largely been developed under the compute-optimal regime, which assumes access to abundant, unique training data. Such assumptions no longer hold in many practical settings, such as dense retrieval. Motivated by the increasing scarcity of high-quality data, recent studies have investigated data-constrained training and proposed scaling laws that characterize the evolution of model performance throughout optimization~. While these studies successfully model the training dynamics, practical deployment is often less concerned with the entire optimization trajectory than with the best performance achievable under a fixed model size and data budget. Consequently, despite recent progress in scaling under data constraints, a fundamental question remains unanswered: what is the best performance achievable with a fixed amount of unique training data and a fixed model size in information retrieval post-training? In practice, the performance observed at a given training stage depends not only on model size and data, but also on the optimization procedure, making it difficult to disentangle the effect of scaling from that of the training recipe. We find that, despite following substantially different optimization trajectories under different learning rates, models with the same model size and unique-data budget reach nearly identical minimum evaluation losses. Moreover, their learning curves can be aligned through a simple rescaling of the optimization-step axis, suggesting that the learning-rate choice primarily affects the rate at which a given level of performance is reached rather than the attainable performance itself. This motivates using the minimum achievable evaluation loss reached during training as a stable, recipe-invariant measure of attainable performance. We therefore study how this quantity scales with model size and unique data under a fixed fine-tuning compute budget. Building on this observation, we study 35 model-size and unique-data-budget configurations spanning a wide range of scales. We find that lower MAEL consistently corresponds to better downstream retrieval effectiveness and that MAEL is accurately described by a simple multiplicative power law. This scaling relationship enables us to quantify the marginal benefits of increasing model size and unique data, providing a principled framework for improving fine-tuning compute efficiency. We further investigate the effect of weight decay and find that appropriately chosen regularization can further reduce MAEL and improve retrieval performance; the optimal weight decay follows a scaling trend consistent with recipes previously observed in large-scale pretraining~. Our main contributions are as follows: We show that, under data-constrained post-training, learning-rate schedules substantially affect optimization trajectories but have little influence on the minimum achievable evaluation loss after convergence. We identify the minimum achievable evaluation loss as a useful characterization for fixed model size and unique training data, and demonstrate that it follows a simple multiplicative scaling law across 35 models. We derive a scaling law for the optimization step at which MAEL is reached and show that combining the loss and step scalings yields a clear compute-efficiency tradeoff between model size and unique data. Dense retrieval has become the dominant paradigm for neural information retrieval by representing queries and documents with dense embeddings learned by neural encoders. DPR~ first demonstrated that dual-encoder models trained on supervised query--document pairs substantially outperform traditional sparse retrieval methods such as BM25~. Subsequent work improved retrieval quality through more effective hard-negative mining~, stronger pretrained encoders~, and knowledge distillation~. Unlike language model pretraining, dense retrievers are typically fine-tuned from pretrained language encoders using supervised datasets containing only tens or hundreds of thousands of labeled query--document pairs. Consequently, models are commonly trained for dozens of epochs~, repeatedly revisiting the same samples. This repeated-data training regime differs fundamentally from the continually expanding data setting assumed in most existing scaling-law studies and motivates the analyses presented in this work. Neural scaling laws describe predictable empirical relationships between model performance and scaling factors such as model size, training data, and compute. Early work showed that generalization error often follows power-law trends as data and model scale increase~. Kaplan et al.~ established such laws for autoregressive language models, showing that language-modeling loss scales smoothly with parameter count, dataset size, and training compute. Hoffmann et al.~ later refined this view by studying compute-optimal training and showing that, under a fixed compute budget, model size and training tokens should be scaled in a more balanced way. These studies make scaling laws useful not only as descriptive models of performance, but also as practical tools for extrapolating large-scale behavior from smaller training runs. Beyond language-model pretraining, scaling-law analyses have been extended to other architectures~, modalities~, and downstream applications~. More recently, scaling-law analyses have also been extended to fine-tuning, where limited data and repeated exposure to the same samples introduce optimization dynamics that differ substantially from the pretraining regime. Some work~ modeled the diminishing information gained from repeated data passes during fine-tuning, while ~ derived scaling laws for different fine-tuning strategies, including full-parameter tuning, LoRA, and prompt tuning. Subsequent studies further investigated scaling behavior under domain shift and catastrophic forgetting~. Since dense retrieval is typically trained through supervised fine-tuning on pretrained language encoders, recent work has shown that dense retrieval also exhibits predictable scaling behavior with respect to model and data scales~. However, existing dense retrieval scaling laws assume that each training sample is observed only once and therefore do not model the repeated-sample regime that is ubiquitous in practical fine-tuning. Consequently, they cannot characterize the overfitting dynamics or predict the minimum achievable evaluation loss under realistic fine-tuning settings. In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to understanding the training dynamics of language models under data-constrained settings, leading to a series of scaling laws that characterize their learning behavior. Early studies introduced a small fraction of repeated samples into the training corpus and observed a double-descent phenomenon, showing that data repetition can degrade model performance~. Subsequent work investigated the learning dynamics when the entire dataset is repeatedly reused, demonstrating that repeated data can provide learning efficiency comparable to fresh data during the early stages of reuse~. Building on these findings, more recent studies have explored how to optimize pretraining under a fixed data budget with effectively unlimited compute~, as well as proposed improved scaling laws that accurately capture the U-shaped learning curves induced by overfitting on reused data~. However, existing studies primarily aim to model the entire learning trajectory, while paying relatively little attention to the scaling behavior of the minimum evaluation loss. In the observed regime, when a larger compatible pretrained checkpoint can be trained and served, allocating fine-tuning compute toward the larger model is more effective than spending the same budget on additional unique in-domain data. Once model size is constrained by hardware, checkpoint availability, or deployment latency, increasing the amount of unique in-domain data remains the next effective way to reduce MAEL. This work studies scaling laws for dense retrieval under data-constrained fine-tuning, where supervised retrieval data is limited and repeatedly reused. We introduce minimum achievable evaluation loss as a stable measure of the best performance attainable under fixed model-size and unique-data budgets, and show that MAEL follows a simple scaling law with respect to model size and data budget across 35 BERT-family dense retrievers fine-tuned on MS MARCO subsets. Beyond serving as a scaling target, MAEL strongly correlates with downstream retrieval effectiveness across standard retrieval metrics. We further demonstrate that MAEL convergence and weight-decay selection can be predicted from training dynamics, enabling more efficient fine-tuning under limited data. Our findings provide practical guidance for allocating fine-tuning resources: scaling pretrained model size is often more compute-efficient than collecting additional in-domain data in the studied regime, while increasing unique data remains an effective strategy when model capacity is constrained.
