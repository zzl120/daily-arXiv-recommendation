Today's retrieval tools have a blind spot: they return their top-$k$ passages even when the index contains nothing that answers the query, so a miss looks exactly like a hit . Fixing this blind spot is hard on either side of the tool call. On the agent side, training a model to abstain on irrelevant, missing or misleading evidence works , but it needs access to the weights. Generally, strongest agents are closed models served through an API, and the open models that can be trained are smaller and less capable. Therefore, for most deployments only prompt engineering is left. On the tool side, a production search stack chains a first-stage retriever and one or more rerankers, each tuned for ranking quality rather than for detecting its own misses; teaching it to report a miss means re-optimizing every stage together. Due to the aforementioned challenges, in practice both components are frozen. What a system builder does control is the contract between them: what the tool returns. We propose to put the control there, as a gate at the tool's outlet that makes one binary decision per call and leaves both the retrieval stack and the agent untouched. A question is , an , when no passage that could answer it is in the index. We change the tool contract so that the tool can : instead of passages it returns a , a short message saying that nothing reliable was found. We then ask what agents do with it: do they , search again, or ignore the refusal and answer anyway? Such a gate depends on two links. The first, , is how reliably a detector recognises a miss from what the retrieval stack returns. It depends on the retriever, the rankers and the detector, and can be summarised by the trigger's recall at a fixed false-refusal budget. The second, , is what the agent does once the tool refuses. The second link comes first: a perfect detector is worthless if the agent ignores the refusal, and how off-the-shelf agents respond to one has not been measured. We therefore study the trigger$ $agent link and leave detector design to future work. We control the refusal directly with an oracle and with noisy oracles of known recall, measure how each agent's behaviour changes as recall varies, and place realistic triggers on the resulting curve only to show where today's detectors land. Unlike score-based abstention in single-shot retrieval-augmented generation or training the agent to abstain , a tool-side refusal is a one-line contract change that applies even to closed models.
