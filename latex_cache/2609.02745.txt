We study pooled LLM evaluation, in which an LLM judges the union of documents retrieved by the current set of candidate systems, and the pool is then expanded incrementally as new systems are introduced by judging only the new documents they contribute. These judgments are reused to evaluate all systems on a common basis. As new embedding models are released , practitioners face a recurring decision: The gold standard for retrieval evaluation, pooled human relevance judgments, as in TREC~, is expensive, slow, and difficult to repeat as new candidate systems arrive. Evaluating $N$ systems independently with an LLM judge costs $O $ judgments , with substantial redundancy since different retrieval systems often surface overlapping document sets. We evaluate . We judge the of documents retrieved by all systems under test, then reuse these judgments to compute standard IR metrics for any system in the pool. As new systems are added to the set, pools grow based on only the new documents that those systems retrieve. This approach offers four advantages: Shared documents are judged only once. Each additional system added to the pool incurs only marginal cost for its uniquely retrieved documents. By definition, system-level P@$k$ and DCG@$k$ will always be invariant under pool expansion, and per-query pairwise orderings are preserved for recall@$k$, AP, and nDCG@$k$. Empirically, macro-averaged benchmark rankings remain highly stable as new systems are added. Unlike classical TREC pooling, where unjudged documents are treated as non-relevant and systems with novel retrieval behavior are penalised~, pooled LLM judging can assess every newly retrieved document contributed to the pool. Teams can begin benchmarking with as few as two or three systems and add new ones over time, without systematic penalties for novel retrieval behavior due to unjudged documents. We focus on how to structure LLM evaluation to minimize cost while characterizing which aspects of ranking stability follow from metric definitions and which must be established empirically. The reusability of pooled judgments is a classical topic in IR evaluation. and established that TREC-style pools produce stable system rankings even when new, unjudged systems are evaluated. analysed the conditions under which affects system comparison: when a new system retrieves documents absent from the original pool, those documents are unjudged and effectively treated as non-relevant, systematically disadvantaging novel retrieval strategies. Our LLM-based approach provides a structural safeguard against this concern: because LLM judging is inexpensive, we judge documents retrieved by any system, ensuring that no relevant document goes unassessed. Prior work has explored reducing annotation cost through active selection~, stratified sampling~, and assessment budgeting. Our approach is complementary: rather than selecting documents to judge, we judge all pooled documents but reuse judgments across systems, exploiting the natural overlap in retrieval results. We note the following stability properties, which follow directly from metric definitions under the assumption that previously assigned labels are not revised and each system's ranked list remains fixed. P@$k$ and DCG@$k$ depend only on each system's own top-$k$ documents and their relevance labels, so they are exactly invariant under pool expansion. Recall@$k$, AP, and nDCG@$k$ each have a system-specific numerator divided by a query-specific denominator shared across all systems and because all systems' scores for a given query are scaled by the same factor, pairwise orderings are preserved within each query. When scores are macro-averaged across queries, the per-query scaling factors may differ, so benchmark-level invariance is not guaranteed in general. We evaluate this empirically in Section~. Classical TREC pooling can penalize systems that retrieve documents absent from the pool, because unjudged documents are effectively treated as non-relevant~. To quantify this risk, we ran a leave-one-family-out analysis: for each of six model families , we removed that family's unique contributions and treated them as non-relevant. Pool bias is dataset-dependent. FiQA and TREC-COVID were most affected , FinRAGBench-V showed limited impact , and NQ was effectively unaffected , consistent with higher cross-family overlap. By judging newly contributed documents whenever systems are added, pooled LLM evaluation avoids this classical failure mode. In our experiments, this removed all 19 pairwise ranking errors introduced by classical pooling across the three affected datasets. The production corpus consists of $ $300k news article pages indexed in OpenSearch, representing one month of news data that is licensed for use in internal generative AI applications. We constructed a benchmark of 303 queries from four sources: LLM-generated questions based on semantic article clusters , LLM-generated questions based on metadata-clustered articles by company, industry, and topic , templatic ``latest news'' questions for frequently mentioned entities , and real user queries from production API traffic, downsampled to remove near-duplicates . Queries range from factual lookups to complex multi-hop multi-source questions . We further perturb these queries by converting them to keyword searches and to ``framed" queries, which specify output structure or formatting e.g. $ $ . Using the pooled evaluation methodology, we benchmarked 62 retrieval configurations comprising 5 embedding models ; 3 retrieval modes ; 2--3 dimensionalities per model ; 3 query formulations per system ; and variants of the top-performing system with and without query expansion. The final pool contains 766,350 unique $ $ judgments . Without pooling, evaluating the same set of experiments independently would have required 3,765,643 judgments, a 79.6\ The pool was built incrementally over four weeks as new model candidates and query variants were added; at no point did previously computed judgments need to be re-run. Higher dimensions improve dense retrieval , but the gap narrows under hybrid retrieval , suggesting BM25 fusion compensates for reduced embedding capacity. Hybrid retrieval is also more robust to query formulation: keyword-reduced queries hurt dense models more , and instruction-framed queries degrade all systems by diluting the retrieval signal. Query expansion does not help on well-formed queries but acts as a robustness mechanism against verbose formulations, nearly eliminating the framing-induced recall@100 penalty . For production teams maintaining retrieval systems, pooled LLM evaluation enables a reusable incremental benchmarking workflow: ~establish an initial pool with 3--5 candidate systems; ~when a new model becomes available, retrieve with it and judge only the documents it contributes; ~compare against all previously evaluated systems, with exact score reuse for previously judged documents and strong empirical ranking stability as the pool grows. The approach is particularly suited to domains where: gold-standard relevance judgments do not exist or are prohibitively expensive to create; new embedding models are regularly evaluated ; maintaining a stable historical benchmark is important for governance.
