 Cross-domain recommendation aims to improve recommendation quality by leveraging user behaviors from multiple related domains~. In many real-world platforms, users interact with heterogeneous services, such as movies, books, music, news, and e-commerce items, and their behaviors in different domains may reveal complementary aspects of their preferences. By transferring useful knowledge across domains, CDR has been widely studied as an effective solution to data sparsity and cold-start problems~. This problem is particularly important in sequential recommendation, where models need to capture users' evolving interests from historical interaction sequences~ while exploiting transferable preference signals from other domains. Existing CDR methods commonly transfer knowledge through shared or parallel architectures~, global alignment objectives such as contrastive or adversarial learning~, domain-general or invariant representation learning~, and cooperative transfer-control strategies~. Although these methods have achieved promising performance, many still rely on predefined representation mappings, global alignment objectives, or coarse-grained transfer strategies, offering limited flexibility in adapting the transfer process to individual users. In practice, users exhibit different degrees of preference consistency across domains and may require different amounts of transferred knowledge. Moreover, cross-domain preference adaptation may involve gradual adjustments among related semantic interests rather than simple representation fusion. As illustrated in Fig.~, some users have well-matched interests, some are only partially matched, and others show weak cross-domain correspondence. Applying similar transfer strengths or inflexible adaptation mechanisms to these users may introduce irrelevant information from the paired domain, resulting in negative transfer. {Fig/moti.pdf} { {Fig/SC_interest_overlap.pdf} } { {Fig/alignment_three_methods_sport_cloth.pdf} } -inferred user interests exhibit diverse cross-domain similarity, while BGAFlow exhibits greater variation in alignment strength across user groups than InfoNCE and adversarial alignment.} To further examine these differences in real cross-domain data, we conduct an empirical analysis on the Sport-Cloth dataset. Specifically, we first use LLM-inferred user interests to measure cross-domain preference similarity. As shown in Fig.~, users exhibit substantial variations in preference consistency, indicating different degrees of transferable interests. We then compare the alignment strengths of our proposed BGAFlow with those of InfoNCE and adversarial alignment. Fig.~ shows that the two conventional objectives yield relatively stable alignment strengths across similarity groups, whereas BGAFlow produces stronger alignment for users with higher interest similarity. This observation highlights the need for personalized transfer control rather than uniform alignment. Beyond determining the transfer extent, effective cross-domain adaptation also requires a mechanism that can progressively refine semantic representations according to their intermediate states. These considerations raise two key challenges: Cross-domain transfer requires a mechanism that progressively adapts semantic representations toward relevant preferences in the paired domain, rather than relying solely on one-step fusion or direct mapping. More importantly, the transfer process should respond to intermediate semantic states and adjust subsequent directions accordingly, while maintaining a coherent and smooth evolution trajectory. To control the transfer extent, the model needs a semantically meaningful signal that reflects how strongly a user's interests are related across two domains. Such a signal should capture user-level transferable preference overlap and determine the appropriate alignment strength, while avoiding unreliable direct comparisons between heterogeneous domain-specific representations. To address these challenges, we propose B\'ezier-Guided Adaptive Flow , which combines state-adaptive semantic refinement with personalized transfer control. BGAFlow first performs unified dual-domain pretraining to learn comparable semantic representations while preserving domain-specific collaborative information. It then estimates cross-domain preference overlap through latent interest prototypes to determine a personalized transfer horizon. For semantic adaptation, BGAFlow constructs a conditional B\'ezier curve as a geometric reference and learns a residual flow that progressively refines the trajectory based on intermediate preference states. Dynamic prototype feedback enables the flow to adjust its correction directions during transfer, while task-guided flow matching provides recommendation-aware supervision. Furthermore, smoothstep-based integration and trajectory regularization encourage gradual and stable representation evolution. Together, these designs enable BGAFlow to adapt both the extent and the process of cross-domain knowledge transfer to individual user preferences. Our contributions are summarized as follows: We propose BGAFlow, a personalized framework for cross-domain sequential recommendation that combines user-specific transfer control with state-adaptive semantic evolution. We develop a B\'ezier-guided residual flow that progressively refines a structured geometric reference using dynamic preference feedback and task-guided velocity supervision. Smoothstep-based integration and trajectory regularization further promote stable semantic adaptation. We conduct extensive experiments on real-world cross-domain recommendation datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness of BGAFlow against state-of-the-art baselines and validating its key components through comprehensive analyses. Cross-domain recommendation aims to alleviate data sparsity by transferring complementary preference knowledge across domains. Early studies mainly establish cross-domain connections through shared parameters or parallel network architectures. For example, CoNet transfers hidden representations between domain-specific networks, while $ $-Net exchanges sequential preference information through parallel information-sharing modules~. DA-GCN further employs domain-aware graph propagation to capture cross-domain relationships under shared accounts~. These approaches facilitate knowledge sharing between domains, but their transfer processes are largely determined by predefined architectural connections. Another line of research focuses on learning transferable representations through cross-domain alignment. C$^2$DSR and Tri-CDR exploit sequential correlations through contrastive learning and multi-sequence modeling~. Other studies employ contrastive or adversarial objectives to encourage representation consistency across domains~. Meanwhile, RecGURU, DIIT, and ABXI seek to extract domain-general or invariant preference information to improve knowledge transfer~. Recognizing that cross-domain signals are not universally beneficial, recent studies have also explored cooperative learning and transferability estimation to mitigate negative transfer~. Despite these advances, user-specific transfer strength and intermediate semantic adaptation are not always explicitly modeled together. In contrast, BGAFlow combines personalized transfer control with a learned semantic evolution process, allowing intermediate representations to be progressively refined according to their current preference states. Large language models have recently attracted considerable attention in recommendation due to their semantic understanding and generalization capabilities~. Existing studies explore their potential through task adaptation and semantic enhancement. TALLRec introduces an efficient tuning framework to align language models with recommendation tasks~. LLMRec leverages LLM-generated information to augment user--item interaction graphs, while LLM-ESR integrates LLM-derived semantic embeddings with collaborative signals to improve long-tailed sequential recommendation~. These methods demonstrate that language-based knowledge can complement behavioral signals and enrich user preference representations. More recently, LLMs have been incorporated into cross-domain sequential recommendation to bridge semantic differences between domains. LeCDSR utilizes LLM-generated semantic embeddings and cross-domain user profiles to facilitate preference transfer~. LLM4CDSR combines unified item representations with hierarchical user profiling to model cross-domain preferences, while LLMCDSR investigates LLM-enhanced knowledge transfer involving overlapping and non-overlapping users~. X-Cross further explores dynamic knowledge integration among domain-specific language models through low-rank adaptation~. These studies highlight the value of semantic knowledge and language-based preference modeling for cross-domain recommendation. Different from directly adapting language models or integrating their internal representations, BGAFlow employs LLM-inferred profiles to establish a shared semantic foundation and guide personalized transfer. Flow matching has emerged as an effective framework for learning continuous transformations between probability distributions. It trains time-dependent vector fields along prescribed probability paths without requiring numerical simulation during conditional velocity supervision~. Related formulations, such as rectified flow, further investigate transport trajectories for efficient generation and data transfer~. By learning the evolution of intermediate states through a velocity field, flow-based approaches provide a flexible alternative to direct representation mapping. Recently, flow matching has been introduced into recommender systems for modeling user preferences and generating recommendation representations. FlowCF incorporates behavior-guided priors and discrete flow modeling into collaborative filtering~. FMRec employs a straight flow trajectory for sequential recommendation, while FlowRec models preference trajectories using personalized behavior-based priors~. More recent studies investigate additional properties of the learned dynamics. PDFlow introduces a residual vector field to mitigate popularity bias during sequential recommendation, whereas ConFM employs contrastive supervision to preserve user-specific distinctions in flow predictions~.
