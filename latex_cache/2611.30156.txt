 A common paradigm in cross-domain recommendation transfers knowledge through users shared by source and target domains. Although this overlap bridge is effective in improving accuracy, its effect on diversity remains unclear. We identify two bottlenecks: the bridge can be too narrow when overlapping users cover limited target-domain tastes, and too loud when shared signals dominate the sparse personal evidence of non-overlapping users. To address both, we propose DivCDR, a model-agnostic Push--Pull framework. Pull constructs confidence-gated virtual positives for overlapping users and optimizes their exposure-entropy gain, broadening the preferences carried by the bridge. Push uses overlap-density-guided negative sampling to move non-overlapping users away from overlap-dense regions. Experiments on three datasets and five representative CDR backbones show that DivCDR improves recommendation diversity while preserving or improving accuracy in most settings. The implementation is publicly available at . Cross-domain recommendation improves recommendation quality in a target domain by leveraging auxiliary knowledge from one or more source domains~. A widely adopted strategy relies on overlapping users, users with interactions in both the source and target domains, as bridges for knowledge transfer~. By transferring source-domain knowledge, CDR has achieved substantial improvements in recommendation accuracy~. As the field matures, however, an important question arises~: Intuitively, incorporating auxiliary knowledge from another domain should broaden user exposure and thereby mitigate filter bubbles and echo chambers. Existing works~, however, indicate a counterintuitive pattern: CDR often yields limited diversity gains and can even make recommendations more homogeneous. The mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain insufficiently understood. To address this gap, we investigate the following question: {} Through theoretical analysis and empirical studies , we characterize this phenomenon through two bottlenecks associated with the knowledge-transfer ``bridge'': The first bottleneck concerns {what can be transferred}. Because overlapping users provide the identity-linked pathways between domains, the resulting transfer bridge can carry only the range of preferences represented by those users. The second bottleneck concerns {what happens after transfer}. A non-overlapping user has no source-domain history. If that user also has few interactions in the target domain, the transferred bridge signal can overwhelm the limited evidence of their individual interests. Consequently, the user's learned representation may remain near the representations of overlapping users, making recommendations across non-overlapping users more homogeneous. These bottlenecks are distinct but related. The former is a menu problem: does the bridge carry a sufficiently broad range of preferences? The latter is a weight problem: does the transferred signal dominate the personal evidence of non-overlapping users? To address these bottlenecks, we propose , a model-agnostic Push--Pull framework. DivCDR builds on the complementary geometric effects of positive and negative samples in recommendation learning: positive samples attract a user representation, whereas negative samples repel it~. Following this principle, DivCDR introduces two targeted interventions. For , broadens the range of preferences carried by the bridge. It perturbs an observed positive item to construct a user-conditioned virtual positive and directly optimizes a differentiable diversity-gain objective. For , weakens the dominance of the bridge. It estimates cocoon strength using an overlap-density surrogate over overlapping-user representations and samples a negative item whose induced repulsive update is aligned with the local escape direction. The resulting repulsive update is designed to move the user representation toward a lower-density, and hence less homogenized, region. Our main contributions are as follows: We provide mathematical analysis and experiments for two overlap-conditioned diversity failure modes: concentrated exposure among overlapping users and overlap-dominated representations for non-overlapping users. We introduce DivCDR, a model-agnostic framework whose Pull and Push components directly address the two bottlenecks without modifying the underlying CDR architecture. Pull broadens the transfer menu through user-conditioned virtual positives, whereas Push weakens information cocoons through overlap-density-guided negative sampling. Across three real-world datasets and five representative CDR backbones, DivCDR improves recommendation diversity while preserving or improving accuracy in most cases. The goal of cross-domain recommendation is to enhance recommendations in a target domain by transferring information from a source domain. CMF~ was among the earliest approaches to jointly factorize multiple rating matrices for knowledge transfer. PPCDR~ adopted a federated graph learning framework to capture user preferences while preserving privacy. GDCCDR~ achieved personalized transfer by integrating meta-networks with contrastive learning. PCDR~ utilized users' interactions on local clients and leveraged natural language to bridge domains. MACDR~ explored unsupervised methods for transferring non-overlapping knowledge. CD-CDR~ employed a shared conditional diffusion model to learn unified item distributions, thereby facilitating cross-domain knowledge transfer. MF-GSLAE~ employed a bootstrapping-based factor selection module to identify preferences unique to each domain. Lasso~ presented a cross-domain training paradigm that fine-tuned an LLM-based simulator. Cross-domain recommendation nevertheless relies on linking user behavior across domains, which may raise privacy and consent concerns when such linkage is not explicitly authorized. Diversity optimization can also change the balance between relevance and exposure; the reported offline gains should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of improved user satisfaction. Practical use should provide transparent data controls and monitor recommendation utility separately for overlapping and non-overlapping users. This work investigated how user overlap shapes diversity in cross-domain recommendation. We identified two complementary diversity risks: concentrated exposure among overlapping users and excessive dominance of overlap-induced signals for non-overlapping users with sparse target-domain evidence. Based on this diagnosis, we proposed DivCDR, a model-agnostic Push--Pull framework. Pull constructs confidence-gated virtual positives to diversify overlap-supported exposure, while Push employs directional negative sampling to help non-overlapping users escape overlap-dense regions. Experiments across three datasets and five representative CDR backbones demonstrate consistent diversity improvements while largely preserving, and often improving, accuracy.
