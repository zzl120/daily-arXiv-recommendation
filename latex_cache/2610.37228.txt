Inspired by these advances, Recommender Systems Engineer Agents have emerged as a promising direction for automating recommender system development~. Specifically, RSE-Agents aim to use LLM-based agents to autonomously improve recommendation algorithms by proposing new ideas, implementing them in code, and validating them through experiments. By taking over the role of human recommender systems engineers, RSE-Agents have the potential to significantly accelerate recommendation algorithm development while reducing engineering cost. However, research on RSE-Agents is still in its early stages. As a result, they provide neither a general framework for quantitatively evaluating and comparing different RSE-Agents, nor a systematic understanding of how such agents actually achieve performance gains. Consequently, two fundamental questions remain open: how should we evaluate RSE-Agents in a unified and realistic manner, and what are the capability boundaries and key bottlenecks of current LLM-based agents on this task? To address this, AutoRSE incorporates a trajectory-level analysis pipeline that segments continuous agent trajectories, which weave together a sequence of code improvements and exploratory attempts, into individual experimental iterations , identifies their underlying optimization strategies, and evaluates the algorithmic novelty of the final submitted algorithms. We then employ an LLM judge to identify the optimization strategy of each segmented experiment. AutoRec~ automates model search hyperparameter tuning for recommendation tasks. AutoFIS~ automatically selects informative feature interactions in factorization models, while AutoCTR~ searches for interaction architectures using evolutionary optimization. These approaches reduce manual effort by optimizing model configurations or components within predefined search spaces, but leave research hypothesis formulation and the broader experimental workflow largely to human engineers. With advances in LLM-based agents, recent work has extended recommendation automation to iterative research workflows. for example, AgentX coordinates specialized agents to generate improvement proposals, implement code changes, evaluate online experiments, and accumulate experimental knowledge. Self-Evolving Recommendation System~ combines an offline agent for hypothesis generation and proxy-metric optimization with an online agent that validates candidates against delayed business metrics. Its optimization targets include model architectures, optimizers, and reward functions. These pioneering systems demonstrate the practical feasibility of agent-driven recommendation iteration within their respective proprietary industrial pipelines. However, their system-specific evaluations leave open how different RSE-Agents compare under a shared protocol and which improvement strategies account for their performance gains across tasks. Our work addresses these questions through AutoRSE, a unified benchmark spanning six recommendation tasks with standardized execution environments, resource budgets, and hidden-test evaluation. Beyond final performance, we analyze complete agent trajectories to characterize their proposed changes and relate improvement strategies and research effort to experimental outcomes. This analysis reveals that current agents predominantly rely on model ensemble and parameter tuning, while changes to architectures and objectives function are comparatively rare. Motivated by this finding, we introduce a idea-injection experiment and selection module to support the exploration of substantive algorithmic improvements before costly implementation and training. LLM-based agents have increasingly been applied to software engineering and machine learning engineering . SWE-bench~ evaluates models on real-world GitHub issues, while SWE-agent~ enables autonomous repository navigation, code editing, and test execution through a dedicated agent--computer interface. Moving beyond software maintenance, MLAgentBench~ evaluates agents on iterative machine learning experimentation, and MLE-bench~ measures their ability to solve competition-derived tasks involving data preparation, model training, and performance optimization. Recent work further extends these capabilities toward autonomous scientific research. The AI Scientist~ connects idea generation, experiment execution, and manuscript writing, while The AI Scientist-v2~ uses agentic tree search to organize and refine experiments. Agent Laboratory~ coordinates literature review, experimentation, and report writing around human-proposed research ideas. AI-Researcher~ integrates literature analysis, algorithm implementation, and experimental validation, and introduces Scientist-Bench for guided and open-ended research tasks. Within this broader research agenda, recommender systems engineering provides a domain-specific setting for evaluating agents' ability to improve algorithms. Our RSE setting requires sustained optimization of faithfully reproduced, strong recommendation baselines under a fixed computational budget. Agents must determine what to change, formulate useful hypotheses, and evaluate their implementations through experimentation. Costly training and noisy validation feedback make unsuccessful directions expensive and require agents to coordinate hypothesis generation, implementation, and evaluation over long horizons. This setting captures practical challenges of recommendation research and allows us to examine whether agents can produce substantive algorithmic advances beyond routine tuning and engineering heuristics. Beyond final performance, our trajectory-level analysis examines the modifications agents implement, providing a basis for understanding their research strategies and the limitations of their algorithmic exploration. In this work, we introduce AutoRSE, a unified framework for evaluating RSE-Agents. Using this framework, we conduct a large-scale evaluation across diverse LLM-Agents, and recommendation tasks. Our experiments show that LLM agents can autonomously improve recommendation performance. And further trajectory analysis reveals that agents primarily explore model ensembling and parameter tuning, while changes to architectures and learning objectives remain uncommon. These findings highlight a lack of substantive algorithmic innovation in the solutions developed by current RSE-Agents. Motivated by the limited exploration of algorithmic ideas, we further conduct idea-injection experiments. Our idea-injection experiments further show that ideas from frontier research and human experts yield larger performance gains than agents' self-generated ideas, highlighting idea generation as a key bottleneck and motivating stronger algorithmic ideation capabilities in future RSE-Agents. In the future, we will continuously expand AutoRSE with more diverse recommendation tasks, larger-scale evaluation protocols, and a growing, community-driven leaderboard to foster open and reproducible assessment of RSE-Agents. Additionally, we aim to investigate advanced reasoning and reflection mechanisms to en- rich the creativity and quality of agents’ self-generated hypotheses, bridging the gap between automated software engineering and substantive algorithmic breakthroughs. Specifically, the process consists of three parts: first, we locate the anchor of each action according to its tool call position in the trajectory; second, we associate any code edits or file modifications that occurred between the end of the previous action and the start of the current tool call as modification evidence for the current action; and finally, we collect the return results within the tool call and its subsequent interaction window , extracting unvalidated metric candidates from them to fully restore the context of each trial. {figs/appendix1.pdf} We trace supplied guidance to concrete implementations and validation feedback. Training initially uses four negatives per example, whereas evaluation uses 99. The agent expands training candidates with positive items from other examples in the minibatch, retains cross-entropy, and masks target duplicates and observed-history negatives. With minibatches of 128 and a target of 128 negatives, the implementation fills 124 additional candidate slots using in-batch items. Full minibatches therefore require no additional popularity sampling, although that fallback is implemented. Models trained with this augmentation enter the final ensemble.
