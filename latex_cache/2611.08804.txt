A is a short, fixed-length sequence of codewords , and a can emit it in two ways. decoding predicts one codeword per step: the prefill encodes the request, and each successive codeword is drawn from a distribution conditioned on the codewords already chosen, so a length-three costs one prefill and two further backbone passes. instead predicts all three codeword distributions from the request prefix alone, in a single prefill, with no codeword attending to another; the catalog index constrains these independent per-position distributions to valid combinations, and a beam walk over it assembles the candidate . MTP trades the exact factorization of the joint distribution for parallelism---dropping the sequential dependence between codewords is precisely what lets it decode in one pass instead of three. The two modes expose a quality--latency trade-off . On its own, MTP scores below AR on both top-10 search metrics, and widening its beam from 100 to 250 barely moves either, so more beam search does not close the gap. The recommendation data teaches to predict what a buyer will interact with next, given their history. It has two parts: interaction sequences and discovery streams. A buyer's clicks, carts, favorites, checkouts and purchases in time order. Each event is a time-since-previous token, an action token and the product's . Raw histories are dominated by old clicks that no longer reflect what the buyer wants, so we compress them: we drop clicks older than 30 days and carts, favorites and checkouts older than 90 days, and keep purchases at any age. We interpret the two metrics together; the observed decline does not establish whether these alternatives would lead to more or fewer purchases if shown. Search and recommendation connect buyers with relevant products across Shopify’s large and diverse catalog across millions of merchants on their web storefront, Shopify-built Shop App, and Shopify's Catalog API designed for conversational commerce and AI agents. Both require understanding products and their relevance to a particular buyer at the scale of billions of products with very low latency. We present , a generative model for search and recommendation. It represents products as semantic IDs , short sequences of content-derived tokens, and learns to generate relevant identifiers from the context available for each task. This gives search and recommendation a shared formulation and allows us to investigate unifying them within one model. This report describes from semantic-ID tokenization and training through production integration. We study the choices that determine retrieval quality and serving cost, and explore distillation from task experts into a unified student. These experiences operate over a multi-merchant catalog, with product eligibility depending on the request context, such as the buyer's market. Each task retrieves candidate products for downstream ranking . We distinguish three tasks according to the context available at retrieval time. Given a buyer's interaction history $h$, retrieve products they are likely to find useful or purchase. The history contains actions such as clicks, carts, favorites and purchases, together with the products involved and their timing. There is no explicit query expressing the buyer's current need, so the model must infer it from this context. The task is to identify products the buyer is likely to engage with next. Given a textual query $q$, retrieve products relevant to the expressed intent without conditioning on buyer-specific preferences. Relevance requires interpreting the requested product type, attributes and constraints even when the buyer and merchant describe the same product differently. Given a query $q$ and a buyer profile $b$, retrieve products that satisfy the query while accounting for the buyer's preferences. The profile summarizes information about the buyer derived from their past behavior. The query specifies the immediate need; the profile helps distinguish among products that could meet it. We represent each product by a short semantic ID obtained by quantizing its content embedding . Given the task-specific context, learns to generate target through next-token prediction; we also study predicting their tokens in parallel . At inference, constrained decoding produces candidate , which are resolved to eligible products before downstream ranking Industrial recommendation systems commonly separate candidate retrieval from ranking . A two-tower retriever encodes the buyer and each product separately and scores their compatibility with an inner product. This supports efficient nearest-neighbour retrieval, but compresses the buyer's history into a fixed vector before scoring candidates. Multi-interest methods use several buyer vectors, each still computed independently of the candidate. Sequential generative recommenders learn to predict the next item from the interaction sequence . Representing products as atomic IDs gives each product its own learned embedding, with no content-based relationship between identifiers. Semantic-ID generation instead shares tokens across related products. In , the history remains part of the prompt throughout generation, and a pretrained language backbone provides an initial representation of product language. We evaluate the resulting generator both on its own candidates and on the purchases it adds to the combined candidate pool . Lexical retrieval matches query terms to product text through an inverted index, using a scoring function such as BM25 . It is useful for exact terms such as brands and model numbers, but encounters a vocabulary mismatch when buyers and merchants use different words for the same thing . Dense semantic retrieval addresses this mismatch by encoding queries and products into a shared vector space and searching an approximate nearest-neighbour index . Like two-tower recommendation, it scores separately computed representations. Generative retrieval replaces the index lookup with identifier generation . In , the query and optional buyer profile condition the prediction of each token. The model learns which identifiers fit the context, while a catalog index constrains generation to identifiers that resolve to eligible products . We jointly pre-train on catalog and behavioral data, giving search and recommendation a shared representation and training objective. We show that reinforcement learning further improves judged search relevance. We evaluate both as a standalone retriever and as a component of Shopify's production search and recommendation systems.
