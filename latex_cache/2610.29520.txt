These results characterize when LLM inference adds predictive value beyond distance-weighted voting in retrieval-based tabular ICL. {figure/draw/motivation.pdf} Tabular classification is a widely studied machine learning task with applications in healthcare , commerce , and energy . The goal is to predict class labels from instances described by numerical and categorical features. Traditional methods include nearest-neighbor classifiers, which predict labels from similar training instances , and tree-based ensembles, which have demonstrated strong performance on heterogeneous tabular data . Recent research has explored alternative approaches that leverage pretrained models for tabular prediction. Specialized tabular foundation models, including TabPFN, learn transferrable predictive patterns through pretraining . Another line of work adapts language models to tabular data through supervised or instruction tuning . More recently, researchers have explored applying frozen general-purpose large language models through few-shot in-context learning . In this setting, labeled tabular instances are serialized as demonstrations, often selected according to their similarity to a test query, enabling prediction without task-specific parameter updates. Despite these advances, it remains unclear how much predictive value LLM inference adds beyond direct voting on the same retrieved examples. In retrieval-based tabular ICL, performance differences may arise from either the selection of demonstrations or the predictions made from them, making these contributions difficult to distinguish when different retrieved examples are used. Moreover, expanding the candidate pool changes the available retrieval evidence, but whether LLM inference benefits more from this change than direct voting remains unclear. Their relative performance may also depend on two characteristics of the retrieved neighborhood: label consensus, the fraction of neighbors sharing the most frequent label, and feature proximity, measured by the mean feature-space distance from the test query to those neighbors, as illustrated in Figure~. A controlled comparison on identical retrieved neighbors is therefore needed to assess when LLMs can provide additional predictive value. In this work, we systematically compare four general-purpose LLMs with distance-weighted nearest-neighbor voting across 19 tabular classification datasets using a shared-neighbor evaluation protocol. At each candidate-pool size, both predictors use the same eight retrieved examples: LLMs predict from their serialized features and labels, while Distance-KNN predicts from their labels and retrieval distances. By expanding the candidate pool while keeping the number of retrieved examples fixed, we examine their performance and how LLM gains vary with label consensus and feature proximity. Our results show that Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy across all evaluated candidate pools, while LLMs exhibit selective gains under low label consensus. Feature proximity further distinguishes these gains, with improvements occurring in selected close and intermediate neighborhoods but disappearing in the most distant ones. In summary, our contributions are as follows: We present a systematic shared-neighbor comparison of four frozen LLMs and Distance-KNN across 19 datasets, enabling a direct assessment of the contribution of LLM inference over distance-weighted voting. We find that Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy across all evaluated candidate pools, while LLMs exhibit selective gains in low-consensus neighborhoods that vary with feature proximity. We further examine how the number of demonstrations, prompt formulation, retrieval representation, and label-balanced demonstration affect the comparison, finding that the advantage of Distance-KNN largely persists across these experiments. Tabular prediction has traditionally relied on distance-based methods such as k-nearest neighbors and tree-based ensembles, including Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost . To circumvent task-specific training while achieving strong generalization across unseen datasets, recent work has introduced tabular foundation models. For instance, TabPFN is pretrained on synthetic tabular tasks to make predictions directly from labeled examples . Extending this paradigm to real-world data, TabDPT introduces a dedicated tabular architecture for in-context prediction, rather than adapting general-purpose LLMs . A common paradigm relies on additional training to adapt language models for tabular prediction. Existing methods fine-tune LLMs on serialized rows for few-shot classification , train on synthetic table tasks , or leverage diverse tabular datasets for cross-dataset transfer , while others explore retrieval-guided tuning and structure-aware parameter adaptation . In contrast, our study investigates tabular prediction using frozen, general-purpose LLMs without additional task-specific training, leveraging Distance-KNN to provide a controlled comparison on identical retrieved demonstrations. {figure/draw/workflow.pdf} In-context learning enables LLMs to adapt to new tasks using a few labeled demonstrations without fine-tuning . Subsequent studies have examined how models exploit these demonstrations, analyzing the effects of input distributions , label mappings , and prompt formatting . Demonstration selection has also been widely explored through semantic similarity , learned retrieval , and influence estimation . In tabular classification, propose a training-free retrieval-based approach that prompts LLMs with relevant labeled examples, while InsightTab distills task-specific insights and combines them with selected demonstrations for classification . However, these studies primarily investigate how retrieval strategies or additional information can improve LLM predictions. In contrast, our work compares frozen LLMs with Distance-KNN using identical retrieved neighbors, revealing when LLM inference provides additional predictive value and how these gains depend on neighborhood structure of demonstrations. For each test query, a shared retriever extracts the $k$ nearest labeled training instances based on feature-space distance. Distance-KNN predicts from their labels and metric distances, whereas the LLM receives their serialized features and labels followed by the query features. By scaling the candidate-pool size under a fixed demonstration budget , we compare their predictive behaviors on identical retrieved evidence . We evaluate four open-weighted LLMs: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B chat models , alongside instruction-tuned Gemma3-12B and Gemma3-27B . All generative LLMs are deployed locally via vLLM without quantization. Qwen3-Embedding-4B is employed for text embedding retrieval in . Throughout our experiments, Distance-KNN serves as the reference predictor, using the exact same retrieved neighbors as the LLMs. Full checkpoint identifiers and implementation details are provided in Appendix~. We experiment on 19 public tabular classification datasets from UCI Machine Learning Repository and OpenML . These datasets span binary and multiclass tasks across diverse domains, including healthcare, education, finance, industry, and software engineering. Each dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test splits with the ratio of 8:1:1 via stratified sampling. All predictors share identical splits within each random seed. The training split serves as the retrieval candidate pool. We report macro-averaged accuracy across all datasets to prevent large test sets from dominating aggregate evaluation. Dataset statistics are detailed in Appendix~. Feature types are determined from the training split. Integer-valued numerical features with at most 20 unique values are treated as categorical. Numerical features are standardized via Z-score normalization, with imputation and standardization statistics fitted strictly on the current candidate pool. For each dataset and random seed, we construct a fixed candidate sequence shared by all test queries and predictors. To ensure initial class coverage, we first randomly draw one training example from each class, and then sample remaining examples in proportion to class frequencies. Candidate pools of size $n \{16,32,64,128,256,512\}$ are formed as nested prefixes of this sequence, with the full pool comprising the entire training split. For any query $x$, let $N_k $ denote the $k$ nearest neighbors retrieved from pool $P_n$. Distance-KNN couples nearest-neighbor retrieval with distance-weighted voting . Predictions are aggregated via inverse distance weighting. Tie-breaking and near-zero distance handling are detailed in Appendix~. The prompt concatenates the task description, feature names, label verbalizers, and the retrieved demonstrations. Demonstrations are serialized as feature key--value pairs with their ground-truth labels and ordered from nearest to farthest, followed by the query features. Predictions are obtained greedily from label-token log-probabilities. Crucially, the LLM receives the exact same retrieved neighbors as Distance-KNN, with retrieval distances omitted from the prompt. The prompt template is provided in Appendix~. Label consensus summarizes neighborhood label homogeneity. Following , we define consensus as the majority class fraction among the retrieved neighbors: C ={k} _c _{i N_k } . For example, among eight retrieved neighbors, a 4–4 label split yields a consensus of 4/8, whereas a 7–1 split yields 7/8. Unlike Distance-KNN voting, this measure does not weight labels by their retrieval distances. For each test query, we compute the mean distance to its retrieved neighbors, with smaller distances indicating closer neighborhoods in feature space. Within each dataset and seed, these distances are ranked across test queries and converted into percentiles to enable comparisons of relative proximity across datasets. Lower percentiles indicate closer neighborhoods, with the 0–20 percentile range representing the closest group. Our work highlights the importance of comparing LLMs against traditional classifiers on the same retrieved examples and motivates further work on identifying the neighborhoods in which LLMs are most useful. *{Limitations} Our study focuses on the predictive value of general-purpose LLMs given the same retrieved neighbors as Distance-KNN. Although this design controls demonstration selection, it does not evaluate independently optimized end-to-end systems or task-specific model adaptation. Moreover, while our evaluation spans 19 diverse datasets, validating whether these behavioral patterns generalize to other emerging tabular benchmarks or highly domain-specific datasets remains a promising direction for future work. *{Ethical Considerations} Our study uses publicly available benchmark datasets and existing pretrained language models, involving no new data collection or human annotation. Our experiments aim to provide empirical insights into retrieval-based tabular prediction rather than establish the suitability of these methods for real-world deployment.
