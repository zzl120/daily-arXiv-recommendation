Several recent CTR models, such as FinalNet and QNN, report higher accuracy when a network also learns from its own averaged predictions, for example by running the same network under different dropout patterns and training each run toward the average. Why this self-distillation helps, and whether its random sampling is needed, remains unclear. This work shows that as the number of dropout patterns grows without bound, the objective reduces exactly to the label loss of the logit-averaged prediction plus a penalty on how much the dropout copies of the network disagree. When dropout does not affect how a network gates its features, this penalty can be approximated in closed form, and FLMNet is designed on this principle and trains without sampling. Networks trained from different seeds disagree in the same form, and distilling several of them into one network recovers part of their ensemble gain. Production systems make predictions billions of times per day and replicate the trained model across data centers for serving~, so the deployed model is expected to answer each request with a single forward pass. The inputs are dozens of categorical fields, some with millions of distinct values, and whether a user clicks depends on how the values of different fields combine. The number of possible combinations grows multiplicatively with the number of fields and far exceeds what any training set covers, so a model cannot estimate each combination separately and must share parameters across them. Factorization machines share them by mapping each value to a low-dimensional embedding and scoring a combination by the inner product of its embeddings, so that every combination draws on all records in which its values occur~. Embeddings and the interactions computed from them have since been the starting point of neural CTR models. Neural CTR models share a common template: each field value is mapped to an embedding, an interaction network combines the embeddings, and a linear head produces the logit. Successive designs have enriched the interaction network, from DeepFM, which feeds a shared embedding to both a factorization machine and a deep network~, to explicit high-order modules such as the compressed interaction network of xDeepFM~ and the cross network of DCNv2~. A recurring building block in this line is the Hadamard product, which raises the interaction order by multiplying a representation elementwise with a learned projection of the input. FINAL stacks Hadamard products into a factorized interaction layer, the basis of FinalNet~. QNN treats networks built on this operation, including factorization machines, cross networks, and FINAL, as neuron formats of a quadratic neural network, compares 25 of them under one setting, and builds its model, QNN-$ $, on the strongest~. Both FinalNet and QNN report strong accuracy on public benchmarks. Both models also add to the label loss a training signal that the model generates for itself. FinalNet computes predictions from two interaction blocks, averages their logits into a single score, and trains each block toward this aggregated score; the authors call this self-knowledge distillation~. QNN instead runs one network twice under independent dropout masks, averages the two predicted probabilities into a frozen target, and trains both passes toward this target; the authors call this a self-ensemble loss~. In both cases the teacher is an average over members of the model being trained, the members differ only in which block or which mask produces them, and no second model is trained or served. We refer to this family of objectives as self-ensemble distillation. Both papers support the added term with ablations, and removing it lowers AUC on every dataset they report. The drop is smallest on Criteo for QNN, 0.06 percentage points, where its authors note that gains between well-tuned models are hard to obtain on large sparse data, and clearer on the smaller Frappe and KKBox datasets, 0.17 and 0.19 points~; for FinalNet it ranges from 0.03 points on Avazu to 0.12 on Frappe~. The two papers explain the gain differently, FinalNet as knowledge shared between blocks and QNN as mutual correction between passes that smooths the decision boundary, but neither runs an experiment that isolates which property of the objective produces it. What the self-ensemble objective regularizes, how its teacher should be formed, and whether the same effect can be obtained without extra stochastic forward passes therefore remain open, and these questions are the starting point of this paper. These objectives and a classical ensemble average over different kinds of members. In FinalNet and QNN, the members belong to one model and differ only in the block or dropout mask that produces the prediction, so the teacher is an average taken at a single set of weights. In a classical ensemble, the members are independently trained networks, and the teacher averages solutions reached from different initializations~ or data orders. The two averages are not interchangeable: functions sampled from the dropout neighborhood of one solution stay within a single mode in prediction space, whereas independently trained networks explore different modes~. Pooled in logit space, however, both averages induce a gap of the same form, the mean of the softplus minus the softplus of the mean, because the label term of the cross-entropy loss is linear in the logit; the gap is taken over masks in one case and over solutions in the other. Distilling an ensemble of independently trained models into one network is an established route to a stronger single CTR model~, with teachers that differ in architecture or in the split of the training data. Our experiments on Criteo suggest that the gain requires neither difference , in line with the finding in speech recognition that random initialization alone gives sufficient diversity~. Towers of one architecture that differ only in their seed pass more than half of the gain of a five-tower ensemble to a student, and towers that differ only in the order of the training examples disagree almost as much as towers from different seeds . The student starts from a converged tower, where the training labels no longer point toward parameters that generalize better but the pooled logit of the other seeds still does. Figure~ sketches both kinds of member and how each reaches a single deployed tower. Codistillation shows that distillation between networks of one architecture can improve CTR models. Two copies of a network are trained together, each distilling from the other, and on Criteo either copy matches the log loss of their two-model ensemble~. The authors attribute the gain mainly to the copies seeing different subsets of the data and leave open why a teacher's mistakes help the student. In CTR prediction, FinalNet and QNN are two of several models that add auxiliary losses built from their own sub-networks or from perturbed views of their features~. Their authors explain the gains differently: FSDNet credits the guidance its deepest layer gives the shallower ones~, FCN credits a label loss tailored to each of its sub-networks~, and CL4CTR credits better representations of rare features learned through contrastive losses~. None of these papers, FinalNet and QNN included, identifies what the added loss regularizes. Outside CTR prediction, dropout, its consistency variants, and distillation among networks of one architecture have been studied for other tasks, from linear regression to language modeling, image classification, and sequential recommendation~, but their explanations do not directly account for the gains in CTR models. For members trained independently, transferring the gain into a single model is established; what remains open is whether the gains from masked members and from independently trained members are distinct, and whether one tower can collect both. Prompted by these methods, we trace where their gain comes from, with ablations that vary the training objective , the seed , and the data order . This paper asks three questions. What does the masked self-ensemble objective become as the number of masks grows without bound, and what does the limit regularize? Which tower structure gives the moments of the logit over masks in closed form, so that the limit can be trained with one deterministic forward pass? And can the further gain of independently trained solutions be collected in the same tower, and at what training cost? {figures/intro_members.pdf} Our answer to the first two questions is analytic. For members pooled in logit space, as the number of masks grows without bound, the masked self-ensemble objective converges to the label loss at the mean logit plus a multiple of the Jensen gap of the softplus over masks, up to a term with zero gradient. The limit therefore regularizes the disagreement among masked members and nothing else. Ordinary dropout training is the special case with multiplier one. In the limit, the QNN objective with its pool taken in logit space has multiplier two, so only the excess over one is distillation added beyond dropout. Training replaces the gap by a Gaussian surrogate, which needs only the mean and variance of the logit over masks. Both moments have closed forms under three conditions: the multiplicative gates of the tower read only unmasked inputs, the readout is affine, and each layer rescales its running representation coordinatewise. The mean logit then equals the logit of the forward pass without dropout, and an inexpensive approximation of the exact variance lets the surrogate be evaluated within one deterministic forward pass. QNN-$ $, the strongest baseline on average in our experiments, does not meet the first condition: its gate reads the masked output of the previous layer, so a closed form misstates the mean and variance of its logit over masks. Letting its gate read the input embedding makes the closed form exact, but on Criteo this change lowers validation AUC by $10.5 10^{-4}$ even without the penalty. We therefore design FLMNet, a single tower whose field-local gate units each read one field of the unmasked embedding, with a learned mixer that spreads each field's gate over the other fields. FLMNet satisfies all three conditions by construction, and without the penalty its validation AUC comes within $0.9 10^{-4}$ of that of the original QNN-$ $. The third question turns from masks to seeds. Towers trained from different seeds also disagree, and pooling their logits lowers Logloss below that of the average member by exactly the gap across seeds. This gap has no closed form, but part of the gain can be moved into one tower by distillation: FLMNet-KD starts from one FLMNet-CF tower, the tower trained with the closed-form penalty, and is trained to match the pooled logit of $K$ such towers, at the cost of $K+1$ training runs. We call the part of a member's logit that departs from the pooled logit its noise: mask noise across the masks of one run, and seed noise across seeds. The pooled logit keeps what the members share, and the noise is what each member fits on its own; in our experiments, suppressing it mainly holds back overfitting . The penalty suppresses mask noise and distillation suppresses seed noise, and the deployed model is in both cases a single tower that predicts in one forward pass. Our contributions are as follows. We derive the infinite-masking limit of self-ensemble distillation for members pooled in logit space, show that it regularizes only the Jensen gap among masked members, with ordinary dropout as the special case of multiplier one, and bound the gap above and below in terms of the variance of the logit. We isolate three conditions under which the mean and variance of the logit over masks have closed forms, and design FLMNet, a single tower that satisfies them and trains the surrogate of the limit without sampled forward passes, with a step time close to that of QNN-$ $. On five public benchmarks against seventeen baselines, FLMNet-CF and FLMNet-KD take the top two places in both AUC and Logloss, and on the benchmarks where each gain alone exceeds the variation across seeds, the gains of the closed-form penalty and of cross-seed distillation largely add up. A bank of reads the embedding: every field's $d$ coordinates feed their own group of ReLU units, and no unit sees more than one field. Cross-seed distillation adds a second training stage but leaves inference unchanged. $K$ towers are first trained independently as above, and their pooled logit $ _K$ is computed once on the training rows and stored. A student initialized from one of these towers is then trained on alone, without the label or the penalty. The student has the same architecture and is deployed as a single tower, so the procedure costs $K$ tower trainings followed by one student run, and still one forward pass per prediction; Section~ reports both costs on Criteo. We evaluate FLMNet on five standard benchmarks against seventeen established models and, under a common protocol, report the highest accuracy on every benchmark. Section~ describes the setup and Section~ the main comparison; the remaining subsections ask where this accuracy comes from. We start with the tower : on QNN-$ $, whose gate violates , the closed form falls short of the Monte Carlo gap and training lowers the estimate far more than the gap it estimates; restoring costs accuracy, which FLMNet recovers. The remaining subsections trace the accuracy beyond the tower to the suppression of the two kinds of noise defined in Sections~ and~: mask noise, the disagreement among a tower's predictions under different masks, which the closed-form penalty suppresses at the infinite-masking limit, without sampling masks ; and seed noise, the disagreement among towers trained from different seeds, which survives that limit. Pooling towers trained with different seeds averages seed noise away, and cross-seed distillation carries the resulting gain into a single tower . For each source we ask whether suppressing it helps, why, and whether it helps for the reason the theory gives. Section~ closes with consistency across seeds, cost, and a summary of the answers. CTR prediction has largely been a search for the right way to form feature interactions. Logistic regression leaves every cross term to hand engineering~, factorization machines learn pairwise interactions as inner products of latent vectors~, and embedding-and-MLP models leave all interaction to depth~. Later models combine explicit and implicit interactions, through a product layer~, a parallel FM branch~, or explicit layers that raise the interaction order step by step~, with bridges between parallel towers in EDCN~. Others make the interaction adaptive, through self-attention over fields~, learned exponents~ or complex-valued orders~, or multiply representations by learned gates~. Quadratic towers make the product explicit, multiplying a linear projection elementwise with an activated one~, or forming a multi-head Khatri--Rao product between each head of the input and an activated projection of the whole input~. These designs are now compared under the open protocol of BARS~. A second line of work supervises a network with an ensemble. Distillation trains a network to match the outputs of a teacher~, born-again networks take as teacher an earlier network of the same architecture~, and in CTR prediction, distilling an ensemble of independently trained models into one network yields a stronger single model~. Its authors attribute the gain to teachers that differ in architecture or in the split of the training data. Towers of one architecture that differ only in their seed already form such a teacher , and towers that differ only in the order of the training examples disagree almost as much as towers from different seeds and gain almost as much when pooled . The gain therefore need not come from distinct views. We trace it to the course of training: different initializations and data orders already accumulate substantial disagreement, and Appendix~ re-reads their six-teacher result in this light. In concurrent work, Chopra et al. also distill the mean logit of independent runs into one CTR model and attribute the gain to the predictive variation that the runs do not share~, which is the second-order form of the gap in . Whether the views of other architectures add to this disagreement and raise AUC further remains open; answering it would separate what the training trajectory contributes from what a distinct view contributes. Self-ensemble methods build the teacher from the network itself: R-Drop penalizes the divergence between two dropout samples~, FinalNet supervises each of two parallel blocks with their averaged prediction~, and QNN averages two dropout passes into a frozen teacher~. Such teachers are built from a few sampled masks and so carry sampling error. Analytic treatments of dropout remove the sampling instead, through a Gaussian approximation~ or an expansion of the expected objective~. Our work builds on these lines: it trains a gated quadratic tower with the closed-form limit of the self-ensemble objective, and distills an ensemble of its seeds into one tower. FinalNet and QNN report gains in CTR prediction from self-ensemble objectives, but do not explain what the added term regularizes. We answered this for members pooled in logit space. As the number of masks grows without bound, the masked self-ensemble objective converges exactly to the label loss at the mean logit plus a multiple of the Jensen gap of the softplus over masks, so the limit regularizes only the disagreement among masked members, which in practice mainly slows overfitting. When the gates of a tower do not read the masked path, as the field-local gates of FLMNet ensure, the moments of the logit have closed forms, and a Gaussian surrogate of the gap trains the limit in one deterministic forward pass. Independently trained seeds give rise to a gap of the same form, which has no closed form; we reduce it by distilling an ensemble of seeds into the tower. On all five benchmarks, the resulting single tower is the most accurate of the evaluated models, consistently across seeds, and predicts in one forward pass. The gain of the closed-form penalty largely adds to that of cross-seed distillation. These results split the advantage of an ensemble over a single tower into two sources. The first is mask noise, the disagreement among masks. This is where the gain of self-ensemble distillation comes from, and the closed-form penalty reduces it during training without sampled forward passes. The second is seed noise, the disagreement among seeds. It accounts for the further gain of an ensemble, and cross-seed distillation reduces it after training without changing the architecture. FLMNet-CF collects the first gain at a per-step training cost close to that of QNN-$ $, and FLMNet-KD the second at the price of $K+1$ training runs; conditions -- give a concrete criterion for designing towers that admit the closed form. These conclusions have three limits. The closed form is exact only for towers that satisfy -- . Training replaces the exact gap by a Gaussian surrogate. The gap across seeds has no closed form, so how much of it distillation recovers is known only from measurement. The two gains are also collected separately: the penalty within one training run and distillation over $K+1$ runs. Two directions merit future work. The first is to collect both gains in a single training run, for example by training several towers jointly and serving them in one forward pass. A test split is read only through a roster that is committed before any test reading, which lists every checkpoint to be reported by tag, seed and file checksum , together with the decision rule: the comparison, its threshold, and how an outcome in the opposite direction will be read. The reveal tool first recomputes the validation AUC of each listed checkpoint and requires agreement with the value recorded at training time , so a substituted or altered checkpoint is rejected before any test data is touched. It then writes a receipt for each member before computing the test metrics, refuses to overwrite an existing receipt, and refuses to rerun after an interruption. All members of the roster are reported and none is dropped or added. The once-only guarantee holds within one fixed output directory; the tool cannot see a reveal made into a different directory, so it rests on the procedure and the committed roster as well as on the tool. After a reveal, the configuration is frozen, and a later change would need a new roster and would have to be reported together with the order in which the two were run. Our towers were trained with the test data unread , and on the re-split Avazu the baseline harness likewise never constructed a test iterator. In~, the teachers differ in architecture or in the split of the training data, and the advantage of three teachers over one is attributed to the diversity of different teachers. Their six-teacher ensemble of different architectures adds to each of the three architectures a second copy retrained from a new random initialization, and the student improves further; the authors report this as a gain from applying more teachers. The number of teachers, however, does not by itself produce a gain. By , pooling lowers the Logloss below that of the average member by exactly the disagreement $A_{ seed} $ among the members' logits, and an exact copy of a member leaves both the pooled logit and the average member unchanged. A retrained copy helps because its training took a different path through the same data to a different solution, as the order-only pair above does. That work does not single out this source: none of its ensembles varies the seed or the data order alone.
