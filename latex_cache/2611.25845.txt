 Large language models are increasingly being developed into research agents for automating scientific workflows~. These agents support different stages of research, including hypothesis formulation~, literature synthesis~, experiment automation~, and manuscript generation~. Across these stages, literature retrieval provides the evidence needed for downstream research. Retrieval methods can also serve as standalone systems for academic search. However, research-oriented retrieval often requires more than finding topically similar papers. For literature synthesis and experiment planning, researchers may need to identify which prior methods are used as empirical baselines and which later papers compare against a given method. These relations reveal a paper's empirical comparison context and support baseline discovery and contribution positioning. Recent studies further show that paper retrieval quality affects downstream reasoning and generation~, motivating benchmarks that evaluate retrieval beyond topical relevance. Existing scholarly retrieval benchmarks primarily build upon relevance-based or citation-oriented criteria. Relevance-based benchmarks use aspect-specific queries to retrieve papers matching a scientific statement~, but topical relevance does not imply empirical comparison. Two papers may study the same problem without being directly compared in experiments. Citation-oriented benchmarks instead use citation recommendation or prediction as supervision~, but citation relations are heterogeneous and often reflect background discussion, datasets, tools, or theoretical support. As a result, neither signal isolates which prior methods are explicitly used for empirical comparison. Existing benchmarks therefore cannot directly evaluate whether retrieval methods recover the empirical comparison context surrounding a paper. To address this gap, we propose as an auditable relation for literature retrieval. As illustrated in Figure~, an empirical lineage relation links a prior paper to a source paper that explicitly uses its method as a baseline for empirical comparison. Each relation is grounded in explicit evidence. These relations capture the empirical reference frame used to evaluate and position a contribution. By linking these relations across papers, we formulate as the task to evaluate whether a retrieval system can recover papers in these comparison-based neighborhoods. {figs/intro-fig.pdf} , empirical baseline evidence induces directed lineage edges from baseline papers to source papers. Given a query paper, its ancestors and successors in the lineage graph define retrieval ground truth. These relations capture empirical comparison structure beyond generic topical or citation-based relevance.} However, constructing empirical lineage graphs from scientific literature is non-trivial. ~Corpus Grounding:} Lineage retrieval requires an auditable retrieval space where each paper node and each lineage edge can be consistently identified. This is difficult because scientific papers are distributed across heterogeneous open-access sources with inconsistent coverage, metadata, and PDF availability. ~Evidence-to-Edge Ambiguity:} Unlike citation links, lineage relations must be identified from full-paper empirical evidence. Baseline mentions can appear in different sections and forms, making it difficult to distinguish true comparison targets from other citations. ~Graph Suitability:} Extracted lineage relations do not directly form graphs suitable for retrieval evaluation. Some widely reused baselines can create overly broad connected regions even when individual comparison relations are limited. Such regions make multi-hop neighborhoods less suitable as retrieval ground truth. To address these challenges, we propose -Constructor}, an automatic pipeline for constructing empirical lineage graphs from scientific literature. The pipeline consists of three stages. ~Unified Literature Collector} builds a unified retrieval corpus from heterogeneous open-access sources, aligning metadata, validating PDFs, and normalizing paper records. ~Evidence-Grounded Lineage Extraction} extracts lineage relations from empirical baseline evidence. Since such evidence can appear across different parts in varying formats, we design a dual-source extractor that provides complementary paper representations and enables cross-checking of outputs, improving coverage of dispersed baseline mentions while reducing spurious edges. We further annotate a human-annotated validation set, -HA, to guide extractor optimization and evaluate extraction quality. ~Graph Calibration} enforces temporal validity and regularizes abnormal graph connectivity, yielding calibrated lineage graphs for retrieval evaluation. Based on these calibrated graphs, we build the } benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval in artificial intelligence scientific literature. Unlike citation-based retrieval benchmarks, which rely on a single paper, lineage retrieval needs broad coverage across AI venues, as each directed edge points from the baseline paper to the source paper that uses it as an empirical comparison baseline. Literature collection is, therefore, a core component of benchmark construction. In practice, there is no single open-access source providing complete and uniform records. DBLP~ offers reliable venue-year paper lists, but abstracts and PDFs often need to be fetched from other providers. These providers differ in metadata schema, naming conventions, URL formats, and file availability. Directly merging them may create inconsistent records and noisy classifiers, which can propagate errors into lineage extraction and graph construction. We built a unified literature collector module to address this issue. The module integrates 11 complementary open-access sources. It first constructs a canonical venue-year paper list from DBLP and filters out records that are unlikely to be regular research papers, including tutorials, demos, workshops, and student abstracts. It then enriches each retained paper using venue-specific open-access sources. Official proceedings are prioritized when available. OpenReview~ and arXiv~ are used to recover missing PDFs. The venue-specific source configuration is provided in . We restrict the benchmark corpus to peer-reviewed conference papers, as they commonly provide explicit empirical comparisons and consistent full-paper evidence for lineage extraction. After metadata enrichment, the module downloads and validates the corresponding PDFs before downstream parsing. A lightweight page-length filter removes short files that are unlikely to be regular papers. The module also performs diagnostic checks for suspicious metadata and PDFs, as detailed in . These checks provide a practical repair interface and reduce the risk of noisy records propagating into the lineage construction process. Unlike citation links, which are explicitly marked and often available from structured metadata, empirical lineage relations must be identified from full-paper comparison evidence. These signals are distributed across papers and often use non-canonical method names, making direct rule-based construction unreliable. We therefore design an evidence-grounded lineage extraction module that progressively narrows the corpus, grounds extracted relations in paper content, and converts the retained relations into a corpus-grounded lineage graph. Regular papers in our corpora include various contribution types, such as methodology papers, benchmark papers, and surveys. Since benchmark and survey papers primarily build evaluation resources or synthesize existing work rather than proposing new technical methods, their citation relations are less suitable for constructing lineage graphs. We therefore first identify methodology papers for downstream lineage extraction. Specifically, we prompt with each paper's title and abstract. The prompt classifies papers by their primary contribution, classifying papers as only when the central contribution is a technical method rather than an evaluation resource or a synthesis of prior work. Detailed prompts are provided in . To robustly extract lineage relations from scientific papers, we propose a dual-source lineage extractor with candidate-restricted integration. Each PDF is parsed by two complementary tools: ~, which produces semi-structured XML, and ~, which produces markdown with paper-related structures. Rather than concatenating the two parser outputs, we prompt to candidate lineage relations from each source independently with the prompt presented in . We then the two candidate sets through a grounding step that re-checks each candidate against the paper content and retains only supported relations. This design uses parser-specific extraction to improve recall while limiting integration to evidence-based conflict resolution. After extracting lineage relations from individual papers, we construct a corpus-grounded directed lineage graph $= $ over the collected paper database. Each node $v $ denotes a paper in the collected corpus, and each directed edge $e= $ indicates that the source paper $v_s$ explicitly uses the method from $v_b$ as an empirical comparison baseline. We retain only source--baseline pairs in which both papers can be matched to the collected corpus, so that every graph node belongs to the same retrieval space used for downstream evaluation. Nodes store basic paper metadata, while edges retain extraction provenance. The resulting graph encodes `` |is_baseline_of|'' relations, with the reverse direction corresponding to `` |uses_baseline|''. To enhance the reliability of constructed lineage graphs, we employ temporal and structural consistency calibration to filter out abnormal relations. Temporal calibration enforces valid edge ordering. Structural calibration controls overly broad connectivity in multi-hop neighborhoods. Stage-wise calibration analysis is in ~. We first calibrate the graph by enforcing temporal validity. A retained lineage edge must point from a baseline paper to a source paper published in the same year or later, since an empirical baseline cannot depend on future work. Edges that violate this ordering are removed as invalid lineage candidates. We further calibrate the graph to make its connected regions suitable for lineage-based retrieval evaluation. Because widely reused baselines can create very large connected components, raw components can be too broad for defining informative multi-hop retrieval neighborhoods. We therefore apply a fixed component-level calibration procedure to split components classified as upper-tail outliers by the rule~ into more coherent subgraphs using bridge pruning~ and Louvain community partitioning~. Finally, we discard isolated nodes that no longer participate in any retained lineage relation. These operations produce lineage graphs with valid temporal ordering and controlled connectivity for retrieval evaluation. To address this gap, we introduce }, a benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval in AI scientific literature. An empirical lineage relation links a prior paper to a source paper that explicitly uses its method as an empirical comparison baseline. These relations form lineage graphs whose multi-hop neighborhoods provide retrieval ground truth for structural evaluation. To construct these graphs at scale, we develop -Constructor}, which converts heterogeneous paper records and full-paper empirical baseline evidence into calibrated lineage graphs. Built from 121,048 papers across 17 top AI venues from 2018 to 2025, contains 3,950 graph components, 67,502 paper nodes, 164,948 lineage edges, and 80,000 multi-facet retrieval queries.
