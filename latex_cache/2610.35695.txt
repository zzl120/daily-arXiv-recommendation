However, predictive performance also depends on how input-side information is configured before interaction modeling. Existing studies have shown that selecting informative samples, features, or embedding units can improve prediction accuracy. Nevertheless, they primarily optimize these selection mechanisms independently along a single input-side dimension, overlooking their shared basic form and the inherent dependencies across input levels, thereby limiting the effectiveness of input-side dimensional configuration. To address this issue, we propose OptIDC, a unified framework for Optimal Input-side Dimensional Configuration in deep CTR prediction. OptIDC jointly learns sample, feature, and embedding-unit level configurations within a unified framework by integrating their corresponding selection mechanisms. To explicitly coordinate selection decisions across different levels, OptIDC further introduces a selection-weighted Optimal Transport mechanism that aligns coarse- and fine-grained selections via transport over finite measures, thereby alleviating cross-level selection conflicts while preserving the semantics of each input level. Moreover, OptIDC is model-agnostic and can be seamlessly integrated into existing CTR models. Extensive experiments on four real-world benchmark datasets demonstrate that OptIDC consistently achieves higher AUC than existing input-side selection methods, validating the effectiveness of unified input-side dimensional configuration. In web applications such as online advertising and recommendation systems, click-through rate prediction estimates the probability that a user will click on a candidate item if displayed and supports content ranking. Much of the progress in deep CTR prediction has come from increasingly expressive architectures for modeling feature interactions~. These architectures, however, operate on the information supplied to them. Real-world training data contain samples of uneven quality, feature fields of varying relevance, and embedding units that do not contribute equally to prediction. CTR prediction performance therefore depends on two complementary choices: how information is modeled by the architecture and what information is allowed to shape the model. We use the term for the latter, spanning the sample, feature, and embedding-unit levels. {figure/OptIDC_overview.pdf} {<A full description of Figure 1 can be found in the second paragraph of Section 1.>} Fig.~ places existing input-side selection methods within a common deep CTR pipeline. At the sample level, instance selection and data denoising reweight or remove training examples~. At the feature level, field- and value-level selection methods learn gates that retain informative inputs before interaction modeling~. At the embedding-unit level, pruning and dimension search allocate representation capacity at a finer granularity~. The gains reported across this substantial body of work confirm that input configuration can materially affect CTR prediction performance and efficiency. In most prior work, however, only one of these three selection paths is optimized at a time, while the other input levels are treated as fixed. Decisions that ultimately shape the same model input are therefore learned without explicit coordination across levels. Despite their different names and granularities, the three selection mechanisms share the same basic form: each assigns a selection weight to an input component and uses that weight to control the component's contribution to model learning. Sample selection assigns one weight to an entire instance, feature selection assigns weights to its field representations, and embedding-unit selection assigns weights to individual coordinates within those representations. When expressed over the embedding tensor, they can all be viewed as masks applied along different input axes. This common form also exposes their dependence. A sample contributes through its selected feature embeddings, and a feature contributes through its selected embedding units. If a feature is masked out, any unit-level decisions within it become ineffective; if nearly all feature representations of a highly weighted sample are suppressed, the sample-level decision has little effect. We therefore view the three mechanisms as coupled decisions about the same effective input, rather than as three unrelated selection problems. Their common form motivates joint modeling. Blindly attaching all three selection mechanisms to the same backbone, however, leaves the final prediction error as their only cross-level signal. Sample weighting controls how much gradient an instance contributes, feature gating controls which field representations receive that gradient, and embedding-unit masking controls which coordinates carry it. Their updates can therefore interfere: a low sample weight may deprive the finer-grained mechanisms of useful feedback, while aggressive feature or embedding-unit masks can make a highly weighted sample almost ineffective. Effective joint configuration must preserve useful specialization at each level and control severe cross-level mismatches, which we call . It must also coordinate outputs with different structures: a sample weight is a scalar, a feature gate is defined over fields, and an embedding-unit mask is defined over individual coordinates. These requirements expose the central challenge of unified input-side configuration: To tackle this challenge, we propose , a unified framework that learns configurations at the sample, feature, and embedding-unit levels. OptIDC introduces a selection-weighted optimal transport formulation over finite measures for cross-level coordination~. For each coarse-to-fine relation, the coarse-level selection weight determines the total transported mass, the finer-grained weights allocate this mass over their semantic support, and representation dissimilarity defines the transport cost. This formulation accommodates different selection cardinalities and retains the semantics of each level through the representation-based cost. We construct three OT objectives for the sample--feature, feature--embedding-unit, and sample--embedding-unit relations. Together, they provide direct coordination signals between adjacent and non-adjacent levels, encouraging each coarse-grained decision to be supported by compatible fine-grained decisions. During configuration learning, the sample weights, feature gates, embedding-unit masks, and transport costs are optimized together with the prediction objective. The learned configuration is then used to retrain the backbone with the selected input information. Since OptIDC operates on the input side, it leaves the backbone's interaction architecture unchanged and can be used with different CTR models. We evaluate the framework on four public datasets and five representative backbones. Across the evaluated settings, joint configuration improves over the corresponding single-level alternatives, and OT coordination provides further gains over joint configuration alone. Ablation, efficiency, and mask analyses further examine the source of these gains. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: We connect sample, feature, and embedding-unit selection through their common masking form and formulate the input-side dimensional configuration as a coupled problem over the three input levels. We develop OptIDC with a selection-weighted coordination objective for the sample--feature, feature--embedding-unit, and sample--embedding-unit relations, combining coarse selection mass, fine-level allocation, and representation compatibility through a finite-measure OT formulation. We evaluate OptIDC on four datasets and five CTR prediction backbones. The experiments compare it with single-level alternatives and disentangle the effects of joint configuration and OT coordination through ablation, efficiency, and selection consistency analyses. In this section, we briefly review some relevant works on two research topics, including deep CTR prediction and automated selection in CTR prediction. Deep learning has become a widely adopted approach to CTR prediction due to its strong capacity for modeling complex interactions among user, item, and contextual features. Early models, such as factorization machines, model pairwise interactions among sparse input features~, while deep architectures such as Wide\&Deep and DeepFM further capture higher-order interactions~. Subsequent advances further improve modeling capacity through explicit feature-interaction networks, attention mechanisms, and multi-task learning~. Despite their success, these architectural approaches typically treat all input-side information, such as samples, features, and embedding units, equally, without selecting or weighting informative signals during modeling. Thus, redundant or uninformative inputs may still limit efficiency and predictive performance when handling large-scale and high-dimensional data. In contrast, OptIDC complements these architectural advances by jointly configuring information at the sample, feature, and embedding-unit levels for CTR prediction. Introducing automated selection mechanisms into CTR prediction has attracted increasing attention in recent years as an effective way to improve prediction performance~. Existing studies mainly investigate information selection in three input-side dimensions. For feature inputs, prior work focuses on identifying informative feature subsets through field-level or value-level selection to reduce redundancy~. For embedding representations, embedding-unit or -dimension selection methods further refine representations by retaining important embedding elements and reducing unnecessary parameters~. OptEmbed~ combines feature selection with embedding-dimension search. For training instances, sample selection methods identify high-value or reliable samples for model training~. Beyond input-side information, automated selection has also been explored for feature interaction~, loss functions~, and hyperparameters~. Unlike these methods, which usually optimize one selection space at a time, our work extends input-side selection to a unified setting that jointly considers the sample, feature, and embedding-unit levels. We first examine whether the three input levels benefit from joint configuration.
