Across three Amazon benchmarks, BRIDGE improves Recall@10 and NDCG@10 by 10.9\ Generative recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm that casts item retrieval as autoregressive sequence generation~. An RQ-VAE quantizer first maps each item into a hierarchical sequence of Semantic IDs , where each level refines the representation of the previous one. At inference time, a decoder autoregressively generates SID tokens level by level, effectively searching the item catalog through structured generation. Compared with dual-tower retrieval, this paradigm explicitly models the semantic hierarchy of items and has demonstrated strong performance on standard benchmarks. Despite this progress, we identify a fundamental asymmetry between the quantization stage and the generation stage that has been largely overlooked. Quantization constructs SIDs from the rich continuous structure of item embeddings: codebook centroids encode coarse semantic clusters, inter-item neighborhood geometry captures relational proximity, and level-wise entropy reflects how ambiguous each level of the hierarchy is. None of this structure is accessible to the decoder, which receives only discrete code indices as supervision. We term this asymmetry the , illustrated in Figure~, and observe that it manifests at three distinct points. {figure2.pdf} The first-level SID token $s^1$ partitions items into coarse semantic clusters and is the highest-entropy, hardest-to-predict decision—errors here cascade through all subsequent levels. Yet the decoder must predict $s^1$ with the least target-side context, having no access to the codebook centroids that defined those clusters in the first place. This makes the model prone to unstable coarse routing, especially when user histories are short or ambiguous. One-hot supervision treats every incorrect SID as equally wrong, discarding the graded semantic proximity encoded in the quantizer's neighborhood geometry. Furthermore, because the decoder is trained on ground-truth SID prefixes but must generate its own at inference time, standard teacher forcing induces exposure bias—a train--test mismatch that one-hot targets cannot mitigate. Residual quantization by design makes early levels high-entropy and later levels increasingly deterministic refinements. A standard decoder allocates uniform compute across all levels, inverting the natural difficulty prior and wasting capacity on easy levels while underserving hard ones, which ultimately hurts top-rank accuracy where early-level mistakes are most costly. We propose , a difficulty-aware framework that reinserts the discarded quantization structure at all three points. injects frozen quantizer centroids as semantic anchors into a latent representation formed before $s^1$ generation, closing the grounding gap. trains an EMA teacher on semantically neighboring items and distills its soft distributions into the on-policy student, recovering neighborhood geometry and mitigating exposure bias. allocates more iterative refinement to early, high-entropy levels and tapers compute with depth, matching the difficulty prior. The three components couple architecturally: latent reasoning constitutes the first recurrent iteration, and its codebook-anchored representation serves simultaneously as the self-distillation teacher, avoiding redundant computation. In summary, our contributions are as follows: We identify and formalize the Semantic Quantization Gap , a previously overlooked encoder--decoder asymmetry in generative recommendation, and decompose it into three actionable gaps. We propose BRIDGE, which closes each gap with a targeted, architecturally coupled component: codebook-grounded latent reasoning , privileged EMA self-distillation , and effort-aware recurrence . Future work includes learning the loop schedule end-to-end and extending to dynamic item sets and multi-modal SIDs. *{Ethical Considerations} We study generative retrieval for recommendation using public datasets and item-content embeddings. Potential risks include propagating or amplifying biases present in the underlying data and text encoder, privacy leakage if deployed on sensitive user logs, and misuse for manipulative ranking. To mitigate these risks in practice, deployments should minimize or anonymize user signals, apply access control and monitoring, and evaluate subgroup performance and calibration to detect disparate impact. Our method does not require additional user-side features beyond standard interaction histories; the privileged neighbor signal used in distillation is computed from item embeddings and is available only during training.
