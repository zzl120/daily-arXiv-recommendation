However, their issued rankings require reliable instance-level uncertainty monitoring. In this scenario, comparing uncertainty quantification methods becomes challenging because LLM rankers lack a standard item-classification head. Their scores may depend on option labels, prompts, generation procedures, and the available user and item evidence. Existing methods derive uncertainty from different sources and apply it at different stages of the recommendation process. Some methods evaluate an issued ranking, whereas others use uncertainty to change the ranking itself, making direct comparison difficult. Here we introduce a fixed-ranking benchmark which evaluates eight heterogeneous UQ signals as record-level monitors under a shared output contract. We evaluate these signals across four recommendation datasets and two LLM backbones under a shared fixed-ranking protocol. From the results, we draw three main observations. First, UQ method ordering changes across backbones even on the same reference instances. Second, ranking-aligned Top10Mass provides the strongest scalar Error@10 detection in several evaluation conditions, reflecting effective cutoff-aligned monitoring, while its advantage narrows for popularity-tail targets. Third, scalar UQ signals are weak at identifying which control has the larger clipped NDCG@10 difference, whereas source-specific signals, including Perturbation Rank Instability and simple listwise distances, provide stronger paired-control agreement, reflecting their sensitivity to the two controlled evidence sources. These findings motivate us to evaluate ranking-failure detection, robustness across recommendation conditions, and responses to controlled evidence changes as distinct properties of UQ. Large language models provide a flexible interface for recommendation systems by representing user histories, candidate items, and task instructions in natural language and asking an instruction-tuned model to rank the candidates . Such an interface can support zero-shot or few-shot recommendation without requiring an additional recommendation-specific prediction head for each domain. In this work, we focus on the LLM-as-reranker setting and use released instruction-tuned backbones directly, without training from scratch or recommendation-specific fine-tuning. However, a fluent and well-formed ranked list may still be unreliable. The relevant item may be missing from the top results even when the model shows strong confidence. Recent work has begun to consider whether average ranking utility alone is sufficient for evaluating LLM-based recommendation systems . This broader reliability perspective examines whether the model outputs remain trustworthy when the confidence is unreliable, fairness concerns arise, or recommendations are sensitive to preference and presentation biases . Practical deployment therefore requires signals that can monitor the reliability of each issued ranking. However, applying uncertainty quantification to LLM-based ranking is not straightforward. Unlike fixed-class prediction, recommendation produces a user-dependent ranked list whose top-$k$ outcome is the object being monitored. An LLM ranker may output candidate labels or the entire ranked list, and its uncertainty can also be affected by the prompt, the option-label format, the decoding or sampling procedure, and the user or item evidence provided in the context. Consequently, different UQ methods may reflect different aspects of the model behavior, which makes their scores difficult to interpret and compare under a common standard. Several kinds of work have approached the uncertainty from different angles. Classical methods focus on the confidence attached to a prediction, either by using predictive probabilities to detect likely errors or by examining whether reported confidence matches the empirical correctness . Language-model research extends this idea beyond a single probability score by studying the self-assessed correctness and consistency across sampled responses . Moreover, in recommendation, uncertainty has taken on a broader role: it has been used to estimate the recommendation quality , distinguish recommendation uncertainty from uncertainty of prompts , and support reranking or model optimization . These methods are difficult to compare directly because they differ both in the uncertainty signals they use and in how uncertainty enters the recommendation process . In particular, methods that use uncertainty for reranking or model optimization also change the recommendation output, making it difficult to separate UQ monitoring quality from changes in the underlying ranking. To isolate monitoring quality, we therefore hold each issued ranking fixed and map every method to the same record-level output. This fixed-ranking design allows different UQ signals to be evaluated on the same recommendation outcome under a common standard. Building on this fixed-ranking design, we construct a benchmark for UQ in LLM-based recommendation. Each 20-candidate base ranking is generated once and remains unchanged during UQ evaluation. Eight heterogeneous UQ signals are compared as the record-level monitors under a shared output contract. The full Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct evaluation spans four recommendation datasets and four evaluation settings: a full-evidence reference condition , user-history truncation , controlled candidate item-text ablation , and a subset of Warm-start records whose positive items fall in the popularity tail . We also evaluate Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode on the same 4,000 reference instances to examine whether UQ method ordering is preserved on a second backbone. This comparison is limited to the reference condition and does not establish broad cross-model transfer. The benchmark defines two complementary evaluation tasks. evaluates whether a UQ score can flag Error@10 cases, where the target item is absent from the fixed top-10 ranking. compares matched user-history and item-content interventions for the same base instance and tests whether a method responds more strongly to the source with the larger clipped NDCG@10 difference. First, the relative effectiveness of UQ monitors is not stable across LLM backbones. Second, ranking-aligned probability mass provides a strong scalar signal for detecting ranking failures, but its advantage varies across evaluation conditions and target-popularity regimes; these results support ranking-aligned monitoring for the corresponding ranking-failure objective. Third, scalar failure detection and paired evidence-response agreement capture different properties of UQ: source-specific signals are better suited to source-specific diagnosis under the clipped paired-control target, with simple non-UQ ranking distances showing similarly strong agreement. Our contributions are summarized as follows: We define fixed-ranking UQ monitoring for LLM-based recommendation and introduce two evaluation tasks: Error@10 detection and paired-control response agreement for user-history and item-content evidence; We build an extensive benchmark that compares eight UQ signals across four datasets. It includes a full Llama evaluation suite and a Qwen Warm-start replication on the same 4,000 instances under a shared record-level contract; We provide practical guidance for evaluating UQ in LLM-based recommendation: ranking-failure detection and paired-control response agreement capture distinct properties of UQ, and method robustness should be assessed across backbones and recommendation conditions because conclusions from a single reference setting may not generalize. Language-model-based recommendation builds on earlier work which formulates recommendation as a text generation task. P5 represents user interactions, item information, and different recommendation tasks in a unified text-to-text framework . With the development of instruction-tuned LLMs, researchers began to examine whether a general language model could perform recommendation directly. A common setting presents the user's history, candidate items, and ranking instruction in natural language, then asks the model to return a ranked list . Later studies adapted LLMs more closely to recommendation data and item spaces. TALLRec tunes an LLM with recommendation instructions for the prediction of user preference ; BIGRec generates semantic item representations and maps them to actual items through a two-step grounding procedure ; GenRec directly generates target items from textual user and item information . Moreover, RecRanker tunes an LLM for pointwise, pairwise, and listwise top-$k$ ranking tasks . LLMs have also been integrated into multi-stage recommendation systems. LlamaRec first retrieves a smaller candidate set with a lightweight sequential recommender and then applies an LLM ranker to those candidates . Together, these studies cover several ways of using language models in recommendation tasks, like task-specific tuning, item grounding, direct generation, candidate ranking, and retrieval ranking integration. More recent research has expanded the evaluation beyond average ranking quality because strong aggregate performance does not guarantee reliable results for every user or candidate set . This broader reliability perspective includes uncertainty and fairness across recommendation cases , as well as the sensitivity to preference and presentation biases . Building on this reliability perspective, our study evaluates whether uncertainty signals can identify unreliable issued rankings while keeping the recommender output and candidate order fixed. Uncertainty quantification provides signals for assessing how reliable a model’s output is . Probability-based methods summarize uncertainty through quantities such as maximum confidence, predictive entropy, and the gap between leading candidates . Calibration addresses a different question by testing whether the reported confidence matches observed frequencies of correctness . Selective prediction examines how prediction risk changes as increasingly uncertain examples are removed . These properties are related, but strong performance on one does not guarantee strong performance on the others. For language models, uncertainty can also be estimated from generated responses. Prior work studied whether a model can assess the correctness of its own answer while repeated generations reveal how stable its outputs are. Self-consistency measures agreement across sampled outputs , and semantic uncertainty groups responses by meaning before measuring their variation . Recent studies further examine the relationship between sample consistency and confidence calibration . However, these methods rely on different outputs and sampling procedures, which makes their uncertainty scores difficult to compare under a common evaluation setup. In recommendation, uncertainty has been used for several purposes. study predictive uncertainty in CTR-based recommendation through selective prediction, out-of-distribution detection, and active learning. construct recommendation sets with finite sample of false-discovery control, and use the list-distribution uncertainty to estimate recommendation performance without relevance labels. For LLM-based recommendation, decompose uncertainty into recommendation and prompt components. Other studies use uncertainty to change the recommendation process. GUIDER adjusts the reranking decisions according to uncertainty , and uncertainty-aware generative recommendation incorporates uncertainty into preference optimization and confidence learning . Unlike approaches that use uncertainty to modify the recommendation process, our benchmark evaluates heterogeneous UQ signals after the ranking has been issued, keeping the candidate order fixed and mapping every method to the same record-level output contract for direct comparison. Robustness research examines whether a recommender remains reliable when its data are sparse, imbalanced, noisy, or perturbed . Sparse user histories make it difficult to represent user preferences accurately . Also, popularity imbalance may reduce the visibility and recommendation quality of tail items . In LLM-based recommendation, these problems appear through the textual evidence included in the prompt because missing user history or item information can change both the resulting ranking and its uncertainty score. Input perturbations provide a direct way to study this sensitivity. measure how recommendation lists change after small perturbations to user interactions. Controlled testing provides a broader framework for evaluating model behavior. For example, CheckList uses targeted behavioral tests to reveal failures which the aggregate accuracy may hide . Moreover, recent LLM robustness work also examines the model stability under controlled prompt variations which preserve the original meaning . These studies suggest that results from one reference setting may not describe how a model behaves after its input evidence changes. Recommendation research has also examined which inputs contribute to uncertainty. Explainable Uncertainty Attribution measures how individual interactions in a user's history affect predictive uncertainty in sequential recommendation . Building on controlled robustness testing, our analysis focuses on two predefined evidence sources, user history and item content, and evaluates whether changes in UQ responses agree with the NDCG@10 differences associated with the two controls. This scoped evaluation differs from general source attribution and does not claim causal identification. Each method outputs a scalar uncertainty score aligned to the same base record and may additionally provide paired-control response scores for user-history and item-content evidence. The two controls are paired at the base-instance level: the same underlying recommendation case is evaluated under full versus truncated user history and under original versus ablated item content. We next describe the eight UQ signals used to produce these quantities. These signals span probability-based confidence, calibration, decomposition, perturbation sensitivity, and sampling . The resulting ranking-level signals may reflect both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, including ambiguity or incompleteness in user or item evidence and sensitivity to the model, prompt, or sampling procedure. We evaluate their observed monitoring behavior under a shared protocol without imposing an aleatoric--epistemic decomposition. All signals monitor the same fixed base ranking. Detailed score definitions, orientation conventions, and implementation parameters are provided in Appendix~. LabelProb-Max derives uncertainty from the maximum next-token probability over the valid candidate labels . LabelProb-Entropy measures dispersion over the same option-label distribution , while LabelProb-Margin measures ambiguity between the two most probable labels . Temperature-scaled LabelProb applies post-hoc scalar calibration to this distribution . LabelProb-Top10Mass derives uncertainty from the probability mass assigned to the fixed base top-10 set, thereby aligning the probability-mass cutoff with the monitored Error@10 event. Top10Mass combines the option-label distribution from a separate one-letter prompt with the issued listwise ranking, so it partly measures cross-elicitation consistency between the two elicitation formats. Appendix~ compares it with teacher-forced scores reconstructed under the original listwise ranking prompt. Decomposed LLM UQ follows and uses candidate-order variants to separate the recommendation and prompt uncertainty components. Self-consistency draws five stochastic rankings for each record and measures their top-10 disagreement, following prior work which uses output consistency as uncertainty evidence . Perturbation Rank Instability measures how strongly the fixed top-10 set changes under the constructed user-history and item-content controls, following perturbation-based behavioral evaluation . In addition to its scalar instability score, the method retains separate user-history and item-content responses for the paired-control evaluation. Beyond the base ranking, the four LabelProb signals share one forward pass; temperature scaling reuses this pass and adds an offline calibration fit. Decomposed LLM UQ uses five forward passes, self-consistency five sampled generations, and Perturbation Rank Instability 7.3 controlled greedy generations on average. The Llama evaluation reuses the controlled rankings generated for the robustness conditions . The overall score for each evaluation condition is computed by pooling all eligible records in that condition. Warm-start confidence intervals use 1,000 bootstrap resamples stratified by dataset and paired across methods. Sparse-history, Item-text robustness, and the tail-target subset use 200 paired bootstrap resamples stratified by dataset. For conditions containing multiple variants of the same base instance, those variants are resampled together. We present a fixed-ranking benchmark for uncertainty quantification in LLM-based recommendation, where UQ methods monitor the same issued rankings without changing the candidate order. Across four datasets, the benchmark evaluates ranking-failure detection, the stability of UQ method performance across backbones and evaluation conditions, and paired-control response agreement for user-history and item-content evidence. Our results show that no single UQ ordering is preserved across all settings. Top10Mass achieves the strongest pooled Error@10 detection performance under several Llama conditions, reflecting effective cutoff-aligned monitoring, while its advantage becomes smaller on the tail-target subset. The same Warm-start evaluation on Qwen produces a different method ordering. For paired controls, Perturbation Rank Instability provides stronger agreement with the relative clipped NDCG@10 differences than the scalar UQ signals; simple listwise distances also agree strongly with these clipped differences, supporting source-specific diagnosis. These results show that UQ quality is task-dependent. A scalar monitor that is effective for ranking-failure detection may not be the most informative for controlled evidence-response agreement. The practical monitoring claim is limited to Error@10 for a single observed positive item. A UQ monitor validated on the target backbone and recommendation condition can route high-uncertainty requests to a fallback recommender. Risk--coverage curves on held-out data can guide threshold selection. Risk@80 and AURC describe ranking errors among retained LLM outputs. An online A/B test of the complete routing policy would measure click-through and conversion rates. By holding the issued ranking fixed and aligning all methods to the same records, our benchmark makes these differences directly comparable without conflating monitoring quality with reranking behavior. This separation provides a common basis for evaluating UQ methods across recommendation conditions.
