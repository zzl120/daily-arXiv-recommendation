Unlike traditional models that generate rankings by learning latent preference representations from user-item interactions~, LLM-based agents introduce explicit reasoning into recommendation and leverage capabilities such as planning, memory, and tool use~ to reason over interaction patterns, item semantics, and contextual information. This shift moves recommender systems from conventional preference matching toward reasoning-driven and explainable decision-making, where recommendation quality increasingly depends on the reliability of agent reasoning. Existing research on agent-based recommendation can be broadly categorized into two paradigms~. Simulation-oriented methods~ employ user agents or interaction simulators to model behaviors and interaction dynamics, whereas recommendation-oriented methods leverage LLM-based agents to improve recommendation decisions through preference reasoning and candidate evaluation. Early recommendation-oriented methods~ typically rely on a single agent to perform the entire reasoning and decision-making process. However, recommendation decisions often depend on diverse information signals, including historical interaction patterns, item semantics, and contexts. Giving all these signals to a single agent can lead it to focus too heavily on some preference cues and overlook others, making it harder to fully model user preferences~. To address this limitation, recent studies have explored multi-agent recommendation frameworks~, which distribute recommendation reasoning across specialized agents to better capture heterogeneous preference signals. {MADRec_intro5.pdf} In existing multi-agent recommendation frameworks, different stages or subtasks of the recommendation process are typically assigned to multiple role-specific agents~. Each agent performs reasoning over different aspects of the recommendation task, and its intermediate reasoning is subsequently incorporated into downstream agents' reasoning contexts~. The downstream agent may rely on the previous agent's judgment without checking the candidate again. If the earlier judgment is wrong, the downstream agent may make further errors that can affect the final recommendation ranking~. As shown in Figure~, occurs when an agent's incorrect judgment influences other agents and affects the final recommendation ranking ~. Although some methods introduce review, reflection, or coordination components~, these mechanisms are typically applied after most reasoning steps have been completed. Consequently, they may fail to prevent unreliable intermediate reasoning from influencing downstream agents during the collaborative reasoning process. This motivates mechanisms that allow agents to review earlier judgments about candidates during collaboration and reduce the impact of errors on later rankings. Multi-agent debate offers a promising process-level mechanism for mitigating this issue. Through multi-round interactions, agents can compare and challenge each other's intermediate judgments, exposing potential inconsistencies and enabling iterative refinement~. This mechanism is particularly suitable for recommendation, where final decisions often require integrating multiple preference signals that reflect different aspects of user interests. Such diversity helps agents form complementary perspectives, enabling agents to cross-check intermediate judgments and expose potentially unreliable decisions. Recent studies have explored multi-agent debate in recommender systems~. However, using debate to reduce error propagation during collaborative ranking requires agents to reassess candidates based on each other's feedback and then review the revised rankings in later rounds to correct the positions of individual items~. In CRAVE's evaluated setting~, one-way critique allows the later agent to revise the recommendation after reviewing an earlier judgment, leaving the earlier agent no further turn to reconsider its judgment or check the revision. Debate4Rec~ uses debate to select a final recommendation list from several generated lists. However, selection alone cannot correct item-ranking errors shared by all available lists. Even if agents identify errors during debate, they may still rely on unsupported claims about item content or attributes when revising their judgments~. If other agents accept these claims, the resulting errors may affect later candidate assessments and ranking decisions. Reducing error propagation therefore requires both reciprocal checking and revision across agents and an assessment of whether item-level evidence supports the underlying semantic claims. Inspired by the above insights, we propose , an evidence-grounded multi-agent recommendation framework that aims to mitigate error propagation in collaborative recommendation reasoning. MADRec follows a two-stage retrieval-and-ranking pipeline, where the retrieval stage first retrieves a user-specific candidate set, and the ranking stage improves the reliability of agent reasoning through three coordinated modules: , , and . Specifically, introduces an and a that assess the same candidate set using interaction signals and item semantics, respectively. Through reciprocal feedback across debate rounds, each agent can challenge the other’s judgments about individual candidates and revise its own assessments and ranking in response to the critiques it receives. However, debate alone cannot guarantee that semantic judgments are sufficiently supported by item-level evidence. Therefore, grounds the 's reasoning in retrieved item-level evidence and refines candidate scores based on how well the evidence supports the semantic claims about each candidate. The two agents may still rank candidates differently after debate because they use different preference signals. Therefore, leverages reliability cues derived from agent interactions to calibrate interaction-based and semantic ranking outputs, producing a unified recommendation ranking. By integrating collaborative debate, evidence grounding, and reliability-aware calibration, MADRec reduces the impact of unreliable intermediate reasoning and improves the robustness of multi-agent recommendation. We have released the code and relevant parameter settings to facilitate repeatability as well as further research. Agent-based recommendation frameworks leverage the planning, reasoning, and task-execution capabilities of LLMs to support recommendation related tasks~. Existing studies can be broadly categorized into user simulation-oriented and recommendation task-oriented methods. The former employ LLM agents to simulate user behaviors, interaction dynamics, and preference evolution through profile, memory, and action modeling~, primarily supporting simulation and analysis rather than directly improving recommendation decisions. Recommendation-oriented methods directly employ LLM-based agents for recommendation. Early studies typically use a single agent for reasoning, planning, tool use, or reflection~. For example, InteRecAgent~ integrates memory and recommendation tools to support task planning and execution. RecMind~ explores agentic reasoning and tool use for recommendation, while ToolRec~ uses tools to retrieve and evaluate items. These methods enable an agent to gather information and refine recommendation decisions beyond a single prediction step. Multi-agent approaches further distribute recommendation reasoning across specialized roles or perspectives. MACRec~ employs a Manager to coordinate User/Item Analysts, a Reflector, and other agents for different recommendation tasks. The Reflector provides feedback that allows the Manager to revise previous outputs. MACF~ instantiates similar users and relevant items as agents and uses an orchestrator to organize multi-round collaboration, incorporating neighborhood-based collaborative signals into recommendation reasoning. These methods demonstrate the value of specialized analysis, iterative feedback, and agent coordination. Building on this direction, MADRec focuses on the reliability of intermediate candidate judgments exchanged between interaction-based and semantic perspectives. It combines reciprocal feedback over a shared candidate set with explicit claim--evidence support assessment and adaptive aggregation of the resulting rankings. Multi-agent debate enables LLM agents to examine and refine their judgments through argument exchange and mutual critique. Existing frameworks explore diverse perspectives, iterative feedback, and aggregation mechanisms to improve reasoning quality~. Recent studies have further extended this paradigm to recommendation tasks, where decisions depend on personalized user preferences and contextual information. CRAVE~ proposes an experience-augmented conversational recommendation framework that leverages verbalized experiences from historical dialogues and user feedback to guide a Debater-Critic process. However, its evaluated one-round configuration follows a unidirectional feedback process, preventing earlier debaters from revisiting their judgments in response to subsequent critiques. This asymmetric interaction may limit mutual error correction and make the resolution of erroneous judgments more dependent on downstream agents. EyeCRS~ employs supporting and opposing agents to identify historical dialogue information that may cause negative transfer. However, its debate focuses on evaluating historical dialogue information rather than directly refining candidate-level recommendation judgments. Debate4Rec~ addresses the trade-off between preference alignment and content diversity in generative recommendation, using debate-based feedback to select among generated recommendation lists. However, the selected list retains its original item order. If every candidate list ranks a relevant item too low, even the best list will retain that error. Correcting such an error requires agents to revisit their assessment of the item and revise its position. In contrast, MADRec focuses on the reliability of intermediate item-level judgments during recommendation.
