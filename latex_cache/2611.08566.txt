Running an expensive model on every request is costly, and for users with sparse interaction histories it often lowers quality. We present CascadeRec, a two-tier LLM reranking cascade deployed in production at a large financial planning and analysis SaaS provider. It serves content recommendations to enterprise tenants on AWS EKS and GKE. A lightweight Tier-1 model reranks every request. A router escalates only the requests where extra inference-time compute is likely to help, and sends them to a more expensive Tier-2 procedure . The system has run in a production A/B experiment for over a month. Compared with uniform use of the expensive tier, CascadeRec has reduced inference cost by about 10\ {https://github.com/GuilinDev/llm-cascade-reranking} Enterprise software now often includes LLM-powered recommendation. A typical task is to surface the right report, driver analysis, or planning artifact to an analyst at the right time. Our production setting is a multi-tenant financial planning and analysis SaaS platform. Each tenant receives personalized content recommendations. Two operational facts shape the design. First, LLM inference is the largest marginal cost of the feature: reranking every request with a large model costs an order of magnitude more than with a small one. Second, tenants differ in history depth, request volume, and content complexity. A single reranker for everyone either overspends on easy requests or underserves hard ones. This paper describes , an adaptive compute-allocation system deployed in our production recommendation path. A lightweight Tier-1 LLM reranks every request. A router then decides, per request, whether to escalate to an expensive Tier-2 procedure . The system has served live tenant traffic behind an A/B experiment for over a month on AWS EKS and GKE. Models are served through Amazon Bedrock and vLLM, and the system is moving to full rollout. The design also rests on an empirical observation: . Our platform serves enterprise FP\&A teams. Tenants work with dashboards, reports, planning models, and analytical narratives. The recommendation surface proposes content items that match the analyst's current intent and workspace state. Request traffic is modest in aggregate , but each request is high-value. A surfaced insight can influence a planning decision, so ranking quality matters more than raw QPS. Three properties set this setting apart from consumer-scale recommendation. Interaction histories per user range from a handful of events to rich multi-quarter trails. Cold-start is the norm for a meaningful share of traffic, not an edge case. Request volume is highly skewed across tenants: a small fraction of tenants accounts for the majority of daily requests. With uniformly expensive reranking, a few heavy tenants dominate GPU spend. During their bursts, latency degrades for everyone else. This imbalance was a primary motivation for per-request compute allocation. Tier-2 inference costs roughly 3--6$ $ Tier-1 per request in tokens and latency. At enterprise SLAs, that spend is hard to justify on every request unless it helps on every request. As we show, it does not. Three features of this surface favor a language model over a ranker trained per tenant. First, the items are documents. Reports, narratives, and planning models carry titles, descriptions, and structure that an LLM can read directly, while a collaborative model sees only item IDs. Second, per-tenant interaction logs are small and catalogs grow as tenants add content. A supervised ranker per tenant would often lack training data and would need frequent retraining. Third, a zero-shot model can take new context as text without retraining. Our deployment serves zero-shot models only, for cost, governance, and model-management reasons . The offline study in Section~ shows the limits of this choice. When item text is short and history is thin, the LLM has less to work with than a collaborative-filtering retriever. First-stage retrieval is implicit ALS~ with fold-in, so truncated users get fresh embeddings from exactly their $k$ visible interactions. Three directions follow for practitioners. Preference summaries extracted from tenant context, or item text richer than titles, change what the model knows. Repeated sampling does not. As a semantic candidate generator or a feature extractor for the retriever, the LLM adds signal instead of reordering a list it cannot evaluate well. Rerankers that learn collaborative patterns during training may avoid the zero-shot degradation. The cost is training pipelines and per-domain model management, which many enterprise settings cannot justify today. A small supervised reranker is the natural baseline for this direction, and we leave that comparison to future work. On these public benchmarks, no cascade configuration exceeds the CF baseline in aggregate. The offline value of the cascade is to limit the harm of uniform reranking. On MovieLens its best configurations are statistically indistinguishable from B0 and keep the warm-segment gains, while spending $40$--$50\ This section collects lessons from development, deployment, and the ongoing move from A/B to full rollout. We expect them to apply to any team that puts LLM inference into a ranking path. Inter-tenant load imbalance was a decisive driver. A small number of tenants generate most requests, and uniform expensive reranking turns their bursts into fleet-wide latency and cost spikes. Per-request compute allocation therefore acts as a quality policy and as an implicit load-management mechanism. We started this project from the intuition that cold users need more compute. On one of our two offline domains, the opposite held. On Amazon, escalating sparse-history users made their loss larger, while the only users who gained from LLM reranking anywhere were in the longest-history MovieLens bin. Had we shipped the $5$ cold-escalation policy to a domain like Amazon, the cascade would have spent its extra compute on the users it was hurting. A router encodes a hypothesis about extra inference helps. That hypothesis must be checked per segment and per domain, from measured deltas, before it controls production traffic. Routers should therefore be cheap to flip and cheap to disable, rather than fixed in the architecture. Aggregate metrics would have approved uniform reranking, and per-segment analysis reversed that decision. Our rollout gates now require per-segment deltas, not just aggregates. Full rollout proceeds cohort by cohort as each tenant cohort clears its gate. LLMs have been used as zero-shot rankers~, pairwise rankers~, two-stage rerankers~, instruction-tuned rerankers~, and conversational agents~. LLMs also orchestrate constraint-compliant multi-agent recommendation~, and the robustness of LLM-generated recommendation explanations has been evaluated~. Surveys cover the space~. Evaluation is dominated by aggregate metrics. Recent work documents cold-start biases of LLM rerankers~. It also shows that LLMs underperform matrix factorization when collaborative signal reaches the model only through the prompt~, which independently supports the cause we identify. Fine-tuned rerankers that learn collaborative patterns~ may avoid the zero-shot degradation we observe. Our deployment is limited to zero-shot serving , which is a common enterprise constraint. FrugalGPT~ introduced cascaded model selection. RouteLLM~, BEST-Route~, Route-to-Reason~, and budget-aware cascades~ extend it. These methods route on query difficulty. Our setting routes on and, operationally, on tenant load. Test-time scaling gains are known to concentrate in reasoning-heavy tasks~, and our results are an instance of that boundary in recommendation. Even in mathematical reasoning, measured gains depend on how the token budget is enforced and reported~. Classical approaches use content features, meta-learning, or contrastive methods~. LLM personalization under limited context includes LaMP~ and multi-path personalized reasoning~.
