At each evaluated node, uses an LLM-based reflection and mutation procedure to inform subsequent candidate generation. After evaluating a configuration, the reflection prompt provides the model with the evaluated configuration, its observed retrieval scores, the branch trajectory leading to the node, and the rationale associated with the proposed configuration. For non-root nodes, the trajectory includes the parent configuration and reward together with the observed parent-relative outcome; root nodes are treated as initial expansions. The reflection prompt follows the template: You are evaluating an IR retrieval configuration for a search optimization experiment. \\ \{embedding\}, \{chunker\}, $ =\{ \}$, $k=\{k\}$, \{reranker\}. \\ \{metric values\}. \\ \{branch trajectory and prior critiques\}. \\ \{candidate-generation rationale\}. \\ Write exactly two sentences: summarize what the scores suggest about the configuration, and suggest what the next expansion should try or avoid. The resulting reflection is stored with the search node and can be reused as context during later candidate generation. Mutation prompts condition on the UCT-selected branch, previous evaluations, the active search phase, and the valid choices or bounds for that phase. The shared context includes: \{selected root-to-leaf path with reflections\}. \\ \{previously evaluated configurations and scores\}. \\ \{summary of prior outcomes\}. \\ \{optimization objective\}. \\ \{target reward\}. Modern retrieval-augmented generation systems rely on retrieval pipelines whose effectiveness is shaped by interdependent design choices . These include how documents are chunked, which dense encoder is used, whether sparse and dense scores are fused, which reranker is applied, and how numerical parameters such as fusion weight and retrieval depth are set. A chunking strategy changes the distribution of candidate passages seen by the embedder; reranking can compensate for some retrieval errors while amplifying others; and the best retrieval depth depends on both the underlying corpus and the evaluation objective . As a result, RAG pipeline optimization is better viewed as a structured configuration-search problem than as isolated tuning of individual hyperparameters. Existing automated optimization methods reduce manual tuning burden , but general-purpose black-box optimization does not explicitly exploit the macro--micro structure of retrieval pipeline decisions. Recent work on RAG hyperparameter optimization demonstrates the value of structured search in large configuration spaces . However, existing approaches do not explicitly organize optimization as a hierarchical search trajectory in which structural and numerical decisions are explored at different stages and prior outcomes inform subsequent proposals. Moreover, many hyperparameter optimization algorithms provide limited visibility into the optimization process . For IR pipelines, inefficient exploration can also incur substantial systems costs when structural changes require constructing or loading new indices. Starting optimization from scratch for each new corpus further discards potentially useful information from prior retrieval tasks.
