These observations motivate a fundamental question: what positional encoding and interaction granularity does CTR prediction actually require? To address this question, we propose , which combines target-aware rank--time positional encoding with temporal-group interaction modeling. Specifically, Factorized Rank Encoding represents each behavior's ordinal position within the historical sequence through a low-rank factorization, while Target-Relative Time Encoding characterizes its temporal distance to the target on a coarse-to-fine scale. By modeling heterogeneous feature interactions and historical behavior sequences, CTR prediction provides engagement estimates that guide candidate ranking and exposure~. CTR models are widely used in e-commerce recommendation~, personalized content delivery~, and online advertising~. These applications support personalized information access and discovery on the Web~. Transformer-based modeling has achieved substantial progress and become an important paradigm for CTR prediction~. Recent approaches unify feature interaction and behavior-sequence modeling within shared Transformer architectures~. Current methods typically inherit index-based positional encodings and token-level causal attention from language modeling~. Positional encodings represent behaviors by their physical indices in the input sequence, while causal attention restricts historical interactions to preceding context. For example, OneTrans~ processes historical behaviors and non-sequential features as a unified token sequence with learned absolute positional embeddings through stacked causal Transformer blocks. As illustrated in Figure~ , historical behaviors in CTR prediction have three positional views: sequence rank, event timestamp, and target-relative time. Sequence rank characterizes a behavior's ordinal context, whereas its timestamp specifies when it occurred. For a fixed history, ranks and timestamps remain unchanged across prediction requests, but the target-relative coordinates change from $T_1-t_i$ to $T_2-t_i$ with the prediction timestamp. Moreover, the same rank can correspond to different target-relative times, while temporally adjacent behaviors can occupy distinct ordinal positions. Thus, effective CTR positional modeling should jointly capture behavioral order, temporal information, and target-relative context, rather than relying on any single positional signal alone. As illustrated in Figure~ , the same history is partitioned into different temporal groups as the prediction time changes, and is thus modeled at different scales for different targets. Within a group, behaviors mutually enrich one another to characterize the user's interest during that period, while successive groups reveal how interests evolve toward the current request. Event-level causal interaction limits this within-group enrichment, whereas global bidirectional interaction removes the directed order across groups. In CTR prediction, sequence rank provides an item's ordinal context, which elapsed time alone cannot fully characterize under irregular user activity. Since the predictive role of a historical behavior can vary across sequence positions, we adopt learnable rank embeddings that allow CTR supervision to determine position-specific representations without imposing a predefined positional geometry. Interestingly, we observe that these learned embeddings exhibit an approximately low-rank structure. As shown in Figure~, reconstructing them with only a few principal directions largely preserves prediction performance. Sequence rank captures behavioral order but overlooks temporal intervals, while event timestamps alone do not explicitly characterize temporal relationships to the current prediction target. Moreover, the temporal relevance of a historical behavior varies with the current target, even when the history remains unchanged. Therefore, we propose TRTE to encode each behavior's temporal position relative to the target. Temporal distinctions are not equally informative across the history: recent behaviors often reflect immediate interests and benefit from finer temporal resolution, whereas distant behaviors can be modeled at coarser scales. We therefore adopt a fine-grained logarithmic time scale to preserve detailed temporal distinctions near the target while compactly representing the distant past. Positional encodings represent sequence indices, event time, target relevance, or joint order--time relationships~. Index-based learned or rotary encodings remain common~. Other methods incorporate event timing or intervals to account for irregular behavior histories~. For example, T-RoPE~ uses multiscale timestamp rotations with prediction-time query alignment, while TO-RoPE~ combines index and time rotations within shared or separate rotary subspaces. In addition, context-aware CAPE~ constructs soft positions from target--history item dissimilarity, expressing item relevance rather than elapsed time. TRIP combines task-adaptive rank representations with prediction-time-anchored temporal encoding and target-relative temporal grouping. In this work, we found that existing positional encodings do not consistently improve CTR prediction, while bidirectional interaction within target-relative temporal groups can outperform both token-level causal and global bidirectional attention. These observations reveal the need to reconsider both historical position representations and interaction granularity. Based on these findings, we proposed TRIP, which jointly models sequence rank, event time, and the prediction-time reference, and organizes history into target-relative temporal groups with intra-group bidirectional and inter-group causal interaction. Experiments on four public datasets demonstrate TRIP's effectiveness, while positional-encoding transfer experiments across three unified backbones validate the portability of its rank--time design. Detailed ablations and representation analyses further support the component designs. We implement all models in PyTorch and train each run on one NVIDIA H20 GPU. TRIP and all baselines share AdamW, a fixed learning rate of $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, training/evaluation batch sizes of 1024/2048, and 64-dimensional sparse feature embeddings. All models are trained for one epoch with BF16 mixed precision and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. This single-pass protocol limits the risk of overfitting sparse embeddings through repeated exposure to the same impressions. Hyperparameters are selected using only training and validation data, and checkpoints are selected by validation AUC before final test evaluation. FRE benefits from sufficient rank-specific capacity, but larger $K$ is not uniformly better. On TAAC, mean AUC rises from 0.6095 at $K=1$ to 0.6340 at $K=10$, then declines at $K=16$. KuaiRec is much less sensitive, with AUC ranging from 0.8502 to 0.8508 across the tested widths; on KuaiRand, $K=2$ already approaches the best result. These differences favor a compact, dataset-adaptive factorization: shared basis vectors capture useful ordinal structure, while the required capacity for distinguishing ranks varies across histories. Finer target-relative coordinates improve temporal discrimination on KuaiRand: increasing $m$ from 1 to 8 raises mean AUC from 0.7711 to 0.7781. In contrast, TAAC and TaobaoAd attain their best results at $m=1$, and KuaiRec shows only small fluctuations. Thus, increasing resolution is useful when finer distinctions help history reading, but does not yield universal gains. All four datasets achieve their highest observed AUC with nonzero $ _{}$. The peaks occur at 0.5 on KuaiRec, TAAC, and TaobaoAd, and at 0.75 on KuaiRand. Further increasing the penalty brings no consistent benefit, indicating that occupancy should be corrected without excessively suppressing dense but potentially informative history. The shared-offset scale has a comparatively small effect: the AUC range across its tested values is below 0.001 on every dataset. Rank-specific variation is more consequential, particularly on TAAC and KuaiRand. Setting the configured rank-branch scale to zero yields AUCs of 0.6068 and 0.7669, compared with 0.6340 and 0.7777 at configuration scale one. Unlike a shared offset, the rank-specific branch distinguishes ordinal contexts. These results support FRE's separation of shared structure and rank-dependent variation, with the latter providing the essential positional distinctions on these datasets.
