Instead of scoring items directly across a massive corpus, GR represents each item as a sequence of discrete tokens, known as a Semantic ID , and casts retrieval as a sequence generation task~. Despite its effectiveness, GR struggles with the highly skewed interaction distributions typical of real-world platforms. A small fraction of popular items receives abundant supervision, whereas the vast majority of tail items suffer from sparse interactions and remain difficult to retrieve~. This challenge is particularly acute in industrial applications, where expanding the beam size to uncover more long-tail candidates directly inflates serving costs~. Effective long-tail retrieval therefore demands both superior recommendation accuracy and the efficient allocation of a constrained search budget. Existing research on long-tail GR primarily focuses on the training stage, investigating how tail items are represented and learned. Recent studies attribute popularity bias to the under-optimization of tail SID tokens, redesigning training objectives and tokenization schemes accordingly~. Other approaches enhance tail and cold-start generalization through adaptive SID encoding and knowledge transfer~. While these methods improve the model's capacity to encode tail items, they treat representation learning in isolation and rely on a rigid inference pipeline of standard beam search followed by exact SID matching. Consequently, a fundamental question remains unexplored: across the complete pipeline of SID construction, decoding, and matching, how can learned tail signals survive to achieve final retrieval? In generative recommendation, retrieving an item from the catalog spans three stages, namely offline Semantic ID construction, online beam search decoding, and downstream candidate matching. Existing studies on long-tail generative recommendation primarily focus on improving tokenization or training objectives during the training stage while keeping the inference pipeline fixed. Before introducing our proposed method, we first conduct controlled analyses across all three stages in this section to investigate where long-tail items are lost along the retrieval pipeline and motivate our framework design. In standard generative recommendation, an offline tokenizer maps each item into a fixed-length sequence of discrete tokens known as a Semantic ID~. During online serving, an autoregressive model predicts next-token probabilities step by step from the user history, while beam search retains the most probable prefix paths at each step until a set of complete token sequences is generated~. Finally, the system performs exact matching and returns only those items whose complete codes appear in the generated set. Partial matching can recover a target item only if at least one sequence within $r$ mismatches survives beam search. Under a small beam size $B$, however, standard beam search prunes most tail prefixes early on. We therefore freeze the base recommender and add two guidance signals during beam search. In standard decoding, popular items appear frequently in training data and receive much higher transition probabilities. As a result, head prefixes quickly fill up the narrow beam and push tail prefixes out. To reduce this bias, we estimate token transition priors from training frequencies and subtract the scaled log-prior from the model logits before softmax~. This penalty lowers the scores of overly popular branches and leaves room in the beam for tail prefixes. Debiasing popularity alone is not enough. If we only penalize popular branches, the beam will keep random tail prefixes that do not match the user's interest. We therefore add a user-semantic score to identify which tail branches are actually relevant to the user. Specifically, a linear layer maps the pooled encoder output into a normalized user vector, which is trained with cross-entropy loss to align with the normalized text vector of the target item~.
