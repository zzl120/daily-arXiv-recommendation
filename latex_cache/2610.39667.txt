Information retrieval-based methods extract a pivotal subgraph from the entire KB to locate candidate answers and then evaluate their plausibility through semantic matching with the question. However, we observed that the extracted subgraphs often include nodes that are weakly related or irrelevant to the question. Without a proper node filtering mechanism, the number of irrelevant nodes grows as the number of hops increases, leading to excessive consumption of computational resources. To address these challenges, this study introduces an efficient LLM-based subgraph retrieval method for multi-hop knowledge base question answering, M-ER. The framework leverages Monte Carlo Tree Search to transform subgraph exploration into a tree-structured search process. During the MCTS selection phase, nodes that are highly relevant to the question are prioritized for inclusion in the subgraph, eliminating the need to traverse all nodes in the KB. The framework further incorporates a large language model to refine the search direction, ensuring that exploration remains focused on nodes relevant to the question. In addition, selected nodes are quantitatively scored, and these scores are fed back into the node selection process to effectively filter out irrelevant candidates, thereby improving the quality of the subgraph. This mechanism not only narrows the search space but also enhances the overall efficiency of multi-hop KBQA. The performance of subgraph extraction is mainly influenced by two factors: subgraph construction quality and answer coverage rate. Subgraph construction directly affects the efficiency and accuracy of the reasoning process, as reasoning paths are formed by expanding nodes and relations within the subgraph. The answer coverage rate defines the upper bound of the performance . Current subgraph extraction methods can be categorized into two main approaches: the k-hop neighbor method and the scoring-based method. The k-hop neighbor approach constructs subgraphs by retrieving a fixed number of hops around the topic entity without requiring additional search heuristics. However, KBQA is sensitive to subgraph size, since a small subgraph may exclude correct answers, while a large one may introduce excessive noise. Scoring-based methods perform KBQA by quantifying the semantic association between the question and KB elements. NSM adopts a teacher-student framework with bidirectional reasoning to generate intermediate supervision signals for answer extraction. SR introduces a trainable subgraph retriever decoupled from inference, and generates accurate question-related subgraphs through weakly supervised pre-training and end-to-end fine-tuning. When combined with NSM , this approach sets a new performance benchmark. Existing scoring-based methods typically construct subgraphs by computing semantic similarity between the question and knowledge base elements , as shown in Figure~ . Representative methods such as SR use language models to compute semantic similarity. However, when a single entity has relations corresponding to diverse attributes, these methods may retain relations that are weakly related or irrelevant to the question, expanding the search space and reducing answer accuracy. Although effective in principle, this approach relies on global answer matching over the entire KB, which introduces substantial noise and increases the overall computational burden. MCTS provides a more targeted exploration strategy . By simulating future states through random sampling, it incrementally constructs a search tree where the nodes correspond to possible reasoning steps within the subgraph. This strategy focuses computation on the most promising nodes, reducing the need for exhaustive enumeration. By balancing exploration and exploitation, MCTS narrows the scope of the search and improves computational efficiency . Extending this idea, KBQA-o1 leverages MCTS to balance exploration quality and search range, while also adopting incremental fine-tuning to reduce reliance on labeled data. Its limitation lies in disregarding the structural and semantic context of the knowledge graph, making it vulnerable to cascading errors from incorrect intermediate predictions. MCTS-KBQA introduces a task-specific scoring function to improve answer selection. However, it lacks explicit guidance for selecting subsequent search directions and does not fully exploit the structural information within subgraphs. Consequently, it often terminates at intermediate states and treats them as final answers, leading to lower accuracy and unstable performance. In addition, MCTS often struggles in scenarios where a single entity is connected to relations corresponding to diverse attributes, as weakly related or irrelevant nodes may be retained during exploration. This reduces the model’s ability to discriminate answer-relevant semantics, leading to the inclusion of noisy entities and a degradation in overall performance. Therefore, M-ER is designed to incorporate MCTS into KBQA while explicitly filtering irrelevant relations. Large language models , on the other hand, exhibit strong contextual reasoning capabilities and can guide node selection toward answer-relevant directions . Therefore, this study proposes an efficient LLM-based subgraph retrieval method for multi-hop knowledge base question answering , which integrates the semantic reasoning capabilities of LLMs with the global search efficiency of MCTS, as shown in Figure . This approach jointly activates model capabilities from two perspectives: internal and external . Specifically, LLMs interpret the question to predict the next search direction, which is then matched with candidate nodes based on semantic similarity. Meanwhile, selected nodes are evaluated based on their contribution to the final answer, and these scores are used to refine the reflection process, thus improving the alignment between the model’s internal knowledge and external information. This mechanism opens new avenues for KBQA. We review subgraph retrieval methods in KBQA from two aspects: the k-hop neighbor method and the scoring-based method. The k-hop neighbor method can extract subgraphs in two ways. The k-hop neighbors of the topic entity are selected directly as a subgraph. The other method first computes similarity between the question and all relations. It then selects the most relevant relations to construct the initial 1-hop subgraph, which is further expanded to form the 2-hop subgraph. However, this rigid hop-based selection may exclude correct answers when the hop size is too small, while introducing substantial noise when the hop size is too large. The methods perform KBQA by quantifying the semantic association between the question and the KB elements. The key is to design a similarity scoring function to screen the key information. The early key-value memory network stores context as key–value pairs, enabling the model to learn how keys can be used to retrieve values relevant to the question. However, this method is limited by hardware memory. With the development of end-to-end neural networks, GRAFT-Net retrieves the nodes and relations related to the question in the KB to construct a subgraph containing high-probability answers. Node representations are then learned over the constructed subgraph, based on which answer candidates are inferred. This method lacks supervision and thus cannot constrain the reasoning process. To address this limitation, some studies propose improvements, such as PullNet , SR , FC-KBQA , CBR-SUBG , and Query Graph Ranking . However, these methods primarily focus on the similarity between the question and the KB, without considering whether the retrieved nodes actually contribute to answering the questions. As a result, irrelevant information that is similar to the question is introduced, leading to subgraph redundancy and unnecessary consumption of computational resources. The advent of LLMs has greatly improved the reliability of KBQA performance . The LLM-based KBQA methods can generally be categorized into several types. The first category adopts the generation-retrieval paradigm, in which LLM generates the initial answer or logical rules, and then refines it using entity/relation retrieval or document verification . ChatKBQA employs an innovative generate-then-retrieve approach. A fine-tuned LLM first produces a skeleton of a logical form, which is then aligned with the knowledge graph via unsupervised retrieval of entities and relations. RoG , on the other hand, integrates structural knowledge from KGs through its planning–retrieval–reasoning framework, generating KG-grounded relation paths as faithful plans for interpretable reasoning. Although these methods reduce dependence on explicit semantic parsers, they remain sensitive to retrieval noise and incur high computational costs. The second category focuses on question decomposition and step-by-step reasoning , thereby enhancing interpretability. GRV-KBQA adopts a three-stage framework that decouples logical structure generation, semantic grounding, and structure-aware verification. It first decomposes a complex query into a chain of subquery templates and then fills these templates to produce executable logical forms for question answering. However, such decomposition methods are limited when relevant information exceeds the predefined query scope. Other methods, such as RGR-KBQA , follow a “retrieve–generate–retrieve” three-stage framework. In this approach, cosine similarity is used to extract the most relevant relation chains, which enhances semantic understanding and improves generation accuracy. Nonetheless, most of these methods are heavily dependent on similarity metrics and prompt engineering, which introduces substantial noise and hampers overall performance. Currently, LLM-based KBQA focuses mainly on how to make full use of the inherent knowledge and the strong reasoning ability of LLM in a large KB, while ignoring the role of subgraphs and efficient search strategies.
