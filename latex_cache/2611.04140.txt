Recommender systems have undergone a significant transformation in how they represent and identify items, evolving from raw IDs as simple lookup keys to semantic IDs as structured interfaces for utilizing and sharing information across recommendation settings. We start with raw IDs. For much of the past two decades, item identity in industrial systems was anchored in discrete and arbitrary integer identifiers . While these identifiers carry no semantic information about the items, they enabled exact lookup, logging, and item-specific memorization at scale~. These raw IDs resolved non-trivial challenges in information utilization in recommendation, such as architectural stability, cross-component consistency, and high-concurrency serving . However, they also come with fundamental limitations: raw IDs provide no identity-level transfer across semantically similar items, leaving cold-start unsupported at the identity level and embedding tables growing unboundedly with the catalog. To capture richer item semantics and mitigate cold-start, an important line of work has explored incorporating auxiliary item information into recommendation models. Often referred to as content-based or multimodal recommender systems, the core idea is to derive item representations from both user-item interactions and item content~, which may come in the form of text, images, graphs, audio, or videos. This auxiliary information complements collaborative signals in capturing how items are consumed together, while enabling the derivation of representations for new items at inference time, effectively addressing cold-start and bridging the gap across domains. Despite these undoubted benefits, significant challenges remain under the raw-ID setting: embedding tables grow prohibitively large with item vocabulary size~, recommendation architectures become increasingly complex to accommodate various modalities~ {DBLP:journals/csur/LiuHXZGWLT25}, and modality-specific designs limit the usability of new or different auxiliary information in existing model settings~. While auxiliary information has significantly advanced recommender systems, it remains peripheral to item identity itself. An emerging paradigm shifts this boundary: rather than treating semantic information solely as an auxiliary input, semantic IDs encode part of that information directly into the item identifier. Concretely, items are represented as discrete token sequences, typically obtained through vector quantization or clustering methods, such as RQ-VAE and RQ-KMeans~. This turns item identity into a structured, model-facing object that can reflect content, hierarchy, relationships, and behavioral signals, enabling cross-item generalization while preserving the lookup and memorization properties that made raw IDs indispensable in practice. In addition to reviewing the main methods for deriving semantic IDs in the recommendation context, we discuss the key design choices behind semantic ID construction, including what information is quantized, how token vocabularies are organized, and how to balance compactness, stability, semantic coherence, and downstream usability. Originally developed for computer vision and approximate nearest neighbor search, semantic IDs have since been increasingly adopted in recommender systems. Their most prominent application is perhaps Generative recommendation, where the model learns to generate the semantic ID of the next item, a direction that is extensively explored, establishing semantic IDs as a core building block of generative recommendation~. Semantic IDs are also being leveraged as structured input features in discriminative ranking pipelines, with successful deployments at Google~, Kuaishou~, and Meta~ demonstrating that structured representations improve cold-start performance and embedding stability in this context as well. We deep-dive into TIGER~ and the YouTube ranking model~ as two representative use cases of semantic IDs across generative and discriminative settings. The focus is on how Semantic IDs support scalability, generalization, and mitigate cold-start. Semantic IDs are mainly derived from item content, which may come in various forms including text, images, audio, and video. This naturally raises the question of multimodality: This tutorial examines existing work along two emerging families of methods, namely early fusion and late fusion~, discussing their respective trade-offs in terms of complexity, flexibility, representational quality, and point out some potential limitations such as redundancy~. Beyond content, recent work has also established the importance of collaborative signals, capturing how users interact with items, for recommendation accuracy. We cover the main approaches to incorporating such signals into semantic IDs, including collaborative embedding quantization~, content model/embedding fine-tuning~, and contrastive training on collaborative data~. This part highlights a central design tension: semantic IDs should be meaningful enough to reflect item content, yet recommendation-aware enough to capture user interaction patterns in practice. Cross-domain recommendation presents a challenging setting where users and items may have little or no interaction history in a target domain. Traditional approaches rely on explicit source-target alignment, introducing model complexity that becomes intractable as the number of target domains grows ~. We briefly review the representative methods in this context ~, and highlight their remaining limitations. We then turn to Semantic IDs, which offer a more principled alternative: shared semantic structure across domains emerges naturally from the quantization process, enabling seamless and scalable cross-domain transfer without explicit alignment. We examine emerging methods in this space~ and show how this paradigm directly addresses the limitations of conventional approaches. We close by discussing emerging directions. One is cross-task unification, where semantic IDs provide shared representations across recommendation and search~. Another is semantic planning, where semantic IDs support reasoning about what the next exposure should accomplish under user, platform, and provider objectives. We also discuss open challenges including ID stability under catalog dynamics and evaluation beyond hit-rate metrics. This is a hands-on tutorial, whereby lectures are complemented by two practical sessions. In the first, attendees train RQ-KMeans to derive semantic IDs, inspect the resulting token structure, and compare it against a raw ID-based baseline on a generative recommendation task. In the second, attendees investigate the architecture proposed in~, leveraging semantic IDs in a multi-domain setting, and assess their cross-domain transfer capabilities. Both sessions use the Amazon Reviews dataset~, with all instructions and code provided as self-contained Jupyter notebooks that run on free-tier resources such as Google Colab. To keep the sessions focused on semantic IDs, we leverage Cornac's~ generative recommendation model implementations, and pre-compute content-based item embeddings and baseline models when necessary.
