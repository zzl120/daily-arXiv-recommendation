We introduced , an automated benchmark construction framework that leverages hierarchical semantic structure to generate realistic queries and graded relevance sets without manual annotation. Using an RQ-VAE with attribute exclusivity supervision, FASHION-RQ produces coherent fine-grained clusters and exploits parent--sibling relations to construct diverse retrieval scenarios. We instantiate this framework as , a benchmark for evaluating fashion retrieval models through complementary retrieval tasks, weighted query-relevance evaluation, and attribute-level metrics. Together, FASHION-RQ and FASHION-RQBENCH provide a scalable and semantically grounded alternative to manually curated fashion retrieval benchmarks. FASHION-RQ depends on catalog quality, the underlying multimodal representation, and LLM-generated queries, which may introduce coverage and linguistic biases. The automatically derived relevance structure also approximates rather than replaces human judgments. Future work will explore human validation, broader domains and attributes, richer semantic relations, and adaptive benchmark generation. Fashion retrieval involves fine-grained attributes such as product type, color, pattern, material, occasion, season, and price. Existing resources address different aspects of this problem. DeepFashion~ and DeepFashion2~ provide large-scale benchmarks for fashion recognition, detection, segmentation, and retrieval; Fashion200K~ and Shopping100k~ support attribute-based search; FACAD~ focuses on fashion captioning; and Fashion Product Images ~ provides the catalog schema used in our work. Language-conditioned retrieval is studied primarily through composed image retrieval, including FashionIQ~, and more general benchmarks such as CIRR~, CIRCO~, and LaSCo~, with fashion-oriented encoders including FashionCLIP~, Marqo-FashionSigLIP ~, and GCL~. Most existing fashion benchmarks use relevance, whereas open-ended product queries can correspond to a set of valid items; CIRCO~ is a notable exception, but at modest scale. Graded set-valued relevance is available in product search: ESCI~, for example, provides exact, substitute, complement, and irrelevant labels, but requires 2.6M manual judgments for 130K queries. Manual construction also fixes the query distribution and catalog snapshot, making large-scale regeneration costly. Outside fashion, QUEST~ automatically constructs set-valued queries from Wikipedia categories, but relies on an existing taxonomy. In contrast, FASHION-RQ derives both relevance sets and queries from the catalog's learned semantic structure, avoiding manual relevance annotation and predefined taxonomies. Generative retrieval directly decodes item identifiers from queries. DSI~ and NCI~ established this paradigm, with hierarchical identifier construction shown to be effective. Subsequent approaches learn identifiers through discrete auto-encoding ~ or residual quantization for relevance-aware, prefix-scorable representations~. In recommendation, TIGER~ represents items using SIDs produced by an RQ-VAE~; its coarse-to-fine codebooks induce a prefix hierarchy, while Singh~ .\ ~ demonstrate benefits for cold-start and long-tail recommendation. Recent work explicitly shapes this hierarchy. HiD-VAE~ supervises quantization levels with hierarchical item tags and introduces a uniqueness objective to reduce code collisions. These approaches, however, use semantic identifiers primarily as representations for downstream retrieval or recommendation. FASHION-RQ instead repurposes the hierarchy as an : prefixes define product groups at different granularities and shared-prefix length provides graded relevance. We further introduce level-wise attribute supervision with an to encourage semantically coherent clusters, using the resulting tree to construct the benchmark rather than the retrieval identifiers themselves. Synthetic query generation reduces the cost of manual annotation. Doc2Query~ generates queries for documents, while InPars~ and Promptagator ~ use LLM-generated query--document pairs for retrieval training. These approaches are primarily : a query is generated from an individual document and typically inherits a single positive target. Rahmani~ .\ ~ extend synthetic generation to evaluation, using LLM-generated queries and judgments to reproduce retrieval rankings, but the resulting relevance labels remain pairwise LLM assessments. BEIR~, in contrast, aggregates existing manually annotated datasets, while ExcluIR~ focuses on phenomenon-specific retrieval with manually constructed queries. FASHION-RQ uses generation: queries are generated from a purified cluster's attribute distribution together with its relational differences from parent and sibling clusters. The cluster itself therefore defines a multi-item relevance set, while the relational context encourages queries that distinguish the target group from nearby alternatives. The LLM only verbalizes catalog-derived evidence; it does not assign relevance, keeping the resulting labels independent of the generator. Graded metrics such as nDCG~ require relevance judgments, motivating LLM-based assessors such as UMBRELA ~. Such assessors produce holistic query--item scores and therefore do not reveal which attributes are satisfied or violated. Attribute-level evaluation has been explored through attribute-specific retrieval metrics~, conditional similarity~, compositionality benchmarks ~, and constraint-satisfaction measures~. However, these settings either evaluate individual attributes, use curated distractors, or operate over restricted constraint types rather than decomposing relevance over a ranked product catalog. FASHION-RQ combines set-valued graded relevance with joint attribute-level evaluation. Our weighted query-relevance score and per-attribute metrics are computed from the structured query representation $ $, separating overall intent satisfaction from fine-grained attribute satisfaction. Because relevance is inherited deterministically from the semantic-ID hierarchy and attribute scores use a fixed catalog schema, the evaluation is reproducible and inspectable without per-pair human or LLM judgments. Fashion retrieval is inherently and . A query such as ``lightweight cotton kurtas for summer'' constrains multiple attributes, including product type, material, and season, and many catalog items may satisfy these constraints to different degrees. Relevance is therefore compositional: an item may match some attributes while violating others. A single aggregate ranking score can hide these differences. For example, two systems may achieve similar nDCG while one retrieves the correct garment in the wrong color and the other retrieves the correct color for the wrong garment. Evaluating such systems requires relevance labels that are set-valued, graded, and decomposable by attribute. Existing fashion benchmarks do not provide these properties. Manual fashion retrieval datasets typically associate a query with a single target item~, penalizing other valid results. Graded set-valued annotations are possible, but expensive: ESCI contains 2.6M judgments for 130K queries~. Moreover, manually collected labels are tied to a particular catalog snapshot and query distribution. Automatic assessment using language models~ removes annotation cost, but typically produces an independent relevance score for each query--item pair and does not identify which query attributes are satisfied or violated. These approaches also treat items independently, overlooking the relationships among products in the catalog. We instead observe that the catalog itself contains the structure needed to define relevance sets. Recent retrieval and recommendation systems such as DSI~, RIPOR~, and TIGER~ represent items using sequences of discrete codes learned with residual quantization~. Because these codes are organized from coarse to fine, they induce a prefix tree over the catalog: items sharing a prefix form increasingly specific groups. We repurpose this structure not as an index for retrieval, but as an . However, vanilla RQ-VAE does not guarantee that a code corresponds to a single semantic attribute, which can produce heterogeneous clusters. We therefore introduce an that encourages each quantization level to specialize to a coherent attribute, making the resulting hierarchy suitable for defining relevance sets. A prefix then defines a relevance set, its length controls granularity, and the shared-prefix length between an item and the target prefix provides graded relevance without a hand-authored taxonomy. Building on this hierarchy, we filter clusters that are too small or heterogeneous to serve as relevance sets and compute each retained cluster's : the attributes that distinguish it from its parent and sibling clusters. A language model then verbalizes this structured evidence into shopper-style queries. Importantly, the language model generates the query but does not determine relevance; relevance is inherited deterministically from the semantic ID ~ structure. We instantiate this framework on the public Myntra catalog~, containing $44{,}446$ items, and construct : $588$ clusters across three granularity levels and $5{,}380$ attribute-enriched queries. The benchmark provides four complementary tasks: query--product ranking, hierarchical relevance classification, sibling discrimination, and paraphrase robustness. We additionally introduce a weighted query-relevance score and per-attribute scores to distinguish overall retrieval quality from fine-grained attribute satisfaction. Evaluation of ten off-the-shelf sparse and dense retrieval models, without fine-tuning, shows that aggregate ranking performance alone can mask important weaknesses. Dense encoders achieve substantially higher ranking quality than BM25~ , yet the best model achieves only $0.51$ accuracy on four-way hierarchical relevance classification. Model scale is also not consistently predictive: the $4$B Qwen3~ encoder slightly outperforms its $8$B counterpart on sibling discrimination. Attribute-level evaluation further localizes these differences: models perform strongly on product type and gender but substantially worse on material and style . Finally, a human study comparing benchmark queries with real user queries shows comparable perceived naturalness and strong intent clarity, supporting Fashion-RQBENCH as a proxy for real-world fashion search. Evaluating fashion retrieval systems remains challenging due to the limited availability of benchmarks that capture the fine-grained, open-ended queries encountered in real-world search and recommendation. Existing benchmarks often rely on manual curation, predefined taxonomies, or synthetic prompts, limiting scalability and semantic coverage. We introduce , an automated framework that derives user-type queries and relevance sets directly from catalog structure. Our key insight is that hierarchical discrete representations learned by Residual Quantized Variational Autoencoders organize products into semantically coherent clusters at progressively finer granularities. We leverage this structure to construct , a scalable benchmark without manual query annotation or predefined taxonomies. We propose four complementary tasks for evaluating ranking, relevance classification, fine-grained sibling discrimination, and paraphrase robustness, alongside query- and attribute-level relevance metrics. Experiments across recent retrieval models demonstrate that FASHION-RQBENCH differentiates model capabilities across query types and attributes. A human study further shows that benchmark queries achieve comparable naturalness and higher intent clarity than real user queries, supporting their validity for real-world evaluation.
