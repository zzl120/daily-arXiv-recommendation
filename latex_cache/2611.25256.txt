Typically, GR assigns each item a short tuple of discrete codes and trains an autoregressive decoder to generate the next item code sequence given a user history. As argued by Rajput et al.~, the semantic ID representation avoids storing a full embedding per item, and enables cold-start handling through a content encoder and the hierarchical ID structure of residual quantization. However, autoregressive GR exhibits a structural problem: the sequence encoder consumes the pretrained quantized item codes, so the predictor and the quantizer cannot be analyzed or optimized in isolation. This coupling has three concrete consequences. Let $M$ denote the number of code tokens per item and $N$ the history length: encoding each history item as $M$ tokens forces the encoder to attend over an $MN$-token sequence, with quadratic attention memory $O ^2)$. As empirically shown in , scaling $M$ also does not guarantee the improvement of ranking quality rather than improves it: a longer semantic ID nominally reduces the per-item quantization error, but the autoregressive prediction task itself becomes harder. Beam-search decoding adds another $O $ sequence-encoder forward passes per query, where $b$ is the beam width. Autoregressive methods are therefore confined in practice to short semantic IDs, bounding the code length they can spend on retrieval quality.
