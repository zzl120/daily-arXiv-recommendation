Source code is publicly released}. {Large language models conversational recommender system positional bias} Recommender systems are a core component of modern digital platforms, supporting users in navigating and filtering large volumes of information. By modeling user preferences and item characteristics, RS aims to deliver relevant and personalized recommendations across domains such as e-commerce, media consumption, and information retrieval. Despite substantial progress, RS remain susceptible to a range of biases that can affect both the reliability and the consistency of their outputs. Previous work has documented several forms of bias—including exposure bias, popularity bias, and positional bias—that can emerge at different stages of the recommendation pipeline and propagate through system components, ultimately shaping the user experience in unintended ways . Understanding, measuring, and mitigating such biases is, therefore, essential for ensuring transparent and reliable recommendation behavior. In parallel, Large Language Models have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for building intelligent systems. Trained in large-scale corpora using self-supervised objectives, LLMs exhibit a wide range of emergent capabilities, including question answering, reasoning, and decision-making . This has led to a shift from traditional model training toward prompt-based interaction, where system behavior is largely determined by how information is presented in natural language form. As a consequence, LLMs are increasingly being adopted as central components in RS, particularly in conversational settings. A common design for LLM-based RS is based on a retrieval-augmented generation architecture . In this setting, a retriever first computes semantic similarity scores between a user query and a collection of candidate items using an embedding model. The top-$k$ most relevant items are then selected and inserted into a prompt, together with the user query, which is provided to the LLM. Based on this prompt, the LLM produces a recommendation, typically by selecting or describing one of the candidate items. This architecture decouples retrieval from generation while allowing the LLM to reason over a constrained candidate set. The increasing adoption of LLMs within information systems has been identified as a major research frontier in the Information Systems community. Recent work emphasizes that generative AI systems introduce new forms of socio-technical risk, requiring systematic analysis of their behavioral properties, reliability, and governance when deployed in real-world organizational settings~. In this context, understanding how design choices in prompt-mediated systems affect model behavior is critical to advance both theory and practice in LLM-enabled information systems. However, LLM-based RS operate largely as black boxes. Although the retriever stage is often well understood and explicitly optimized, the final recommendation decision is made by the LLM through implicit reasoning over a sequential text prompt. As a result, unintended behaviors may arise that are not directly related to user preferences or item relevance. These behaviors are not necessarily associated with socially sensitive attributes such as gender or race but instead stem from subtle interactions between prompt structure and model internals. Ideally, a recommendation should depend only on the semantic relationship between the user query and the candidate items. In practice, additional latent variables may influence the outcome. One such variable is the position of candidate items within the prompt. Because text is inherently sequential, the retrieved items must be presented in a specific order. This ordering introduces positional information that is not semantically meaningful, but can nevertheless affect the LLM’s output. When the probability of recommending an item depends on its position in the input list rather than solely on its relevance, the system exhibits positional bias. Although positional bias has been extensively studied in traditional RS and user interfaces, its manifestation and measurement in LLM-based RS remain unstudied. In this work, we focus on the measurement and statistical characterization of positional bias in top-1 and single-turn LLM-based RS, where the model recommends a single item from the retrieved candidates in one conversational turn. Rather than proposing mitigation strategies, our objective is to rigorously determine whether positional bias is present, how it can be detected, and how strong its effects are under controlled experimental conditions. To structure this investigation, we formalize the following research questions: Does output selection in LLM-based RS depend on the position of candidate items within the input prompt? Is any observed dependence between item position and model output statistically significant under permutation-based testing? How large is the positional bias, and does its magnitude vary across models? To address these questions, we propose two complementary statistical methodologies for positional bias detection. The first, Positional Bias detection based on Independence of the Contingency Matrix , models the relationship between input positions and output selections as a contingency table and evaluates whether these variables are statistically independent. Under the null hypothesis of no positional bias, the distribution of recommended items should be independent of their input positions. Violations of this assumption are assessed using a query-level permutation test of independence that allows both detection and interpretation of positional bias. The second methodology, Positional Bias detection based on Uniformity assuming Non-Biased Positions , is designed to reduce sample complexity by leveraging a stronger null assumption. Specifically, in the absence of positional bias, on expectation over all the possible queries, the probability of recommending the item at a given position is uniform. By repeatedly permuting the order of candidate items and analyzing the resulting recommendation distributions, this method enables efficient detection of systematic positional preferences while requiring fewer samples than full contingency-based testing. Together, these methodologies provide a principled and statistically grounded framework for evaluating positional bias in LLM-based RS. Using these approaches, we conducted an empirical study on state-of-the-art LLMs embedded within a RAG-based RS for product recommendation, built on a real product catalog with template-generated user queries and a fixed prompt template. Our experiments reveal statistically significant positional bias, demonstrating that item order can systematically influence recommendation outcomes even when item relevance is kept constant. We complement these statistical tests with three proposed metrics. Permutation sensitivity metric evaluates how the model changes its decision on permutation changes, so it is an upper bound of positional bias. Positional bias metric measures the total variation distance between the uniform distribution and the distribution of positions of items selected by the RS, as deviation from the uniform distribution indicates a systematic preference for certain positions. Finally, the strong positional bias metric builds on relevance sets, the query-dependent sets of candidate items that the model treats as comparably relevant. Relevance sets decompose the overall positional bias detected by our methodologies into soft positional bias, where the position determines which item of the relevance set is recommended, and strong positional bias, where the position displaces the recommendation outside the relevance set, which this metric quantifies. In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows: We introduce POB-ICM, a statistically grounded methodology for detecting and interpreting overall positional bias in LLM-based RS based on contingency matrix independence. We propose POB-UNBP, a sample-efficient positional bias detection methodology based on uniformity assumptions under the absence of positional bias, which allows for stochastic decoding, and complement it with two quantification metrics that disentangle generic permutation sensitivity from structural positional preference. We decompose overall positional bias into soft and strong positional bias through query-dependent relevance sets, quantify the strong component, and show through counterfactual analyses that it is not explained by the identifiers assigned to the items and, for six of the eight evaluated models, not by stochastic decoding alone. The formal detection of each component separately is left as future work. We implement and evaluate an end-to-end experimental framework to assess positional bias in LLM-based RS using a RAG architecture in a single-turn product recommendation setting, including a systematic robustness analysis across candidate-set size $k$ and decoding temperature $t$ on eight state-of-the-art LLMs, showing that positional bias is a structural, model-dependent property rather than an artifact of deterministic decoding or candidate-set size. The empirical results indicate that positional bias is a systematic and statistically detectable factor in LLM-based recommender systems implemented under an RAG pipeline, within the evaluated setting. Across permutation-based evaluations, both proposed methodologies reject the null hypothesis that recommendation outcomes are independent of the positional placement of candidate items within the prompt in all evaluated models, and the resulting selection distributions exhibit structured, non-uniform positional bias with certain prompt positions associated with reliably elevated selection probabilities. Conversational RS generate personalized recommendations by interpreting user preferences expressed in natural language. Early conversational RS relied on rule-based or traditional machine learning approaches, while more recent systems increasingly incorporate deep learning models. With the advent of Large Language Models , recommendation pipelines have undergone a substantial shift, as LLMs enable direct reasoning over textual user queries and item descriptions. Previous work has demonstrated the applicability of LLMs to a variety of recommendation tasks, including rating prediction, top-$N$ recommendation, and conversational recommendation scenarios~. These systems can generate recommendations as item identifiers or as natural-language responses, leveraging the linguistic and reasoning capabilities of LLMs. A common instantiation of LLM-based RS adopts a retrieval-augmented generation architecture. In this setting, a retriever selects a set of $K$ candidate items from a larger database based on semantic similarity to the user query, and these candidates are subsequently provided to the LLM for recommendation generation~. In the top-$N$ recommendation, the model selects or ranks multiple items , while in the special case of the Top-1 recommendation, the LLM is required to select a single item from the candidate set. The latter setting is particularly relevant for conversational interfaces, where concise recommendations are often preferred, and is the setting studied in this work, in which a single item is recommended in a single conversational turn. Bias in RS has been extensively studied and is commonly categorized as and . Unfair biases involve systematic disparities that affect sensitive user groups, such as those defined by gender, race, or age~. In contrast, structural biases arise from the design of the system itself, including data preprocessing choices, model architecture, or prompt formulation. In LLM-based RS, structural biases can emerge from seemingly innocuous prompt design decisions, such as wording or synonym selection, which can influence the model output independently of user intent~. Beyond recommender systems, structural bias in LLMs has been studied across several application domains. We define positional bias as a special case of structural bias in which a model's output depends systematically on the sequential position of an input element such as a candidate item, an answer option, or a retrieved passage, rather than solely on its semantic content. Because language models process text as ordered sequences, any system that presents multiple elements in a list implicitly encodes positional information that the model may exploit, independently of relevance or quality. In the context of , it has been shown that the order in which choices are listed can affect model answers~. LLMs also exhibit selection bias by assigning more probability mass to specific option ID tokens regardless of content~. Additional studies have demonstrated that even the symbols used to delimit answer options can introduce systematic biases in model output~. In , demonstrated that LLM performance follows a U-shaped curve as a function of where relevant information appears in the context. They named this phenomenon as ``lost in the middle'' effect, with tracing this degradation to intrinsic U-shaped attention patterns in transformer models independent of content relevance. In the domain of , documented that strong LLMs used as evaluators exhibit positional bias when judging response quality, a finding confirmed at scale by across 15 judge models and over 150,000 evaluation instances. In , observed that recommendation performance deteriorates when highly relevant items appear in later positions of the candidate list. These findings provide empirical indicators of positional bias in different areas. However, across all these domains, these characterize positional bias through performance degradation metrics and does not provide a hypothesis-testing framework that formally isolates position as a statistically independent variable. Top-1 RS based on LLM can be naturally framed as a multiple-choice decision problem: a set of $K$ candidate items is presented to the model, and the LLM must select the item that best satisfies the user's preferences. However, unlike standard multiple-choice tasks, recommendation scenarios often admit multiple plausible answers rather than a single correct option. This distinction complicates the direct transfer of positional bias analysis from question answering to recommendation settings, and similarly limits the applicability of long-context QA approaches such as the lost-in-the-middle framework~, which rely on a ground-truth answer to measure positional bias and do not isolate position as a statistically independent variable. Moreover, unlike ranking-based formulations of LLM-based recommendation~, where the model orders all the candidates, a top-1 recommender only reveals the item it selects, so the relevance it assigns to the remaining candidates must be inferred indirectly, which makes it harder to separate positional effects from item relevance. There are nevertheless strong reasons to hypothesize that positional bias affects LLM-based RS. Training data for LLMs include numerous instances of recommendations and ranked lists, which may themselves reflect user interaction biases, such as a tendency to favor items appearing earlier in lists~. Previous surveys have warned that such biases can propagate into learned recommendation behaviors~. Empirical observations further suggest that the performance of LLM-based RS deteriorates when highly relevant items appear in later positions within the candidate set~, though these studies do not isolate positional bias from confounders such as retrieval quality or item relevance. Despite these observations and intuition, the closest existing approaches to statistically grounded quantification of positional bias in LLMs rely on scalar summary metrics rather than formal hypothesis testing. propose the standard deviation of per-option recall as a measure of selection bias in MCQ tasks. define positional bias as the divergence between the marginalized selection distribution and the uniform distribution, implicitly assuming, as we formally prove in , that absence of positional bias implies uniformity. introduce the Fluctuation Rate, a label-free metric defined as the fraction of instances where a fixed forward ordering and a single fixed backward ordering yield different answers; because it compares exactly two predetermined permutations rather than the full permutation space, it may not capture the full extent of positional bias. Crucially, none of these approaches frames detection as a hypothesis test or provides statistical significance guarantees, and all are developed for MCQ tasks where a unique correct answer exists. Moreover, positional bias analysis developed for multiple-choice question answering does not directly transfer to recommendation settings, where multiple candidates may be plausibly relevant and a single ground-truth label may not exist. To address these gaps, we develop two complementary hypothesis-testing methodologies tailored to single-turn Top-1 LLM-based recommendation under controlled permutations: , which detects and interprets positional dependence via contingency-matrix independence testing, and , which provides a sample-efficient alternative via a uniformity-based null model with formal statistical guarantees. Furthermore, since several candidates may be comparably relevant, we distinguish soft positional bias, where the position only determines which of these candidates is recommended, from strong positional bias, where the position displaces the recommendation towards clearly less relevant items, and, besides the overall positional bias, we quantify its strong component. Together, these methods enable principled detection and quantification of positional bias independently of item quality and retrieval performance. Quantifying positional bias requires distinguishing between and positional bias itself, as noted by~. Permutation sensitivity captures the instability of the model under different orderings of the same candidate set, a property that may change on a per-query basis. Positional bias, by contrast, refers to a systematic tendency to favor certain prompt positions regardless of item relevance, a property reflecting consistent patterns across queries. Crucially, permutation sensitivity does not necessarily imply positional bias, but positional bias necessarily induces some degree of permutation sensitivity. The metrics PS and PB in --, as well as the POB-ICM and POB-UNBP methodologies, treat any dependence of the recommended item on its position as positional bias, regardless of the relevance of the candidates involved. Such a dependence can manifest in two distinct ways. When several candidates are comparably relevant for the user query, the position of the items may determine which of them is recommended; we refer to this as . This form of bias does not degrade the relevance of the recommendation, but it still makes the exposure of comparably relevant items depend on the prompt construction rather than on their content. Alternatively, the position may lead the model to recommend an item that is clearly less relevant than the alternatives; we refer to this as . Positional bias, as detected by POB-ICM and POB-UNBP and quantified by PB, comprises both forms; we refer to this combination as . To make this distinction operational, we introduce a query-dependent relevance set $ \{1, 2, , k\}$, identifying the candidate items considered comparably relevant for instance $X$. For each instance, we count how often each candidate item is recommended across its sampled permutations. Given a threshold $ $ of an instance $X$ is the smallest set of its most frequently recommended items that together account for at least a fraction $ $ of its recommendations. The threshold $ $ is calibrated from the data. The recommendation frequencies of an instance, sorted in decreasing order, usually show a natural break between the items the model treats as plausibly interchangeable and those it rarely selects regardless of position. For each instance, we locate this break as the largest gap between consecutive sorted frequencies and compute the fraction of recommendations captured by the items above it; $ $ is the average of this fraction across instances. Using a common threshold, instead of locating the break separately in each instance, makes the relevance set less sensitive to the noise of the frequencies of individual instances, which are estimated from a limited number of permutations. We then define strong positional bias as the proportion of recommendations that fall outside the relevance set: = 100 _{X , U } . To avoid calibrating the threshold on the same instances on which sPB is evaluated, we use $5$-fold cross-validation over instances. For each fold, $ $ is calibrated on the other four folds, and the relevance sets and sPB are computed on the held-out fold. The reported sPB is the average over the five folds, so each instance is evaluated exactly once. Note that sPB and PB are not on a comparable scale, and sPB may exceed PB even though it targets a component of the overall positional bias. Addressing , our empirical analysis provides statistical evidence that the output selection of LLM-based RS depends on the position at which candidate items are presented in the input prompt. Through extensive permutation-based evaluations, we show that recommendation behavior is not invariant to item ordering, even when item relevance and retrieval are held constant. Addressing , we demonstrate that this dependence is statistically significant under formal hypothesis testing, with all evaluated models rejecting the null hypotheses of positional independence and uniformity using two complementary methodologies. Finally, with respect to , our results indicate that the magnitude and structure of positional bias vary across models, even within the same family, such as LLaMA 3.1 8B and 70B, suggesting that positional bias is influenced by design and training choices rather than being purely incidental. By increasing the number of presented items in the prompt, the positional bias is observed to generally increase across models. This idea is supported by how LLMs attend to different regions of the input prompt, where information appearing at the beginning or end of the prompt is often more influential for the model, regardless of the semantic information distributed across the prompt~. Furthermore, we distinguish two components of positional bias: soft positional bias, where the position determines which of several comparably relevant candidates is recommended, and strong positional bias, where the position displaces the recommendation towards clearly less relevant items. While the proposed statistical tests detect the overall positional bias, which comprises both components, the proposed metrics quantify the overall positional bias and its strong component, and our counterfactual analyses show that strong positional bias persists when controlling for the identifiers assigned to the items, and for six of the eight evaluated models when controlling for stochastic decoding. These findings should be interpreted within the scope of our experimental setting. Our evaluation covers single-turn product recommendation, combining user queries generated from templates with real components: a real product catalog , a pre-trained embedding-based retriever, and a single fixed prompt template. The heterogeneity of the real product titles and descriptions makes the task non-trivial; for instance, the titles generated by the models do not always match the original ones exactly. However, the template-generated queries encode a limited number of user preferences, and other domains, query formulations or prompt designs may yield different magnitudes and patterns of positional bias. Moreover, we focus on top-1 recommendation, a particularly demanding setting for positional bias analysis, since the relevance the model assigns to the candidates can only be inferred from a single selected item rather than from a full ranking. Within this scope, the main contribution of this work is methodological: a statistically grounded framework to detect overall positional bias in top-1 LLM-based RS, together with metrics to quantify it and the distinction between soft and strong positional bias, quantifying the latter, which is applicable beyond the evaluated setting, as discussed below. Beyond its methodological contributions, this work has direct implications for the design, evaluation, and governance of RS based on LLM in practice. In operational settings, positional bias can undermine the reliability of A/B testing, distort offline evaluation metrics, and introduce unintended variability in user-facing recommendations. From a governance perspective, such biases complicate auditability and accountability, as system behavior may change solely due to prompt construction choices rather than genuine differences in item relevance. For practitioners deploying LLM-based RS, our findings highlight the importance of treating prompt order as a first-class design variable rather than an implementation detail. Based on these findings, we outline several practical recommendations for organizations deploying LLM-based RS. First, evaluation pipelines should explicitly incorporate randomized or counterbalanced item ordering to detect and monitor positional bias. Second, prompt construction should be standardized or controlled to ensure consistency between deployments and experiments. Third, bias audits should include permutation-based tests, such as those proposed in this work, as part of routine model validation and monitoring processes. Finally, when positional bias is detected, mitigation strategies such as order randomization, ensemble prompting, or post-generation re-ranking should be considered to reduce its impact on end-user outcomes. Auditing positional bias is inexpensive: in our simulation study, POB-UNBP reaches $80\ Regarding the extensibility of the proposed framework and future research lines, both POB-ICM and POB-UNBP are designed to operate on any black-box system that receives an indexed list of candidate products and returns a recommendation expressed as a product position within that list. This abstraction is intentionally general and does not depend on the internal architecture of the LLM, the retrieval mechanism, or the number of conversational turns. Concretely: The framework can be applied to multi-turn settings provided that the recommendation at each turn is expressed as a positional selection from a fixed candidate set. The permutation-based evaluation can then be applied independently at each turn, treating each turn as a self-contained Top-1 instance, although confirming that our empirical findings carry over to multi-turn interactions requires a new set of experiments, which is outside the scope of this work. These settings are less straightforward. When the system returns an ordered list of $N > 1$ items, or reorders the full candidate set, the notion of positional bias must be generalized to account for how the relative ranking of selected items is affected by input ordering. This requires adapting the contingency-based and uniformity-based null models to joint distributions over ranked outputs, which we leave as a direction for future work. We use a single fixed prompt template, not derived from a systematic prompt engineering phase. This does not affect the validity of the proposed detection methodologies, but the magnitude and pattern of the measured bias may depend on the prompt design, so evaluating how different prompt formulations modulate positional bias remains an important direction for future work. Our experiments are restricted to product recommendation, although the proposed methodologies apply without modification to other domains, such as news, music or long-document recommendation. Since the position preferences are stable across decoding temperatures and, for several models, across candidate-set sizes, we expect the differences between models to persist, although their magnitude may vary: longer item descriptions may accentuate effects such as lost in the middle~, and queries encoding more user preferences may shift the balance between soft and strong positional bias. The proposed statistical tests detect the overall positional bias, while strong positional bias is currently quantified through a metric based on relevance sets estimated from the selection frequencies of the model itself. A natural research line is to develop statistical methodologies that detect each component independently, with formal guarantees and robust to the noise introduced by stochastic decoding and by the estimation of the relevance sets. The proposed metrics can guide mitigation, either by adding PB as a regularization term when fine-tuning the recommender, as related work does in reranking settings~, or, without access to the model weights, by optimizing the prompt template with a genetic algorithm whose fitness is the negated sPB on random batches of queries. Such an optimization should preserve the recommendation task, e.g., by also penalizing PB, since a purely position-driven prompt could also lower sPB, and be validated on held-out queries. More broadly, our framework provides a principled protocol to benchmark any mitigation strategy. Future work should therefore further extend positional bias analysis to top-$N$, multi-turn conversational and other recommendation domains, incorporate explicit quality and utility metrics, and explore the interaction between positional bias and other structural biases such as popularity or exposure bias.
