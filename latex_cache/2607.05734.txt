Large teachers approach the recommendation task with unusually high reasoning uncertainty, repeatedly rechecking their answers without revising them; supervised fine-tuning on such traces produces verbose students that never revise their initial guess. Furthermore, due to the novelty of the recommendation domain, the teacher's reasoning traces are highly out-of-distribution for the small student LLM. We propose a CoT summarization framework tailored to recommendation that first parses each teacher trace into typed segments and uses the student LLM's attention to score the importance of each segment. Then we dynamically select a per-segment edit based on the output length and comparative log probability lift of the answer given the edit as per the student. Therefore, our framework prunes redundant sections of the reasoning trace while preserving information-dense sections and adapts raw teacher traces to the student's output distribution. The most relevant line for our work is reasoning-aware generative recommendation. propose OneRec-Think, which extends OneRec-style implicit prediction with explicit in-text reasoning through itemic alignment, reasoning activation, and recommendation-specific reward design. similarly make reasoning traces central to reranking: a large teacher generates rationales for SFT, and the resulting student is further optimized with verifiable ranking rewards. In both cases, distillation is a critical step for transferring large-model reasoning into a deployable recommender. However, because recommendation labels are noisy and multi-valid, the teacher does not reliably zero-shot recover the logged target, motivating trace-construction procedures such as pruned-context rationale generation, targeted sampling, or rejection sampling. Chain-of-thought distillation trains smaller models to imitate teacher-generated rationales, and has been widely studied in reasoning domains such as mathematics, symbolic reasoning, and question answering . Recommendation distillation is qualitatively different from these settings. In math or code, the ground-truth answer is usually fixed and the teacher trace can be treated as an imperfect derivation of a well-defined target. In recommendation, even the teacher may not believe the logged label is optimal. Targeted sampling can force label-aligned rationales but risks post-hoc justification, while rejection sampling preserves the teacher's unaided prediction process but is inefficient, biased toward teacher-solvable examples, and still leaves redundancy inside accepted traces. Existing CoT distillation methods generally do not account for whether a teacher trace is easy, hard, or out-of-distribution for the student, even though this mismatch is central when distilling recommendation reasoning into small models. A separate line of work compresses long reasoning traces to reduce training or inference cost . These methods establish that CoTs contain substantial redundancy, but they generally treat the original trace as correct and focus on shortening it while preserving as much performance as possible. As a result, compression is typically an efficiency--accuracy trade-off: shorter traces reduce token cost, but raw traces remain the strongest supervision signal. This is especially natural in math, code, and science, where compression risks deleting necessary logical steps. Recommendation traces exhibit a different failure mode: the teacher often repeats candidate verification without changing the answer. Our work targets this redundancy not merely to shorten the trace, but to improve the supervision signal by removing repeated uncertainty while keeping the compressed trace in-distribution for the student. Our compression pipeline consists of three stages: LLM-based chain-of-thought segmentation, attention-based importance scoring, and per-segment edit selection based on each segment's importance score and relative advantage under a reward function. Generative recommendation addresses this limitation by casting recommendation as sequence generation, allowing a single model to directly produce recommendation outputs. P5 unifies rating prediction, sequential recommendation, and explanation as personalized text-to-text tasks, while M6-Rec adapts an industrial pretrained model across retrieval, ranking, explanation, and content generation with efficient tuning. TIGER instead learns semantic item identifiers for autoregressive retrieval, whereas LLMRank prompts LLMs to perform zero-shot listwise reranking. OneRec further replaces the retrieve--rank cascade with a unified generator and iterative preference alignment. Reasoning-aware systems extend this line: OneRec-Think grounds item tokens in text, activates explicit reasoning through scaffolding, and uses rewards designed for multiple valid recommendations; GR2 mid-trains on semantic IDs, distills teacher-generated reranking rationales through SFT, and applies DAPO with verifiable ranking rewards. Both rely on obtaining reasoning traces that match logged recommendation targets despite the fact that recommendation labels are noisy and often non-unique. A common solution is targeted generation, where the teacher is conditioned to explain a predetermined item. While this increases label alignment, it also encourages post-hoc rationalization: the teacher is no longer explaining its own decision process, but constructing a justification for an externally specified outcome. CoT prompting showed that intermediate steps can improve large-model reasoning, while self-consistency aggregates diverse sampled paths instead of trusting one greedy derivation. Distillation turns this behavior into supervision: Distilling Step-by-Step jointly trains on labels and teacher rationales; Symbolic CoT Distillation and Teaching Small Language Models to Reason show that much smaller students can acquire stepwise reasoning from sampled traces; and MiniLLM uses reverse-KL sequence-level distillation to reduce mode averaging and exposure bias. Distillation also plays a central role in recent reasoning-based recommendation systems: both OneRec-Think and GR2 first transfer reasoning behavior from a large teacher into a smaller recommender through supervised distillation before applying reinforcement learning. However, recommendation differs from traditional reasoning tasks because logged labels are noisy and represent only one of many plausible items. As a result, teachers may not naturally predict the target item, motivating targeted generation or filtering to obtain label-compatible traces. These procedures can introduce rationalization, selection bias, or traces that are difficult for the student to learn, making recommendation-specific CoT distillation an important but still underexplored problem. Reasoning compression operates at prompt, token, step, and trajectory levels. LLMLingua performs budgeted coarse-to-fine token pruning; C3oT jointly trains on long and compressed traces; and TokenSkip learns controllable shortcuts by dropping low-importance tokens. Step Entropy removes low-information steps, whereas CRISP uses model-internal saliency to prune redundancy. For distillation, Compress-Distill compresses correct teacher traces post hoc before SFT, and mixed-policy distillation has a teacher rewrite student-sampled trajectories, preserving the student's policy while transferring concise behavior. Existing compression methods generally view reasoning traces as correct and seek shorter versions that preserve as much performance as possible. In practice, however, compression almost always incurs some accuracy degradation because shortening a trace can remove steps that are important for a downstream model's reasoning process, alter the logical structure of the derivation, or produce compressed traces that are difficult for the target student to model. Since compression is typically performed without considering the student's capabilities, the resulting traces may also contain high-perplexity tokens or transitions that are out-of-distribution for the learner. Recent work shows that learning from high-perplexity tokens can contribute to catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning .
