 Multi-vector retrieval has emerged as a powerful paradigm for modern retrieval systems, in which each query and document is represented not by a single embedding, but by a set of embeddings . Such representations arise naturally in late-interaction models for text retrieval , where relevance is computed via token-level interactions using operators such as $ $, as well as in region-based representations for images and other modalities . This added expressiveness yields significantly better retrieval quality than single-vector encoders but the increased complexity of the representation makes search substantially more expensive. The gold-standard multi-vector similarity, introduced by ColBERT~ and used by most subsequent late-interaction models, is $ $. Given a query point cloud $A ^d$ and a document point cloud $B ^d$, $$ := _{a A} _{b B} a, b ,$$ i.e., each query embedding is matched to its most similar document embedding and the resulting scores are summed. Thus, unlike single-vector retrieval, even a single query--document $ $ score can require many inner product computations. At scale, the central challenge is therefore to recover the top-$k$ documents under $ $ without exhaustively scoring the entire collection. A large body of recent work has focused on making multi-vector retrieval under $ $ efficient. Most practical approaches follow a common philosophy: rather than indexing point clouds directly, they reduce candidate generation to a simpler proxy or filtering problem built using single-vector retrieval techniques, and reserve exact multi-vector scoring for a smaller set of candidates. For example, PLAID~ and its follow-up, WARP~, use single-vector search over clustered token centroids to generate candidates, which are then scored using quantized and exact $ $; formalize this style of pipeline as the . MUVERA~ instead constructs fixed-dimensional encodings , large single-vector representations whose inner products provably approximate multi-vector similarity, thereby reducing the problem to standard single-vector MIPS. These approaches are highly effective, but the quality of the final top-$k$ results depends on how well the surrogate representation or candidate-generation stage preserves documents that rank highly under $ $. This motivates a complementary direction: A smaller body of work has explored such native multi-vector indexing through hashing- and graph-based approaches, including DESSERT~ and IGP~. In this work, we investigate a different direction: extending clustering-based inverted-file indexing, one of the most widely used paradigms for large-scale single-vector retrieval~, to multi-vector data. IVF is an appealing candidate for this setting. In the single-vector case, an IVF index partitions the dataset into clusters and probes only those whose representatives are most relevant to the query. This organization is simple and scalable to build, has low indexing overhead, exposes substantial parallelism, and is naturally suited to high-throughput batched search. These properties are particularly attractive for multi-vector retrieval, where scoring each candidate is substantially more expensive. However, extending IVF to multi-vector data is non-trivial: We address these challenges through a set of contributions centered around , our native IVF-style index for multi-vector retrieval. Our main contribution is , a hierarchical IVF-style index for multi-vector retrieval. Our approach represents each cluster by a center that is itself a point cloud containing at most $s$ vectors, and uses $ $ scores between the query and these center point clouds for routing. In this sense, is a multi-vector index: both the indexed objects and the routing representatives remain point clouds, rather than being reduced to single-vector surrogates. The key algorithmic ingredient is . We first formulate a natural multi-vector analogue of $k$-median, in which each cluster center is a point cloud of size at most $s$, and assignment costs are measured using the analogous Chamfer distance: $$ := {|A|} _{a A} _{b B} \|a-b\|_2. $$ We then develop , a practical Lloyd-style algorithm for this problem. Our key observation is that, once the point-cloud assignments to clusters are fixed, finding the optimal center point cloud for a cluster reduces exactly to a weighted $s$-median problem over the flattened embeddings of the point clouds assigned to that cluster. This allows the center-update step to leverage standard, highly optimized single-vector clustering machinery. recursively applies to construct a tree-structured partition of the document point clouds and stores the resulting center point clouds at internal nodes for routing. At query time, performs a best-first beam search over the tree to identify a small number of promising leaf clusters and scores only the documents contained in those leaves. We further accelerate search using low-bit quantization, query compression, and early termination. For high-throughput workloads, many queries probe overlapping sets of leaves, creating an opportunity to reuse leaf data across queries. Rather than processing each query independently, we group queries that probe the same leaf and score them together, allowing the leaf data to be reused while cache-resident. This is particularly valuable in the multi-vector setting, where each leaf contains many document point clouds, each comprising many embeddings, and repeatedly loading the same leaf can incur substantial memory traffic. To further explore the design space of native multi-vector indexes, we also develop , a graph-based index extending the Vamana algorithm~ to operate directly over point clouds. builds a proximity graph over document point clouds and performs beam search using multi-vector scores directly. We find that it is highly competitive in the single-query regime, where graph-based search is effective at minimizing the number of expensive point-cloud comparisons. Given a query $Q$, both compression methods considered below produce a partition $=\{B_1, ,B_r\}$ of the query embeddings, and replace each block $B_i$ by a single representative $ q_i := _{q B_i} q.$ The compressed query is then $ Q=\{ q_1, , q_r\}$. Using sums rather than averages preserves the scale of the original MaxSim score: a block with more query embeddings contributes proportionally more to the final score. Larger compression thresholds produce fewer representatives and faster scoring, but can reduce retrieval quality. Ball carving is the greedy query-compression method used by MUVERA~. Given a distance threshold $ $, the algorithm repeatedly selects an unassigned query embedding $q$, forms a block consisting of all currently unassigned embeddings within distance $ $ of $q$, replaces this block by the sum of its vectors, and removes the block from the active set. This process continues until all query embeddings have been assigned. Thus, the number of compressed query vectors is not fixed in advance; it is determined by the geometry of the query and the threshold $ $. We also consider compression based on Ward's agglomerative clustering~. Each query embedding is initialized as a singleton cluster. We then repeatedly merge the pair of clusters that causes the smallest increase in within-cluster variance, stopping once the next merge exceeds a threshold $ $. As with ball carving, each final cluster is represented by the sum of its embeddings. Ward compression tends to form more balanced and coherent groups of query vectors, and in our experiments gives a slightly better quality--compression tradeoff. Figure~ compares the two compression methods as the threshold is varied. Both methods are effective: moderate compression reduces the number of query embeddings substantially with little effect on recall. We use Ward's method with threshold $0.5$ by default, since it is slightly faster in our implementation and gives comparable or better recall across the operating points we consider. The cost of query compression itself is negligible compared to the overall search time; its main effect is to reduce the cost of the subsequent multi-vector scoring kernels. We introduce , an inverted-file index for multi-vector retrieval built on , a new Lloyd-style algorithm for clustering point clouds under a Chamfer objective. Our key observation is that once point clouds are assigned to clusters, the center-update step reduces to a standard single-vector clustering problem over the flattened embeddings in each cluster. This enables efficient construction of a hierarchical IVF tree whose internal nodes are represented by center point clouds. At query time, MV-IVF performs best-first beam search over the tree to identify promising leaf clusters, then scores the documents in those leaves. We further accelerate search using low-bit quantization, query compression, and cache-efficient batched search. We implement MV-IVF as part of , an optimized C++ library and unified evaluation framework for multi-vector retrieval that includes several major multi-vector retrieval approaches. Across BEIR and LoTTE text retrieval benchmarks, MV-IVF achieves strong latency--recall tradeoffs, the best batched throughput frontiers, and favorable build-time and index-size profiles. The retrieval task is to return the top-$k$ document point clouds most relevant to $Q$ under a suitable set-to-set scoring rule. Let $A,B ^d$ be finite sets of embeddings. The Chamfer distance from $A$ to $B$ is $$ := {|A|} _{a A} _{b B} \|a-b\|_2.$$ The $ $ similarity is $$ := _{a A} _{b B} a,b .$$ Multi-vector retrieval is typically phrased in terms of $ $: throughout the paper, we use $ $ to rank documents and to route queries during search. In the single-vector setting, an inverted-file index partitions the documents into clusters and stores the points assigned to each cluster in an inverted list. Each cluster is represented by a centroid; at query time, the query is compared against these centroids and only a small number of promising lists are probed. In this section, we develop , a practical Lloyd-style heuristic for clustering point clouds under a Chamfer-based objective. We now formally define the multi-vector clustering problem. Let $ = \{P_1,P_2, ,P_n\}$ denote a collection of point clouds, where each $P_i ^d$ is a finite set of embeddings. For clustering, we seek $k$ representative center-sets, each of size at most $s$, that minimize the total assignment cost under Chamfer distance. Given $ = \{P_1, ,P_n\}$ and parameters $k,s $, the goal is to find a collection of center-sets $ = \{C_1,C_2, ,C_k\}$, $C_j ^d$, $|C_j| s$, minimizing $$ := _{i=1}^n _{j } .$$ This objective can be viewed as a natural multi-vector analogue of the classical $k$-median clustering problem. Each center is itself a point cloud, and the budget $s$ controls the size, and hence the expressiveness, of the representative stored for each cluster. Larger values of $s$ allow the center-set to capture more of the structure of its assigned point clouds; if $s$ is large enough, the center-set can contain the union of all embeddings in the cluster. The main challenge in the multi-vector setting lies in the update step. Unlike vanilla $k$-means, where the optimal update is given by the arithmetic mean of the assigned vectors, here each updated center must itself be a point cloud of size at most $s$. Given a cluster $ _j$, the new center is found by solving C_j = _{C ^d,\ |C| s} _{P _j} . This is precisely a Chamfer $ $-median problem on the point clouds assigned to cluster $j$. Specifically, for a cluster $ _j $, let $ := _{P _j} P$ denote the multiset obtained by flattening all points in the point clouds assigned to $ _j$. Computing the optimal Chamfer $ $-median center-set for $ _j$ is equivalent to solving a $s$-median problem on the multiset $ $. Proposition~ shows that the center-update step has a particularly simple structure: once the assignment of point clouds to clusters is fixed, updating each center-set reduces to an ordinary weighted single-vector clustering problem on the flattened embeddings in that cluster. Thus, the multi-vector nature of the problem appears only in the assignment step; the update step can reuse standard single-vector clustering routines. In the formal objective above, the update corresponds to a weighted problem. In our implementation, however, we use an update with cosine similarity, matching the geometry of the normalized embeddings used by the retrieval model and allowing us to leverage highly optimized $k$-means implementations. In this section, we describe , a clustering-based retrieval method that generalizes the inverted-file framework to multi-vector data. During indexing, the data point clouds are recursively partitioned using , producing a tree-structured IVF index whose internal nodes are represented by centroid point clouds. During search, a query is routed to relevant leaf clusters, and the document point clouds contained in those leaves are scored to obtain the final candidates. The key distinction from standard single-vector IVF is that both the indexed objects and the routing representatives are point clouds. Thus, routing decisions are made directly in the native multi-vector space, using $ $ between the query point cloud and the centroid point clouds stored in the tree. We create one child for each cluster $ _i$, store the corresponding centroid point cloud $C_i$ at that child, and recurse on $ _i$. In this way, builds a tree whose leaves contain document point clouds, while the internal node store centroid point clouds used for routing. The resulting structure is a direct multi-vector analogue of a standard $k$-means tree, except that clustering and routing are done over point clouds. To identify the top leafs to probe for a query point cloud $Q$, we perform a greedy beam search over the tree, prioritizing nodes by their $ $ score with $Q$. The search maintains a beam of active internal nodes, truncated to size , and a list $ $ of the best leaves encountered so far. At each step, it expands the highest-scoring internal node, scores its child centroid point clouds, and inserts the most promising child nodes into the appropriate collection: internal nodes into the beam, and leaves into $ $. We also employ the following early termination strategy. Suppose the algorithm has already found leaf nodes. If the best remaining internal node in the beam has $ $ value no larger than the worst leaf currently in $ $, then the search terminates. Intuitively, this indicates that the remaining frontier is unlikely to contain a better probe leaf, and further exploration is unlikely to improve the final probe set. We note that this rule is a heuristic: the centroid point cloud stored at an internal node is used as a routing representative, rather than a certified upper bound on the scores of all leaves in its subtree. Empirically, however, this early termination substantially reduces search time with little effect on retrieval quality. Algorithm~ summarizes the greedy search procedure. In high-throughput settings, queries either arrive in, or can be readily grouped into . A naive approach for this batch setting is to simply run the single-query algorithm independently per query, but this misses significant optimization opportunities as many queries end up probing the same leaf clusters, and repeatedly loading the same leaf data, wasting memory bandwidth. This structure is particular to IVF-style indexes: unlike graph-based beam search, whose traversal is irregular and query-dependent, IVF exposes shared leaf-level work that can be batched and scored with good locality. To exploit this structure, uses a batched search procedure that preserves the logical behavior of the single-query algorithm, but reorganizes the work to improve locality and vectorization. The input is a batch of query point clouds $Q_1,Q_2, ,Q_m$, where each query may have its own search parameters such as $k$ and . For each query, the goal is still to identify a set of probe leaves, score the document point clouds in those leaves, and return the final results. The difference is that schedules the expensive scoring work across queries so that shared tree and leaf data can be reused while it is cache-resident. At the root, every query must score the same set of child centroid point clouds. We therefore compute these scores for the entire query batch together, turning root scoring into a many-to-many $ $ computation. After the root, each query continues its own greedy search independently. Although the same batching idea could be applied deeper in the tree, doing so requires additional synchronization and gives diminishing returns as queries quickly diverge to different subtrees. After probe generation, each query has a set of leaves that it needs to scan. Rather than processing each query--leaf pair independently, groups the work by leaf. For each leaf $u$, we collect all queries whose probe set contains $u$, load the document point clouds stored in $u$, and score them against all queries assigned to that leaf. This improves cache locality by reusing the same leaf data across multiple queries and avoids repeatedly streaming large point-cloud data from memory. The leaf-grouped organization introduces a final gathering step: after each leaf has scored the queries assigned to it, the resulting candidates must be collected back into per-query result sets. We implement this by having each leaf-scoring kernel produce local top candidates for its assigned queries, then scatter these candidates into per-query buffers. Each query then aggregates the candidates returned from all of its probed leaves, keeps the best $k$ candidates, and returns them as the final top-$k$ results. This scatter-and-gather step adds synchronization and memory traffic, but in our setting this overhead is outweighed by the improved locality from grouping leaf scans and reusing leaf data across multiple queries. The common computational primitive in both batched probe generation and leaf-grouped scoring is a many-to-many $ $ problem: a group of query point clouds must be scored against a group of document point clouds stored in the same leaf. For exact scoring, we decompose this into a sequence of vectorized matrix multiplications and reduction operations, using optimized linear-algebra kernels where appropriate. We also use standard systems techniques such as cache-aware blocking and software prefetching to reduce memory stalls during leaf scans. Nevertheless, exact many-to-many $ $ scoring remains expensive, especially for large leaves and long point clouds. In Section~, we describe how the same batched organization is combined with quantization, compact representations, and specialized low-bit scoring kernels. Overall, batched search keeps the same retrieval semantics as single-query . The advantage is in how the work is organized. By grouping queries by leaf and using vectorized many-to-many $ $ kernels, better exploits cache locality, memory bandwidth, and SIMD parallelism in high-throughput retrieval workloads. In this section, we describe the main optimizations used in our implementation to accelerate routing, leaf scoring, and batched retrieval. A key bottleneck in multi-vector retrieval is the cost of computing Chamfer distances, which requires evaluating many pairwise single-vector distances. We leverage three quantization schemes at different points on the accuracy--compression tradeoff curve. All three share the same preprocessing step: a via a Fast Hadamard Transform with random sign flips, following the approach used in RaBitQ~ and TurboQuant~. Here we quantize each rotated coordinate to a single bit by simply taking its sign. This yields $D'/8$ bytes per vector (i.e., $32 $ compression over float32). Because the rotation isotropizes the coordinates, sign bits after random rotation preserve angular relationships between vectors: the expected fraction of differing sign bits between two vectors is $ / $, so the Hamming distance of the sign-bit codes is a faithful proxy for angular distance. Rand-1Bit is amenable to very fast SIMD implementations as the distance kernel reduces to XOR plus population count, avoiding all multiply-add arithmetic. On hardware supporting VPOPCNTDQ, each inner-loop iteration processes 512 dimensions via a single XOR and a single per-64-bit-lane popcount. Furthermore, the per-cloud running minimum in the Chamfer computation can be maintained entirely in integer arithmetic, with a single conversion to floating point at the end. We integrate an existing 4-bit scalar quantization scheme~ that uses a fixed, non-uniform codebook of 16 centroids whose spacing is tailored to the approximately sub-Gaussian distribution of coordinates after rotation. Each rotated coordinate is mapped to the nearest centroid and two 4-bit codes are nibble-packed per byte, giving $D'/2$ bytes per vector. Queries are encoded asymmetrically at 8-bit precision. We adapt the single-vector SIMD kernels from this implementation to the multi-vector setting by tailoring the GEMM kernel to small matrices, circumventing the heavy cache-blocking machinery and unrolling. We pre-transpose database vectors into panels of sub-vectors to compute 16 4-dimensional dot products per instruction using VPDPBUSD. Our 8-bit scheme uses adaptive per-vector uniform quantization: after rotation, each coordinate is scaled by $$127/ _i |x_{} |$$ and rounded to the nearest integer in $ $. This is $2 $ more storage than our 4-bit format, but yields very high-quality quantization , and also simplifies the implementation. In particular, since each dimension occupies a full byte, the SIMD kernel requires no codebook lookup or nibble unpacking. The multi-vector panel layout and GEMM micro-kernels are similar to the 4-bit case. We also explored other popular quantization strategies, including Product Quantization with FastScan-style optimizations and RaBitQ~. We discuss these variants, together with some empirical comparisons, in }. Query point clouds often contain substantial redundancy, so many $ $ computations can be accelerated by scoring a smaller representative set of query vectors. Following this observation, also used in , we compress each query point cloud before routing and leaf scoring. Given a query $Q$, both compression methods considered below produce a partition $=\{B_1, ,B_r\}$ of the query embeddings, and replace each block $B_i$ by a single representative $ q_i := _{q B_i} q.$ The compressed query is then $ Q=\{ q_1, , q_r\}$. Using sums rather than averages preserves the scale of the original $ $ score: a block with more query embeddings contributes proportionally more to the final score. Larger compression thresholds produce fewer representatives and faster scoring, but can reduce retrieval quality. Ball carving is the greedy query-compression method used by MUVERA~. Given a distance threshold $ $, the algorithm repeatedly selects an unassigned query embedding $q$, forms a block consisting of all currently unassigned embeddings within distance $ $ of $q$, replaces this block by the sum of its vectors, and removes the block from the active set. This process continues until all query embeddings have been assigned. Thus, the number of compressed query vectors is not fixed in advance; it is determined by the geometry of the query and the threshold $ $. We also consider compression based on Ward's agglomerative clustering method~. Each query embedding is initialized as a singleton cluster. We then repeatedly merge the pair of clusters that causes the smallest increase in within-cluster variance, stopping once the next merge exceeds a threshold $ $. As with ball carving, each final cluster is represented by the sum of its embeddings. Ward compression tends to form more balanced and coherent groups of query vectors, and in our experiments gives a slightly better quality--compression tradeoff. In our implementation, we use Ward's method by default. We also align the compressed query size to the blocking requirements of our many-to-many kernels, avoiding inefficient epilogue cases in the vectorized scoring routines. An empirical comparison of the two compression methods is provided in }. We presented , a native IVF-style index for multi-vector retrieval. The key ingredient is , a Lloyd-style clustering primitive for point clouds under a Chamfer-based objective, which lets us build an IVF tree whose routing representatives are themselves point clouds. Together with quantized scoring, query compression, and cache-efficient batched search, gives a practical clustering-based approach to multi-vector search. We also presented MV-Vamana, a native graph-based index, and MVSIC, a unified C++ framework for implementing and evaluating multi-vector retrieval methods. Our experiments show that provides strong latency--recall and throughput--recall tradeoffs while maintaining moderate build time and index size. Several directions remain open. First, the clustering problem underlying deserves further study, including approximation algorithms, initialization schemes, and variants based on different normalizations or set-to-set similarities. Although clustering with non-metric objectives is generally hard, it is not clear whether existing hardness results fully capture the structure of Chamfer objectives arising from multi-vector embeddings. Second, the design space for multi-vector IVF is still far from exhausted: spilling, probe allocation, and reranking strategies may lead to further improvements, especially on larger datasets. Finally, MV-Vamana is a promising direction in its own right. While its build time is high and its irregular search pattern is less favorable for batching, graph-based search is effective at minimizing expensive multi-vector comparisons, and merits deeper study.
