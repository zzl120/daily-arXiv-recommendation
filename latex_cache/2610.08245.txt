However, it has paid limited attention to a distinct distributive perspective: whether users' contributions to model learning should be reflected in the recommendation benefits they receive. We argue that, in addition to existing fairness protections, a fair system may account for the alignment between users' estimated contributions and the recommendation performance they receive. CPFR constructs ordered user groups from a training-dependent contribution considering interaction volume, loss alignment, and optimization intensity, and jointly optimizes recommendation accuracy with the two fairness requirements. Fairness in recommender systems has attracted significant research attention in recent years~. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into group fairness and individual fairness. Traditional group fairness methods aim to ensure equitable treatment across user groups defined by sensitive attributes such as gender, age, or race~. Common formulations include demographic parity and equalized odds~, which enforce statistical equality in recommendation outcomes across groups. Following this line, Leonhardt et al.~ first explicitly formulated user fairness concerns, Rahmani et al.~ conducted systematic experiments on generalizability, and Bisht et al.~ proposed CUGF for credibility and fairness; additionally, Xuan et al.~ leverages group-specific negative sampling to enhance inter-group fairness. Individual fairness, following Dwork et al.~, requires similar individuals to receive similar treatment~. Recent work also studies the relation between group and individual fairness~. Non-uniform notions exist as well: Deldjoo et al.~ provide an evaluation framework with configurable target distributions; Biega et al.~ require item attention to be proportional to item relevance over repeated rankings; and Rampisela et al.~ evaluate user fairness using interaction-history similarity and effectiveness disparity. Our CPFR differs in its fairness basis and target: it estimates each user's contribution to model learning and constrains the recommendation performance received by users across contribution groups. Multi-objective optimization is widely adopted to balance accuracy against group fairness or multi-stakeholder interests~. Recent works have expanded objectives to include serendipity and user satisfaction~, or explored the accuracy-fairness trade-off frontier~. Techniques like MGDA~, PCGrad~, and CAGrad~ are commonly used to mitigate gradient conflicts. Our work operationalizes Contribution-Performance Fairness through a specific multi-objective formulation. Distinct from conventional fairness setups, our optimization simultaneously balances recommendation accuracy, within-group equity, and cross-group alignment. We leverage MoCo~ to navigate this optimization landscape, aligning recommendation performance with the estimated user contribution. The concept of valuing data contributions has been explored in machine learning contexts. Ghorbani and Zou~ proposed Data Shapley for principled data valuation, and Koh and Liang~ introduced influence functions to estimate training sample impact. Federated learning faces related challenges in contribution evaluation and fair incentive distribution~. Shi et al.~ surveyed fairness-aware federated learning, Liu et al.~ proposed GTG-Shapley for efficient contribution evaluation, and Shi et al.~ introduced FedFAIM for fair incentives. While federated learning addresses contribution fairness at the client level, our work tackles this at the user level within a single recommender system. CPFR complements existing non-uniform fairness notions by using a training-dependent contribution estimate as the basis for user-side performance constraints. It enforces comparable performance within contribution groups and monotonic average performance across groups. Recommender systems have become the cornerstone of modern information services, alleviating information overload and driving user engagement across e-commerce, social media, and streaming platforms~. As these systems increasingly mediate access to opportunities and content, ensuring algorithmic fairness has emerged as a critical research frontier~. Many fairness-aware recommendation methods seek parity in outcomes across groups defined by sensitive or behavioral attributes~. Complementary work considers non-uniform fairness based on target distributions, relevance, or user similarity~. Together, these studies provide diverse definitions and perspectives on fairness in RSs. However, existing work pays limited attention to a fundamental asymmetry: users are not merely recipients but active contributors of data. High-contributing users provide abundant, high-quality interactions that are critical for model training, whereas others may contribute sparse or noisy signals. Existing methods do not directly address whether a user's contribution to model learning should be reflected in the recommendation performance the user receives. When contribution heterogeneity is ignored, parity-oriented treatments may create a structural misalignment by suppressing the performance of high-contribution users to match lower-contribution users, thereby weakening incentives and potentially undermining long-term ecosystem health. We advocate explicitly incorporating estimated, training-dependent user contribution into model optimization to achieve . We introduce , a novel fairness perspective in which users with comparable estimated contributions should receive comparable performance, and higher-contribution groups should receive no worse average performance compared with lower-contribution ones. Unlike immutable demographic attributes , estimated user contribution depends on engagement and may change over time. Users can therefore receive better recommendation performance by contributing more informative data. Notably, this principle is intended for settings with voluntary, model-relevant feedback, and complements rather than replaces equality-based or protected-group fairness. Low contribution should not reduce service below a reasonable quality floor, since sparse activity may reflect cold-start status, limited access, or privacy choices rather than unwillingness to contribute. Realizing this perspective introduces two key challenges: User contribution is not directly observed and may be reflected by multiple signals from user data and model training. Deriving a stable contribution estimate and ordered groups from these signals is non-trivial. Enforcing contribution-aligned fairness requires jointly optimizing recommendation accuracy, cross-group alignment, and within-group equity, which introduces non-trivial optimization trade-offs and potential gradient conflicts. To address the above challenges, we propose , a model-agnostic framework that operationalizes contribution-based fairness through a unified learning paradigm for contribution-performance-aligned recommendation. To address C1, CPFR uses multi-dimensional signals derived from each user's data and model-training dynamics as proxies for user contribution. It then partitions users into ordered contribution-aware groups for subsequent optimization. To address C2, CPFR incorporates two complementary fairness objectives directly into model training. First, enforces a monotonic relationship between contribution and recommendation performance, encouraging that higher-contribution groups achieve no worse outcomes compared with the lower-contribuiton ones. Second, minimizes performance disparities among users with similar contribution levels. These objectives are instantiated through two dedicated regularization terms, which are jointly optimized with the recommendation loss in a multi-objective framework. By integrating cross-group ordering constraints and within-group variance reduction into the gradient updates, CPFR balances fairness and accuracy without ad hoc post-processing. We evaluate CPFR on three benchmark datasets and three backbone models using accuracy and fairness metrics as well as comprehensive recommendation quality as a holistic measure of the accuracy–fairness balance. CPFR improves $Q$ over fairness baselines in most settings while maintaining competitive recommendation accuracy. Under the idealized allocation model in Sec.~, our theoretical analysis shows that contribution-proportional allocation yields higher equilibrium contribution and system accuracy than egalitarian allocation. In summary, our contributions are: We introduce Contribution-Performance Fairness, a novel fairness perspective requiring users with comparable estimated contributions to receive comparable recommendation performance, while ensuring that groups with higher average contributions receive no worse average performance than groups with lower average contributions. We propose CPFR, which estimates user contribution from interaction volume and training dynamics, partitions users into ordered groups, and jointly optimizes differentiable objectives for cross-group alignment and within-group equity. We conduct a theoretical game-theoretic analysis under an idealized allocation model, showing that contribution-proportional allocation induces higher equilibrium user contributions and, consequently, higher aggregate recommendation performance than equal-performance allocation. Experiments on three datasets and three backbone models show that CPFR improves Comprehensive Recommendation Quality over fairness baselines in most settings while maintaining competitive accuracy.
