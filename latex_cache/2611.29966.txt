Answering a question against a full-length book is a retrieval problem before it is a generation problem. A modern large language model can read a passage and answer well, but a novel or treatise spans tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens --- far more than fits comfortably in a context window, and long enough that ``stuff everything in'' both wastes tokens and degrades answer quality as relevant evidence is buried among distractors~. The standard remedy is retrieval-augmented generation ~: embed the document as chunks, retrieve the top-$k$ by vector similarity~, and condition the model on those chunks. But flat top-$k$ retrieval treats a structured book as an unordered bag of passages, discarding exactly the signal --- chapters, sections, narrative order --- that a human uses to find an answer fast. Two broad strategies add structure back. The first, exemplified by RAPTOR~, a hierarchy: it clusters chunk embeddings, summarizes each cluster with an LLM into a parent node, and recurses, producing a tree of increasingly abstract summaries that helps thematic and multi-hop questions. This is powerful and corpus-agnostic, but the hierarchy is manufactured from content and must be rebuilt when the document changes. The second strategy --- ours --- observes that many valuable long documents an authored hierarchy, and that exploiting it directly is both cheaper and better matched to precise localization. We present a structure-exploiting hierarchical retriever with three properties that distinguish it from learned-abstraction approaches: We use the document's own chapters as the coarse layer (matched via a BM25{+}dense query over compact chapter recaps) and its chunks as the fine layer, retrieved with a chapter-filtered dense search. There is no recursive re-summarization. Rather than returning scattered top-$k$ chunks, we expand the highest-ranked passages into contiguous spans under a token budget, trading a knob between quality and passage . Content-hash gating at chapter and chunk granularity re-embeds only changed text, so keeping the index current after edits costs a fraction of a full re-index. Our contribution is as much methodological honesty as it is architecture. Long-document retrieval papers are easy to oversell; we therefore report results only on reproducible, deterministic indices, subject each significant effect to a recap-seed-stability test , and retire earlier figures that a clean re-ingestion could not reproduce. When the task is to localize an answer inside a structured document, exploiting the authored hierarchy returns tighter, more on-target passages than either flat retrieval (which ignores structure) or learned abstraction (which manufactures coarse structure that spans too much to localize). The QuALITY result sharpens this: even on short articles with only pseudo-structure, coarse-to-fine retrieval matches full-context accuracy while flat retrieval falls well short --- evidence that the gain is about evidence is selected, not merely . The RAPTOR comparison then clarifies the boundary of the claim: the two methods are complementary, and the fair-metric analysis guards against the easy over-claim that a coverage-inflated summary tree ``wins.'' The section-expansion knob is the practical takeaway for practitioners: turn it off when downstream generation is sensitive to distractor passages , turn it on when the answer benefits from contiguous context and ranking matters more than purity. Delta ingestion makes either choice cheap to keep current, which is the operational argument for structure-native hierarchies over ones that must be periodically rebuilt. All in-house results are on English narrative and classical prose. We make no empirical claim about legal, medical, educational, or non-English corpora; the method also assumes documents with exploitable native structure and would degenerate on flat text, where RAPTOR-style learned hierarchies are expected to win. We presented a structure-exploiting hierarchical retriever for long narrative documents and evaluated it with an emphasis on reproducible, honestly-scoped claims. The robust findings are a seed-stable, significant passage-precision gain over flat retrieval controlled by a section-expansion knob; a significant win over flat retrieval on QuALITY that matches the full-context ceiling from a fraction of the text; a with flat retrieval on the harder NovelQA benchmark, with a per-aspect breakdown that bounds where native hierarchy helps and where it does not ; a complementary relationship to RAPTOR under a fair localization metric; and large maintenance savings from content-hash delta ingestion.
