The scoring stages of these cascades have benefited from scaling. Architectures such as HSTU, Wukong, and OneTrans consistently translate additional data and computation into improved ranking accuracy~. Slate generation, however, has not followed the same trajectory. The slate ultimately exposed to users is still assembled by an expert-engineered system, built around hand-crafted rules and specialized components for tasks such as scattering, quota allocation, de-duplication, fatigue control, and commercial boosting. These are combined with a shallow model that scores candidates under a strict latency budget and with manually tuned fusion weights. Slate generation is a combinatorial problem, and only a small part of the search space is reachable under this design. Extra computation at serving time is therefore spent on a more exhaustive search over this restricted space, and expanding the space requires additional rules and components. Slate quality thus improves with expert knowledge and engineering effort rather than with data and computation. We instead formulate slate generation as a learning problem. A reward model is trained on logged user feedback to evaluate complete slates, so the objectives that shape a slate are learned from data rather than encoded by hand. A single general policy is then optimized against this reward to generate slates from the candidate pool, and the search space is no longer restricted by hand-crafted rules but covers all feasible slates over the candidates. The reward model is used only during training, and its capacity is therefore limited by offline resources rather than by the serving budget. With a larger reward model and a deeper policy, additional data and computation now translate into better slates, exactly the scaling behavior that the expert-engineered system lacks. What changes is the role of the rules rather than their presence. Hard constraints are enforced during decoding, and soft preferences are incorporated into the reward. Instantiating the formulation in production, however, introduces three challenges. First, unlike language generation over a fixed vocabulary, slate generation selects from a request-specific pool of pre-scored candidates. Second, reward is defined over the complete slate rather than individual items. An item's utility depends jointly on its position, neighboring items, and the slate's composition across content types. Slate-level feedback is therefore non-decomposable into independent item-level supervision. Third, every generated slate must satisfy production business constraints, whereas unconstrained autoregressive generation provides no such guarantees. These challenges lead to the central question of this work: can a single learned policy, optimized offline against slate-level rewards, replace the expert-engineered system that assembles the slate today? answers this question in production. The system models slate generation as autoregressive decoding over the per-request candidates. A single decoder receives them from the upstream fine-ranker and emits the exposure slate directly, so no separate rule-based assembly engine, specialized components, or downstream reranking remain on the serving path. The decoder is the only model served at this stage. The training signal, in turn, comes from a slate-level reward model that scores complete slates rather than summing independent item scores. The decoder and the reward model are both built from OneTrans blocks~ and use the fine-ranking score as a candidate feature. OneTrans thus serves as a shared backbone across fine ranking, reward modeling, and slate generation. Deployed at full traffic, OneTrans-GSR paves the way for gradually integrating fine ranking into slate generation. We train the decoder in two stages. The decoder is first pretrained on long user-behavior sequences with a next-token prediction loss, and then post-trained with reinforcement learning against the reward model. For the post-training stage we propose Geometric-Mean Reward Policy Optimization , a method for multi-objective policy optimization. GMR-PO aggregates the reward model's scores for multiple objectives into a scalar reward. The scalar reward and the reward for business preferences are standardized independently, and the resulting advantages are combined to optimize the policy. Soft business preferences thus serve as training signals, while advertiser value and hard business constraints are controlled during decoding. Scalable sequence backbones for generative recommendation include HSTU and OneTrans~. Generative rerankers instead produce complete slates, with designs varying along two axes. Optimizing a policy against learned rewards raises two questions: how to update the policy and how to combine multiple rewards. Policy updates have been studied extensively in language modeling. In the standard setting for preference alignment, a reward model is trained from preference data, and the policy is subsequently optimized against its scores~. Direct preference methods avoid an explicit reward model and optimize policies directly from preference data, as in DPO~ and its extensions IPO, SimPO, and S-DPO~. Group-relative methods instead estimate advantages by comparing outputs sampled for the same input. GRPO computes advantages within a group of sampled outputs~, while GSPO and GMPO differ in how they aggregate importance ratios or per-token terms~. Existing alignment methods therefore differ primarily in the policy update rather than in the treatment of multiple reward dimensions. The second question concerns multiple objectives. Reinforcement learning commonly reduces multiple objectives to a scalar reward, an approach also used in e-commerce recommendation~ and, more recently, in preference optimization. GDPO standardizes each reward dimension before aggregation, reducing dominance caused by differences in raw reward scales~. SAGE separates the multi-objective advantage from the importance ratio and computes the latter geometrically over the sequence~. We compare the output-only, cross-attention, and prefill designs described in Section~, which introduce candidate interaction at progressively earlier stages of the decoder. Output-only scoring defers candidate comparison to the final dot-product head, cross-attention introduces candidates within each decoder layer, and prefill places them directly in the input context. Earlier interaction consistently improves RG, with prefill achieving the strongest result on all three objectives. Prefill also avoids the per-layer overhead introduced by cross-attention and is therefore used as the default design. Removing the output-side stop-gradient from the candidate embeddings lowers RG on all three objectives, consistent with the gradient conflict discussed in Section~. Removing the upstream OneTrans fine-ranking score has a larger effect and makes all three RG values negative. The decoder selects candidates, allocates exposure across content types, and determines their order through one generation process. A slate-level RM learns from logged user feedback and provides rewards for click, order, and GMV objectives, complemented by a delayed repurchase proxy for LTV. GMR-PO aggregates these objectives into a scalar reward and combines the standardized RM advantage with a separately standardized advantage derived from soft business preferences. Hard masks enforce strict requirements, while finite logit adjustments provide soft control during decoding. Because the RM is used only for offline post-training, its capacity can scale independently of the online serving model. In the evaluated production system, OneTrans-GSR replaces the Generator--Evaluator cascade. Offline experiments show positive RM-estimated utility gains relative to logged G-E slates. The generated slates also exhibit greater diversity and lower redundancy and fatigue than score-based ranking baselines, including when additive logit adjustments are disabled. The scaling experiments show improved RM discrimination or RM-estimated policy RG across the tested capacity and sampling configurations. In the randomized production A/B test, OneTrans-GSR increases GMV/u by \(2.63\ Although evaluated in a single production system, OneTrans-GSR is based on a general formulation: generating an ordered slate from a request-specific candidate pool using slate-level rewards and constrained autoregressive decoding. Different applications may define their own inputs, objectives, and constraints, while the current production experiment validates the resulting system as a whole rather than attributing its gains to individual components. This formulation applies broadly to recommendation, search, advertising, and other settings that construct ordered slates under multiple objectives and constraints. By keeping slate evaluation offline and serving a single policy that generates the final slate directly, OneTrans-GSR provides a scalable framework for learning these decisions from data while retaining explicit business control. Its ability to benefit from additional reward-model, policy, and sampling compute suggests a promising direction for learned slate generation beyond e-commerce reranking and mix-ranking.
