This section reviews prior work related to multimodal affect modeling for affect-aware conversational meme retrieval. We first discuss how affect functions in meme communication, drawing on pragmatic and cognitive accounts of meme interpretation. We then review multimodal affect modeling and meme understanding benchmarks, followed by work on conversational and multimodal retrieval. Finally, we position our work by clarifying how it differs from existing approaches. Memes are not merely image--text composites but pragmatic acts embedded in digital discourse . Their meaning often emerges through implicature, exaggeration, irony, and shared contextual knowledge . Shifman's framework of content, form, and stance highlights that meme interpretation depends not only on what is represented, but also on how a communicative position is affectively expressed. Cognitively, meme understanding involves resolving cross-modal incongruity while preserving sufficient coherence for interpretation . Affect is central to this process. Emotional arousal and contagion shape meme propagation , while meme interpretation also depends on contextual cues to stance and emotional orientation . Cultural context further modulates affective signaling, particularly in high-context communities where implicit humor and nuanced cues play an important role . Together, these studies suggest that multimodal affect provides observable evidence for how users interpret, respond to, and socially resonate with memes. However, most computational approaches still focus on semantic correspondence, leaving the affective basis of conversational meme response largely underexplored. Research on meme understanding has developed from early sentiment-oriented tasks such as Memotion to increasingly multilingual and culturally nuanced benchmarks. A major line of work focuses on harmful content detection, including hate speech and misogyny . Other benchmarks examine humor and persuasion techniques . Unified frameworks such as MemeLens further attempt to consolidate multiple meme understanding tasks. These benchmarks have substantially advanced meme classification and interpretation, but most are designed around predefined labels rather than conversational response retrieval. As a result, they rarely provide thread-level query--response structures or socially grounded relevance signals needed to study how memes function as affective responses in online interaction. On the modeling side, vision--language pre-training models such as CLIP and BLIP establish joint embedding paradigms for image--text alignment. Multimodal large language models , including GPT-4 and Qwen-VL , further improve multimodal reasoning. However, multimodal models can still struggle with irony, metaphor, and culturally specific humor . Recent studies introduce emotional, knowledge-injected, or multi-view reasoning mechanisms to improve meme understanding . KID grounds harmful meme detection in background knowledge using label-constrained distillation and structured reasoning chains. Its dual-head learning jointly optimizes semantic generation and classification. This work illustrates the importance of contextual knowledge beyond literal image--text correspondence. Its output is a harmfulness prediction, whereas CARE ranks candidate memes for a textual query; the tasks therefore require different supervision and evaluation. Pragmatic accounts explain how communicative intent is inferred beyond literal meaning . Although these approaches have advanced pragmatic reasoning in language understanding, their connection to multimodal retrieval remains limited. In conversational meme settings, intent is expressed not only through text but also through visual affective tone, character emotions, and cross-modal consistency. This makes meme retrieval closer to response retrieval, where relevance is socially and contextually grounded rather than defined by one-to-one semantic correspondence. Multimodal retrieval has progressed from shared embedding approaches such as VSE++ to large-scale contrastive pre-training. Contemporary systems commonly adopt a two-stage architecture: a bi-encoder for efficient coarse retrieval followed by a cross-encoder reranker for fine-grained interaction modeling . This architecture is well suited to conversational meme retrieval, where candidate responses must first be efficiently retrieved and then ranked according to their contextual and affective appropriateness. For meme-related tasks, prior work improves CLIP through retrieval-guided contrastive learning or robust adaptation strategies . More directly related to recommendation, present . This is a relevant antecedent for combining cross-modal retrieval with affective information. We include the associated IEF comparator in the experimental evaluation and do not claim that introducing emotion into meme recommendation is itself new. MemeMatch provides a large-scale meme dataset and retrieval system with nearly one million records from Reddit and Imgflip. It separates local context, including overlaid text and titles, from global visual or template context, and enriches these representations with sentiment, emotion, topic, and usage-intent annotations. It supports retrieval from natural-language or image queries. MemeMatch therefore establishes a direct precedent for context-aware, affect-enriched meme retrieval, rather than only meme classification. CARE focuses on a thread-associated protocol in which textual main posts supply queries, multiple images from a thread supply weak positives, and engagement supplies ranking feedback. Its evaluated pipeline combines gated emotion descriptors, multi-positive contrastive learning, and supervised reranking. This task and training configuration distinguish the present study's focus from the representation and retrieval capabilities described above; the existing results do not establish superiority over MemeMatch, which has not been evaluated under the same protocol. CARE studies the use of automatically inferred emotion descriptors in a conversational retrieval pipeline. Relative to MemeMatch , KID , and the emotion-infused recommendation benchmark , the scope of the contribution is the thread-associated task formulation and the evaluated two-stage training configuration. Its application differs from meme classification because each query can have several relevant candidates and the output is a ranked list. The architectural components draw on established vision--language retrieval, gated fusion, and learning-to-rank methods. Modeling multimodal affect for affect-aware conversational meme retrieval requires datasets that preserve both conversational context and response-level social feedback. Classification datasets address tasks such as hate detection, sentiment labeling, and humor recognition, while retrieval-oriented resources such as MemeMatch and the emotion-infused recommendation benchmark explicitly support search or recommendation . We formulate the task as . Given a textual query $q_i$ corresponding to the main post of a discussion thread, the objective is to retrieve meme responses that are appropriate to the textual situation and its affective expression. Unlike conventional one-to-one image--text retrieval, this task is inherently open-ended: a single conversational context may elicit multiple acceptable meme responses that differ in humor style, affective stance, and pragmatic expression. Therefore, relevance is not defined as exact semantic correspondence, but as observed membership in the same conversational thread. Formally, let $Q=\{q_i\}_{i=1}^{|Q|}$ denote the set of textual queries. Each query $q_i$ is associated with a source meme image $m_{q_i}$ in the main post and a set of comment meme responses $M_{q_i}=\{m_{q_i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N_i}$ in the corresponding thread, where $N_i$ denotes the number of valid meme responses. Let $M$ denote the collection of all meme images. The retrieval task is to return the top-$k$ meme responses from $M$ for a given query $q_i$. The ordered relevance list for $q_i$ is defined as follows. The source meme $m_{q_i}$ is placed first because it provides the original visual context of the conversation. The remaining comment memes in $M_{q_i}$ are ranked in descending order of their number of likes, which serve as implicit engagement feedback within the thread. {figures/emotion_distribution.png} } To transform raw conversational meme records into structured variables, we implement an automated annotation and representation pipeline that extracts multimodal affective and semantic signals for empirical analysis and response ranking. The extracted attributes include: sentiment polarity expressed in meme text ; overall affective tone conveyed by the visual content; free-form emotion descriptors for retrieval and facial-expression labels for regression, with the latter drawn from seven categories ; textual representations of visual content and embedded meme captions for semantic consistency estimation. In this study, is an umbrella term for multimodal emotional signals, and denotes textual or image-level polarity. The same multimodal annotation pass produces two distinct character-level fields. For regression, seven facial-expression labels are converted into meme-level binary indicators: each indicator equals 1 if the corresponding facial-expression category occurs for at least one character in the meme, and 0 otherwise. For retrieval, the original free-form emotion descriptors provide richer affective information, including reactions inferred from the full meme context, and are not restricted to these seven facial-expression categories. The study yields four main observations. First, under the topic-held-out protocol, the complete CARE system leads in fifteen of sixteen reported metric columns, with a Douban Hit@50 trade-off. Second, CARE-Retriever exceeds fine-tuned CLIP at all evaluated cutoffs, and CARE-Reranker exceeds the trained head-only control in both collections. These comparisons support the evaluated configurations without isolating every component. Third, LambdaRank supervision combining likes and source-image priority improves Main-Post Recall; on Imgflip, MR@10 more than doubles relative to retrieval alone. Fourth, the exploratory affective associations and the reranking margins differ between collections. Their agreement motivates further study of affect-aware retrieval rather than establishing a universal emotion rule. The results suggest design considerations for further evaluation. A semantic retrieval backbone can be combined with a weighted affective representation, as in CARE's residual fusion. Multi-positive learning accommodates the thread-based label structure, although thread membership is incomplete relevance supervision. The two-stage design restricts cross-encoding to the top 100 candidates; the Douban results illustrate that gains at early ranks can accompany losses at deeper cutoffs. Likes provide scalable ranking labels, but exposure and audience effects limit their interpretation as measures of response quality. CARE offers a potential design for response-suggestion applications. The retrieval backbone is fine-tuned together with the emotion projection and gate, while the emotion extraction module and BERT remain frozen. During reranker training, the pretrained cross-encoder backbone is frozen, and only its scoring head and emotion adapter are updated. Restricting cross-encoding to the top-100 candidates limits the number of reranker evaluations per query; latency, memory, and throughput are not measured here. Applying this pipeline to stickers, emoji, or short-video replies would require further validation. The benchmark provides 98,506 thread-organized records across 5,583 topics and two languages, with automatic affective annotations, engagement labels, and a topic-held-out evaluation protocol. Emotion similarity is one component of relevance and is not sufficient on its own. Memes expressing the same emotion can concern unrelated events, and an appropriate reply may convey a different emotion from the query, as in reassurance or irony. CARE combines the semantic embedding with an emotion representation and uses query--image interactions in reranking. Further work could examine calibrated context and affect scores, query-conditioned fusion, and hard negatives that share emotion but differ in context. These extensions could help distinguish semantic relevance from affective compatibility. Thread membership is incomplete supervision, and likes are influenced by exposure and audience composition. The source image is assigned the highest rank by design, so Main-Post Recall gains measure recovery of the conversational anchor rather than superior comment-response selection. The topic-held-out protocol separates training and test discussions, but does not establish isolation of near-duplicate images or shared templates across topics. Human assessment of semantic relevance, affective compatibility, and conversational usefulness, together with response-only evaluation, remains future work. Retrieval emotion descriptors may draw on captions and contextual cues as well as facial expressions. Regression instead uses seven facial-expression indicators, together with sentiment and consistency variables. Both kinds of automatic annotation can contain errors. Naming the models and releasing the zero-shot prompts in~ makes the annotation procedure more transparent and auditable, but does not guarantee deterministic outputs or annotation accuracy. An independent human annotation audit remains future work. Douban and Imgflip differ in language, platform conventions, and collection period, so the separate evaluations do not demonstrate cross-lingual transfer or isolate cultural effects. The exploratory blockwise regressions do not account for topic-level dependence or identify causal effects. The results describe associations and ranking performance within the two reported settings; their generalization to other platforms and periods remains to be evaluated. Four directions follow directly from this study. The first is human-centered validation: collecting graded judgments of semantic relevance, affective compatibility, and conversational usefulness, and adding response-only evaluation that excludes the source meme. The second is architectural refinement along the path identified above: query-conditioned gates, disentangled context/affect scores, and emotion-sharing hard negatives. The third is cross-platform and cross-lingual transfer, including shared multilingual encoders and explicit modeling of cultural affect differences. The fourth broadens the learning signal: temporal drift in meme usage, larger platform networks, multimodal large language models as rerankers, and quasi-experimental designs that move the engagement analysis from association toward causation. We presented CARE, a two-stage conversational meme retrieval framework combining gated emotion descriptors, multi-positive contrastive learning, and engagement-supervised reranking. Under the reported topic-held-out protocol, CARE leads in fifteen of sixteen metric columns and achieves Hit@10 of 24.03\ The thread-organized benchmark and evaluated pipeline provide a basis for further research on affect-aware response retrieval.
