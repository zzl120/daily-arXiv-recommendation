Evaluating each round solely through its latest model assumes that the successor consolidates the update, although pre- and post-update models may retain complementary ranking decisions. We term this and quantify it using cross-generation advantage , a marginally matched contrast between cross- and within-generation model pairs. A rank-separation statistic, label-free at selection time, predicts which family to retain. Across four datasets and three sequential recommendation encoders, the preferred retention regime varies by architecture: cross-generation pairing benefits GRU4Rec and SASRec, whereas FMLP initially favors within-generation pairing and shifts toward cross-generation pairing after a second update. Rank separation selects the stronger family in 12/12 first-update and 5/6 second-update dataset--encoder settings; on held-out tests, the selected family outperforms the direct successor in 34/36 trajectories. Five transfer mechanisms do not consistently reproduce these gains in one model. These findings establish state retention as a distinct Rec-RSI problem: progress may reside in relations between generations as well as in the latest model. Code is available at {https://github.com/Jinfeng-Xu/RecRSI}. {Information systems~Recommender systems} Recommendation recursive self-improvement places a recommender in a learning loop that generates supervision, trains a successor, and lets that successor shape later updates. Recent systems instantiate this loop through fidelity-controlled interaction generation~ or agent-based revision of objectives, code, and pipelines~. Each update changes both the ranking policy and the evidence available to future generations. Evaluating each round only through the latest model adopts a replacement view, attributing progress to the successor whenever Recall or NDCG rises. Yet these aggregate metrics obscure which relevant items support the score. Models with similar utility may retrieve different items, and an improved successor may not preserve all useful predecessor behavior. Since the retained state governs the next round, Rec-RSI raises a question beyond successor accuracy. What should persist across an update? We posit that recursive progress can be relational. An update may create complementarity across its boundary even when no individual model contains the joint behavior. We call this stage-structured complementarity and ask whether cross-generation pairs provide greater utility than pairs formed within either generation. We quantify this state with cross-generation advantage , which contrasts cross- and within-generation pairs under matched marginal exposure and equal inference cost. The contrast targets the interaction associated with crossing the update boundary. Rank separation then uses earlier-window top-$k$ overlap to select a pair family without outcome labels at selection time. Figure~ illustrates the relational state and its matched contrast. Across four datasets, three encoders, and two recursive updates, retention regimes vary by architecture. Rank separation predicts these regimes, and the selected family outperforms the direct successor in 34/36 held-out trajectories. RSIR generates interactions and retrains successors under fidelity control~; self-evolving systems revise objectives, code, and pipelines~. We study the relational state created by such loops through a marginally matched contrast across their update boundary. RISE uses policy distillation for recursive improvement~. Recommender distillation transfers scores or ranks~; EMKD combines sequential encoders~, while Hydra and FLAME preserve ensemble diversity in one network~. These methods consolidate ensemble diversity; CGA diagnoses complementarity aligned with an update boundary under matched marginals. Continual recommendation balances stability and plasticity~; ReLoop and meta-learning carry errors or experience forward~. Snapshot ensembles retain an optimization path~. CGA targets update-boundary complementarity through marginally matched cross- and within-generation pairs. Recommendation recursive self-improvement produces learning states that need not collapse into the latest model. CGA exposes progress distributed across an update, while rank separation identifies the stronger pair family at selection time. Their variation across architectures and rounds, together with incomplete single-model consolidation, establishes relational state retention as a central design problem for self-improving recommender systems.
