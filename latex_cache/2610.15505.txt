Item text and images add semantic information that can reorder the list. Existing Federated Multimodal Recommenders fuse this content into their models, which can obscure how content reorders the list. We therefore propose the mantic esidual dapter , which keeps content out of an existing FedRS and adds semantic residual scores to its collaborative scores. Content can then lift an item above another only when its semantic residual score exceeds the other's by more than their collaborative score gap. This score grows with the item's content similarity to the user's interaction history and is capped at a residual bound. We prove that for any content, an item can enter the list only if its collaborative score is within twice this bound of the lowest one in the list. A larger bound lets content lift more items, including irrelevant ones, so the bound trades off how far content reaches against accuracy. With a validated bound, SERA improves all three FedRSs on all six datasets without adding personalized parameters. It outperforms FedMRs in most comparisons and lifts some FedRSs above centralized baselines. Federated Recommendation System, Multimodal Recommendation System, On-Device Learning Federated Recommendation Systems learn collaborative scores from interaction histories stored on user devices and rank items by these scores, so an item appears above another if it scores higher. Observed interactions provide the main supervision , and each user or item embedding is learned from the gradients of its own interactions . As a result, users and items with sparse histories have too few interactions to learn reliable ID-based scores . Item text and images ease this sparsity, because they exist before an item collects many interactions . They add semantic information to interaction histories and help multimodal recommenders refine collaborative rankings . {taxonomy2.pdf} Fig.~ illustrates how Federated Multimodal Recommenders add this content. ~Feature-level fusion combines content with the raw inputs through early fusion, as in AMMFRS . The model can then learn fine-grained cross-modal interactions, and the FedRS has to be rebuilt and retrained. ~Representation-level fusion merges content into the learned representations through intermediate fusion . FedVLR uses it to adapt content to each user with fusion parameters on every client. ~Content also enters training through extra loss terms , and we treat this as objective-level fusion. FedMMR and GFMFR add alignment and distillation losses, which alter the training objective of the FedRS and leave inference unchanged. All three levels build content into the model behind the collaborative scores, so the final ranking mixes the effect of content with the collaborative signal. Score-level fusion in Fig.~ instead keeps the collaborative scores and adds a content score to each. An item then rises above another only when its content score exceeds the other's by more than their collaborative score gap. Each rank change thus traces to an item pair, and capping these scores limits how far content can reach into the collaborative ranking. We therefore propose SERA, a federated mantic esidual dapter that brings score-level late fusion to existing FedRSs. SERA keeps content out of the inputs, representations, and training objective of a FedRS and adds a semantic residual score to each collaborative score. Collaborative scores weight each user's history items to form a personalized content query, and each candidate's semantic residual score grows with its content similarity to this query. A residual budget, scaled by the standard deviation of the user's collaborative scores, caps each semantic residual score at a user-adaptive residual bound. Combining the pairwise condition with this cap, we prove that, for any content, an item outside the collaborative top-$K$ list can enter the final top-$K$ list only if its collaborative score is within twice the residual bound of the $K$-th item. The bound can trade off reach against accuracy, since a larger bound lets content lift more items, including some irrelevant ones. Our contributions span method, theory, and experiments: We propose SERA, a federated adapter that adds semantic residual scores to the collaborative scores of an existing FedRS. SERA uses these scores both to weight each user's history items and to scale the residuals per user. Every rank change under SERA traces to item pairs, and we prove a reach condition that holds for any content. On six datasets, SERA improves all three FedRSs with shared parameters alone, beats existing FedMRs in most comparisons, and shows the predicted trade-off. SERA's stop-gradient in Eq.~ passes the collaborative score forward and blocks its gradient, and SERA also applies the stop-gradient to the collaborative scores used for the history weights and $ _u$. Consequently, the residual loss in Eq.~ trains only the projector, and the backbone keeps learning from $_{} $ alone. All of SERA's content correction therefore sits in the semantic residual score $r $, which Proposition~, Corollary~, and Theorem~ bound relative to the trained collaborative scores. Joint training opens a path from the residual loss to the backbone through $s $, and this path changes what the backbone learns. As Sec.~ notes, the pairwise loss in Eq.~ becomes less sensitive to score changes as the margin between the observed item and the sampled negative grows. Under joint training this margin includes $r $, so pairs that $r $ already ranks correctly send weaker gradients to the backbone and pairs that it ranks wrongly send stronger ones. The backbone then learns from errors that already include the semantic residual score, so its collaborative scores carry content information, and the bounds of Sec.~ cover only the content effect left in $r $. Table~ supports this choice. Its two consistent differences, both for PFedRec on Food, favor SERA's stop-gradient. Every other difference changes sign across seeds, including joint training's largest mean advantage, for PFedRec on Cards. The stop-gradient thus keeps SERA's guarantees in Sec.~ intact and matches joint training within seed variation. SERA builds its content query from the user's history and weights each history item by its collaborative score in Eq.~. The temperature $ _q$ sets how sharply these weights favor high-scoring items, and a larger $ _q$ spreads them more evenly. Uniform weighting is the large-$ _q$ limit. In Table~, validation picks one of the two largest $ _q$ values in five of the nine configurations. How much the weights matter depends on the history itself. This content table also explains how SERA compares with the other adapters. Compared with GFMFR, which distills server-side multimodal content into each client's backbone using group preference targets, SERA also exchanges its content table. In return, SERA keeps content in $r $, which leaves the backbone's training unchanged. FedVLR uses the same collaborative item table as SERA, so their communication differs by SERA's $M d$ content table and FedVLR's fusion network of about $1.24 10^5$ parameters. Consequently, SERA exchanges less than FedVLR on the four datasets with fewer than about $1.9 10^3$ items and more on ML and Movie. SERA stores $d$-dimensional rows, a sixteenth of the width of the $d_x$-dimensional features that FedVLR stores, so SERA needs far less on-device storage. The server-side projector also keeps SERA's GPU memory low. Because the projector's parameters, optimizer states, and $d_x$-dimensional activations stay on the server, SERA's peak GPU memory stays close to that of a backbone of the same width. SERA's added tensors, namely the content table, its gradient, and the collaborative scores over the item set, all grow with $M$, so its peak GPU memory on Dance, Food, and Movie rises roughly in proportion to the number of items. At each history position, SERA keeps a $d$-dimensional row, whereas FedVLR keeps $d_x$-dimensional features and fusion outputs. The long histories of ML multiply this gap, so SERA's peak GPU memory on ML is a small fraction of FedVLR's. SERA's time cost follows from the size of its operations. Its client batches hold one user, and its server projects at most a few thousand items per round. At these sizes, fixed kernel launch overhead sets the duration of each GPU operation. SERA's added time therefore depends on how many operations it adds and changes little with the embedding width or the number of items. Table~ confirms this: outside ML, SERA's client computation stays nearly constant across item sets differing in size by over an order of magnitude. Operation counts also explain how SERA compares with the other methods at each stage. In local training, SERA adds the parameter-free steps of Eqs.~ to~ to its backbone. These steps need fewer operations than FedVLR's fusion network, so SERA's client computation is lower than FedVLR's outside ML. On the server, SERA runs one forward pass, one backward pass, and one optimizer step per round for all clients, so its server stage is on the same order of magnitude as FedMMR's and more than two orders of magnitude below GFMFR's clustering and fusion rounds. At inference, SERA computes all similarities of a user with one matrix-vector product between the content table and $ q_u$. This is more work than GFMFR, whose inference uses the backbone alone, and less than FedVLR, whose inference runs its fusion network, so SERA's time per user lies between theirs everywhere. ML departs from this pattern because two effects raise SERA's client computation there. First, the data loading and negative sampling that SERA shares with its backbone grow with each user's interactions, which are more than an order of magnitude more numerous on ML. Since the longest ML history is an order of magnitude longer than elsewhere, most positions of an ML history are padding and write in turn into the row of item~0. Profiling confirms this second effect. The accumulation takes about 4.6\,ms per ML client with SERA's server-side projector and about 0.3\,ms with a client-side projector.
