A large number of enterprise document stores face a different problem in terms of data policy and lifecycle. The second failure observed in enterprise settings is that similarity search has no mechanism for filtering by entity, because ``belongs to company $X$'' is not a semantic property of a passage. Looking into the SEC EDGAR data, two risk-factor sections about supply-chain disruption are near each other in embedding space even if they belong to entirely different companies. While for smaller datasets this issue might be alleviated by increasing the top-$k$ candidate budget, we show that it bites hard at 508{,}714 chunks drawn from 5{,}210 filers, and---more usefully---that it bites on exactly the questions where similarity search is supposed to be the right tool. This problem could theoretically be addressed by embedding extensive metadata into each chunk and refining the retrieval and ranking process. However, in an enterprise setting, such metadata is often dynamic in nature, and continuously re-indexing or re-embedding processed chunks is computationally prohibitive and operationally impractical. An alternative is to formulate the data storage layer to decouple dynamic metadata and relational structure from the passage chunks stored in the vector database. This leads directly to storing the structure explicitly: resolve entities to stable identifiers, materialize the relationships as edges, and attach a provenance key to every edge so that any claim can be traced to the document that supports it. This is the family of designs now loosely called GraphRAG and knowledge graph-augmented generation . Grounding a generator in retrieved passages was formalized by and rests on dense retrieval and sentence-level encoders . The literature since is extensive , and much of it optimizes the retrieval and ranking steps: better encoders, rerankers, hybrid sparse-dense scoring , iterative and active retrieval , and corrective retrieval . Our negative result on similarity search is orthogonal to that line of work. Translating questions into executable queries has its own long line, from to the cross-domain Spider benchmark , and more recently to large-scale realistic enterprise database benchmarks such as BIRD and multi-step decomposition and self-correction frameworks like DIN-SQL . In realistic schemas, schema linking, nested joins, and fan-out aggregations present severe failure modes for LLMs. We treat text-to-SQL not as a competitor to be defeated but as the strongest available baseline, and we give it the complete schema including the tables that make the multi-hop answers reachable by joins. This matters for the interpretation of our results: text-to-SQL wins one of the four categories outright and beats our graph implementation in a second. Knowledge graphs and their synergy with language models predate current interest, and ``GraphRAG'' is now an overloaded label . Recent systems combine graph structure with LLMs via graph neural reasoning , textual subgraph retrieval , or neurobiologically inspired associative memory indexing such as HippoRAG . The best-known formulation builds a community structure over an LLM-extracted graph and is aimed at query-focused summarization --- global questions such as ``what are the themes in this corpus''. Our design targets a different shape. The graph is built from filing metadata and structured exhibits rather than by LLM extraction; the traversal resolves are in scope, and the answer text is then fetched by primary key from the relational store rather than summarized from the graph. Only the first hop is LLM-authored; expansion is deterministic, for reasons we quantify in . HotpotQA , 2WikiMultihopQA , and MuSiQue established multi-hop evaluation, generally over Wikipedia, where the hops are between topically related articles and entity names are largely unambiguous. In dense entity retrieval and zero-shot entity linking , distinguishing mentions without authoritative identifiers remains difficult. Three distinct people in this corpus share the surname of our featured subject, and only a stable identifier separates them. A benchmark where names resolve cleanly cannot expose the failure mode we observe. Automated RAG evaluation increasingly uses an LLM judge . We deliberately do not, because the object under study is whether a system states falsehoods, and hallucination in the judge would be indistinguishable from hallucination in the system. Our scoring is a deterministic function of required entities, forbidden entities, keyword presence and citation correctness . This aligns with recent literature on citation attribution and grounded generation, such as the ALCE benchmark and attribution measurement frameworks , which stress verifiable provenance over qualitative evaluations. This is a lookup, not a search: the graph has already decided which documents are in scope, so there is nothing to rank and nothing to miss. The model receives the graph facts and the exact passages, and writes the answer with citations. Stage 2 being system-authored rather than LLM-authored is the design's central claim, and it is a response to a measured failure rather than an aesthetic preference. An LLM writing a single query over a fan-out join will attach a , and one entity's hundreds of child rows then consume the entire budget --- after which the model reports, fluently and wrongly, that the other entities have no subsidiaries or do not exist. Splitting identification from expansion makes that class of error structurally impossible rather than something to be prompted against. This separation contrasts with approaches that delegate multi-hop traversal or subgraph synthesis entirely to generative prompting , while sharing the motivation of associative knowledge graph indexing explored in HippoRAG . The stage-1 planning prompt instructs the model to return a JSON object containing one read-only Cypher query and 2--4 keyword phrases for text search. Key rules enforced by the prompt: return one row per entity with , do not traverse down to children, or fewer, follow documented relationship directions exactly, and never join one edge's to another's. The stage-2 expansion is a fixed Cypher query that takes the list of CIKs from stage~1 and, for each company, retrieves its subsidiary count, a sample of up to 12 subsidiary names, its EX-21 filing identifier, all risk-factor filing accession numbers, up to 6 insiders, and any reverse links. This expansion is marked as authoritative in the final prompt so the model does not infer absence from a ed traversal. The answer prompt instructs the model to act as a financial-disclosure analyst, answer only from the graph facts and verbatim excerpts, cite accession numbers for every claim, name the traversal path, and stay under 300 words. The repair contract for GraphRAG includes an additional trigger beyond exceptions and zero rows: if the stage-1 query returns rows but none of the returned columns is a company CIK, the repair fires, because stage~2 cannot expand entities it cannot identify. This prevented the failure mode where the model returned 2{,}000 rows of names with no identifiers, leading to a confident ``none exist'' answer. The same failure threatens both query languages, so the same two transforms are applied to both, identically. Any trailing in a generated query is raised to 2{,}000 rows before execution; the result set is then balanced to at most 6 rows per entity and 48 rows overall, where the entity column is detected by name. Applying these to SQL and Cypher alike means the comparison measures retrieval architecture rather than which prompt happened to dodge the trap. Both query-writing arms also get exactly one repair attempt, triggered when a query raises an exception or returns zero rows: the model sees the error text and rewrites once. All three arms call a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint provided by Bifrost, an open-source LLM gateway, which routes to DeepSeek or to AWS Bedrock. The benchmarked configuration is at temperature 0 with reasoning disabled, 700 output tokens for query generation (800 for GraphRAG's Cypher planning) and 1{,}500 for answers. The client is with a 180-second timeout and up to 2 retries per call. LLM clients are cached by the tuple , so each unique configuration creates exactly one client instance. Token accounting is per-thread: each retrieval mode runs in its own thread, and a thread-local counter prevents the cross-attribution bug that afflicted an earlier version of the harness . Routing through one endpoint is a methodological device, not a convenience. It makes ``all three arms used the same model with the same settings'' a structural property of the deployment rather than a convention maintained by discipline: the model is a request parameter read from one place by all three arms. It also makes the cross-model probe in a configuration change rather than a code change. Reasoning was disabled because an uncapped reasoning trace consumed roughly 33{,}000 completion tokens per call, which does not bound; the setting is identical for every arm, so the comparison is unaffected.
