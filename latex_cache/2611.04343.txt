We formalize timeline-constrained relevance , describe the relevance assessment protocol used to construct and validate the collection , and present the collection and its validation . To study the contribution of temporal order to document ranking, we represent a timeline query as an ordered sequence of natural-language statements, $q= $, where each $q_i$ describing an event—a real-world occurrence or change of state, such as infrastructure damage, displacement, or aid delivery. The query statements are arranged according to the required chronological progression. Given a document collection $D=\{d_1, ,d_n\}$, the objective is to rank the candidate documents according to how well the document internal event timelines align with the progression specified by $q$. Importantly, temporal-order consistency is defined over the chronological relations between events, rather than the textual order in which those events are mentioned. Documents frequently describe events non-chronologically. For example, in the sentence ``The found that food was the cause of the ,'' the investigation is mentioned before the poisoning and sickness, although both occurred earlier in time. Therefore, a relevant document need not provide evidence for $q_1, ,q_m$ in the same textual order as the query statements. Instead, the extracted from the document---in our example, $ $ $ $ ---must support the query timeline . Following standard information-retrieval evaluation , we define binary relevance. A query--document pair is considered if it satisfies two conditions: every query statement is supported by evidence somewhere in the document under a textual entailment criterion . In our setting, evidence from the document serves as the premise, and a query statement serves as the hypothesis. The evidence the query statement if it entails that the statement is true, and is labeled if it either entails that the statement is false , or if it does not provide sufficient information to determine whether the statement is true or false ). the temporal ordering of the supported events in the document is consistent with their ordering in the query. We adopt a broad definition of temporal consistency: for any two consecutive query statements $q_i$ and $q_{i+1}$, a document is considered temporally consistent if the corresponding evidence appears in the same temporal order, simultaneously, or when their temporal relation is ambiguous or cannot be determined from the provided context. Only documents in which the evidence clearly contradicts the query ordering, that is, $q_{i+1}$ precedes $q_i$, are considered temporally inconsistent. This definition differ from topical retrieval: a document may mention the same types of events as the query, but still be irrelevant if the events appear in an incompatible temporal order . Assessing timeline relevance is hard because it couples two conditions: statement-level semantic support and global order consistency. We therefore operationalize the relevance definition through a query--document assessment protocol, illustrated in Figure~. Given a timeline query and a candidate document, we first derive a natural-language timeline of the events described in the document. We then assess each query statement independently under an entailment-style framework: for each statement, we identify the relevant text segment from the derived document timeline as evidence, and assign a statement-level label indicating whether the evidence supports, is neutral toward, or contradicts the statement. Finally, determine whether the chronological relations among the supported events are compatible with the event progression specified by the query. We use this assessment protocol in two ways. We apply it with a strong LLM to label our evaluation collection , and again during human validation to assess the quality of the generated labels. We construct an evaluation collection from disaster reports published on ReliefWeb, comprising a document corpus, a set of timeline queries, and query--document relevance judgments., a service of the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs that collects and disseminates information about humanitarian crises and disasters. The dataset is licensed under CC BY 4.0.} We select ReliefWeb because it provides a large, public collection of real-world reports describing evolving disaster events. Comparable documents from domains such as healthcare, law, and criminal investigations are difficult to obtain because of privacy, legal, and confidentiality constraints. Starting from 108,841 ReliefWeb documents, we use the collection metadata to identify disaster-related reports and an LLM-based filter to retain those centered on a single major disaster sequence . We exclude regional updates and summaries of multiple unrelated events to isolate reports with a coherent event timeline and reduce ambiguity in both timeline extraction and relevance assessment . This process yields 6,145 documents suitable for timeline-aware retrieval evaluation. To construct the queries, we prompt GPT-5.2 to generate bullet-point timelines from a random sample of 200 documents, instructing it to avoid specific identifiable entities, such as locations, dates, times, and names . We manually review the generated queries and filter out those containing fewer than three timeline statements or more than ten, since in our preliminary experiments such queries tended to yield either too few or too many relevant documents. Finally, we assess the generated queries for quality and uniqueness, removing redundant and low-quality queries, including cases where event-specific information was leaked. For a timeline query containing $m$ statements and a document containing $n$ sentences, the order-preserving alignment runs in $O $ time after the sentence-level score matrix is computed. Thus, the alignment cost grows linearly with both the number of query statements and the number of document sentences. Since timeline queries are short, this procedure is suitable for use as a second-stage scorer over a bounded set of candidate documents. Overall, the alignment remains tractable in practice, taking 1--5 seconds per query, calculated using a single A100 GPU instance. Figures~ and~ analyze the sensitivity of the monotonic alignment model to the query-gap penalty $g_q$ and the coverage weight $ $. The results show that the query-gap penalty is an important parameter: performance is strongest for moderately negative values of $g_q$, while both overly harsh penalties and overly weak penalties tend to reduce performance. This pattern is consistent with the role of $g_q$ in the alignment objective. The coverage weight $ $ has a more moderate effect. Across the tested values, performance is relatively stable compared to the variation induced by $g_q$, suggesting that most of the ranking signal comes from the alignment path itself, while the coverage term acts as a useful regularizer. In particular, the best-performing configurations tend to combine a moderate query-gap penalty with a nonzero coverage weight, encouraging the model to prefer documents that both align well semantically and cover a larger portion of the query timeline. This reflects the high cost of evidence-backed temporal relevance assessment, which requires verifying multiple query statements and reconstructing their chronological relations within each document. Although paired significance tests indicate that the larger observed differences are robust, the collection provides limited statistical power for smaller effects and fine-grained subgroup analyses. We mitigate concerns about assessment reliability through repeated-assessment consistency checks, a category-pruning audit, and targeted human validation. The benchmark is derived from ReliefWeb disaster documents, which provide public, real-world event narratives but represent only one domain. Finally, our benchmark construction relies on LLM-assisted relevance assessment and chronological sentence ordering; although we use evidence validation and human assessment to improve reliability, errors in these steps may affect both labels and preprocessing. Our framework compares two alignment conditions that share the same statement--sentence compatibility scores and differ only in whether matches must preserve the query progression. Because textual document narratives may not reflect event chronology, we first temporally order each document , compute pairwise compatibility scores , and apply either order-preserving or order-agnostic alignment . In an offline preprocessing step, we split each document into sentences and prompt an LLM to order them chronologically by the events they describe . Only the ordering changes; the sentence text is untouched. The resulting sequence is stored and reused at ranking time, and since the step is query-independent, it runs once per document..} Given a timeline query $q= $ and a candidate document now represented as a temporally ordered sentence sequence $d= $, we first compute a sentence-level similarity matrix between the query and the candidate document. For each query statement $q_i$ and document sentence $s_j$, we use a pretrained sentence-pair scoring algorithm to assign a semantic compatibility score $S_{ij}= ,$ where $ $ is the scoring function and $S ^{m n}$ . Intuitively, $S_{ij}$ estimates how well document sentence $s_j$ is semantically similar to query statement $q_i$. We next describe our order-preserving and order-agnostic alignment methods, which isolate the contribution of temporal constraints. Both methods operate over the same score matrix $S$ and differ only in whether the selected statement--sentence matches must preserve the temporal progression specified by the query. Given the score matrix $S$, we search for the highest-scoring monotonic alignment between the query statements and the temporally ordered document sentences. Let $F $ denote the best alignment score between the query prefix $ $ and the document prefix $ $. We use a dynamic-programming procedure inspired by classical sequence-alignment algorithms , adapted to a semi-global setting . This formulation fits our retrieval task because a relevant document may contain additional material before, after, or between the sentences that support the query timeline. The recurrence considers three transitions: {3pt} \[ F = F + S_{ij}, \\ F + g_q, \\ F + g_s . \] The first transition matches query statement $q_i$ with document sentence $s_j$. The second leaves a query statement unmatched, while the third skips a document sentence. The gap penalties $g_q$ and $g_s$ control the cost of leaving query statements or document sentences unmatched, respectively. Since skipping a query statement indicates incomplete support, it is penalized. Document sentences, however, may be skipped without penalty, as documents often contain background or intervening content. We therefore set $g_s=0$ and tune $g_q$ on the development set . The final alignment score, $F $, is computed after processing the full document. To further encourage full query coverage, we add an explicit coverage term. After computing $F $, we recover the corresponding alignment path $P$ by backtracking from $ $. The path $P$ consists of match and gap transitions, and we let $|P_q|$ denote the number of query statements that are matched to document sentences along this path. The order-agnostic condition computes a maximum-weight partial one-to-one bipartite matching between query statements and document sentences using the Hungarian algorithm . This condition captures local semantic compatibility without constraining the order of the matched evidence. To permit partial matching, we augment the document side of the bipartite graph with \ dummy nodes. Matching a query statement to a real document sentence contributes \ , whereas matching it to a dummy node contributes the query-gap score \ . We tune \ on the development set and use the selected value for both OA and OP. The two conditions use the same compatibility matrix, partial one-to-one matching objective, query-gap score, coverage reward, and final scoring function. They differ only in their feasible alignments: OA permits matches in any document order, whereas OP requires the matched document sentences to preserve the order of the query statements. Neural retrieval and reranking models aim to capture semantic compatibility beyond lexical overlap. However, strong performance on conventional retrieval benchmarks does not necessarily imply sensitivity to the specific semantic relations that determine relevance. Controlled evaluations have shown that neural retrieval models can struggle with constraints expressed through negation or natural-language instructions . More closely related to our setting, find that general retrieval models often neglect the sequence structure of procedural documents. These findings motivate our investigation of temporal relations as a focused test of whether semantic retrieval models capture relational constraints expressed in queries. Temporal information has long been studied in information retrieval. Prior temporal-IR approaches incorporate signals such as publication time, recency, temporal expressions, and a query's temporal intent . These approaches primarily model information or documents are relevant. In contrast, we examine whether the internal progression of events described by a document is compatible with the temporal relations expressed in a natural-language query. Event timelines have also been studied in timeline extraction and temporal-relation modeling , timeline summarization , and temporal reasoning and question answering . These tasks generally assume that the relevant source documents have already been identified and seek to extract, organize, summarize, or reason over their temporal information, rather than use event order to rank documents. The closest line of research combines retrieval with timeline or event-sequence modeling. retrieve query-relevant event sentences and organize them into a timeline. retrieve structured continuous-time sequences of timestamped events, while retrieve such sequences from textual descriptions. These settings retrieve individual event sentences or structured timestamped sequences. We instead rank full, unstructured documents according to both semantic support for the query events and consistency with their specified temporal progression. Semantic retrieval models aim to move beyond lexical overlap by assigning high relevance to documents that match the query with respect to its abstract concepts, and the relationships between them. In this paper, we focus on information needs that specify temporal relations between events. For example, a user may seek reports describing a natural disaster followed by infrastructure damage, population displacement, and the subsequent delivery of aid. A document that discusses all these events may be topically and semantically relevant, yet fail to satisfy the information need if its event timeline contradicts the required progression. This distinction is difficult for standard retrieval and reranking models, which primarily capture topical relevance or local semantic compatibility . Such timeline-constrained information needs arise across several domains. In healthcare, patient records describe trajectories of diagnoses, treatments, medications, and outcomes. Retrieving prior cases with comparable clinical courses aids prognosis and treatment planning . In law-enforcement and investigative settings, reports of incidents such as kidnappings, assaults, or murders may be compared to identify related cases and recurring temporal patterns indicative of a common modus operandi. In this work, we investigate whether retrieval and reranker models can capture temporal relations expressed in queries and whether explicitly modeling these relations improves retrieval performance. Our setting consists of a collection of full documents, and timeline queries expressed as ordered sequences of natural-language statements, each describing an event within a broader progression. Given such a query, the task is to rank documents according to both whether they support the specified events and whether those events unfold in a temporal progression compatible with the order expressed in the query . In this context, we address two research questions: How effectively do semantic retrievers and rerankers capture temporal relations between query events? We compare dense retrieval and neural reranking models with lexical retrieval. The results show that state-of-the-art retrieval models provide only limited improvements over lexical retrieval, indicating that their semantic matching capabilities do not effectively capture the temporal constraints that determine relevance. Does preserving temporal order improve reranking performance? We introduce an order-preserving alignment method and show that enforcing temporal order substantially outperforms standard reranking models. To support this investigation, we make the following contributions: We formulate a controlled setting for timeline-aware text retrieval and construct a focused evaluation collection using a decomposed protocol that separately assesses statement-level semantic support and temporal consistency . We systematically evaluate and compare lexical and dense retrieval, as well as neural reranking, under timeline-aware conditions, thereby isolating the contribution of explicitly preserving temporal order We show that order-preserving alignment significantly outperforms both standard retrievers and rerankers, demonstrating the specific contribution of explicit temporal-order preservation . We studied full-document retrieval for timeline sensitive queries, asking whether current retrieval and reranking models capture temporal progression, and whether explicitly preserving event order provides a ranking signal beyond semantic compatibility. Using a novel focused evaluation collection that separates statement-level support from temporal consistency, we find that strong semantic models provide only limited gains, while explicitly instructing compatible models to consider temporal progression yields no improvement. In answer to our first question, existing models therefore appear to poorly capture temporal constraints. For our second question, a controlled comparison between otherwise equivalent alignment models shows that order-preserving alignment significantly outperforms both its order-agnostic counterpart and all evaluated retrievers and rerankers. The degradation caused by randomizing sentence order further confirms that meaningful internal event order contributes beyond local semantic matching. Overall, our findings establish temporal progression as a valuable but yet unaddressed ranking signal, thus motivating future development of generic retrieval and reranking models that would effectively address temporal modeling as well. We investigate this question by studying timeline-constrained document retrieval, where a query is an ordered sequence of natural-language event statements while a relevant document should match those events in a compatible temporal progression. We then test whether widely used dense retrievers and neural rerankers capture such constraints, and whether explicitly preserving temporal order improves ranking, using a controlled setting over a novel focused evaluation collection. Experiments show that state-of-the-art semantic retrievers and rerankers provide only limited improvements over lexical retrieval, suggesting that their semantic matching capabilities do not reliably capture temporal constraints.
