Images and text reveal appearance, style, atmosphere, and theme beyond what can be learned from interaction IDs alone. Successive advances in multimodal feature learning, graph construction, and self-supervised learning have made these signals increasingly effective for recommendation~. As a result, recommenders can now describe the content of an item at an unprecedented semantic resolution. This progress, however, exposes a growing asymmetry in user--item matching: item representations have become increasingly fine-grained, while user preference is still largely inferred at the granularity of whole-item interactions. A click or purchase indicates which item a user chose, yet leaves the factors that motivated the choice entangled with all other properties of that item. User-aware weighting and target-conditioned attention can emphasize relevant parts of multimodal representations~, but the resulting preference factors remain implicit within continuous representations, without persistent identities that can be directly addressed and shared across items. Consider a user whose history contains diverse items that nevertheless share a recurring multimodal factor. Treating each interaction as positive evidence for the whole item leaves that preferred factor entangled with the item's other semantics, as illustrated in Figure~ . Giving the recurring factor a canonical identity instead provides a shared coordinate that persists across otherwise different items, as shown in Figure~ . The user's preference can then be grounded in what recurs across the history rather than represented only through an aggregation of whole-item embeddings. This shared identity changes the unit of user modeling from whole items alone to the multimodal factors repeatedly supported by their content. {figures/motivation_concept_path_v34.pdf} A shared-coordinate representation raises three challenges. First, its semantic units must cover domain-specific and compositional multimodal factors while resolving equivalent observations to consistent identities across items. Second, shared item semantics must be converted into personalized preference: among all concepts present in a user's interacted items, the model must identify those supported by the history at different temporal scales. Third, concept-mediated evidence must work together with direct collaborative signals, which remain indispensable for capturing behavioral regularities beyond observable content. Multimodal large language models provide the capacity to recognize open-ended, domain-specific factors from item images and titles, while knowledge graphs provide persistent identities through which these factors can be shared. Building on these complementary strengths, we propose , a multimodal knowledge graph framework for grounded user preference learning. MKGE induces a domain-adaptive vocabulary of canonical concepts, grounds each user's chronological history in the same vocabulary, and learns a dual-path recommender over direct user--item interactions and concept-mediated user--concept--item relations. In this structure, the item graph establishes reusable semantic coordinates and the user graph records which coordinates are supported by each user's interaction history. This formulation leads to concrete empirical questions. Does canonical identity strengthen the reuse of recurring semantics? Does recommendation depend on the correspondence between each user and their grounded concepts, beyond concept frequency, global distinctiveness, or graph connectivity? Do concept-mediated paths complement direct collaborative evidence, and are they especially useful when a user provides fewer interactions? Across three benchmarks, MKGE leads collaborative, knowledge-aware, multimodal, and recent large-model baselines on all six metrics. Controlled studies support shared concept identity and personalized grounding, with the largest gains over removing or rewiring the user graph for low-activity users. In summary, we make the following contributions: To establish shared semantic identities, we formulate and induce a domain-adaptive vocabulary in which recurring multimodal factors become canonical, addressable coordinates across items. To turn shared item semantics into user preference, we introduce temporal history grounding and a dual-path recommender that combines short- and long-term user--concept relations with direct collaborative evidence. To determine when and why these representations improve recommendation, we conduct controlled grounding, activity, component, canonicalization, case, robustness, and transfer studies across three benchmarks. The results verify the importance of preserving user--concept correspondence, shared concept identity, and complementary recommendation paths. Multimodal recommendation enriches interaction signals with item content. VBPR projects visual features into a latent space and learns user-specific visual factors for personalized ranking~. MMGCN subsequently propagates modality-specific information over user--item graphs to learn both user and item representations~. These models establish the central value of content-aware matching: behavior determines which directions in a multimodal representation space are useful for recommendation. Later work improves the structure, supervision, and reliability of this representation process. LATTICE learns modality-aware item--item graphs from content~; BM3 bootstraps latent views and aligns modalities without auxiliary graph augmentation or sampled negatives~; and FREEDOM freezes the multimodal item graph while denoising user--item interactions~. SMORE suppresses modality-specific and cross-modal noise during fusion~, and GUIDER recalibrates noisy feedback and distills modality representations toward recommendation semantics~. These approaches learn continuous user and item representations by improving graph structure, supervision, and modality fusion. Several multimodal recommenders focus more directly on the user side of the match. MONET strengthens the contribution of modality features to final embeddings and uses target-aware attention to construct a user representation conditioned on the candidate item~. Tavern similarly derives a visual preference signal for each historical click from its shared visual information with the target, removes irrelevant information through selective disentanglement, and aggregates the resulting signals with a recurrent encoder~. Both demonstrate that a user's relevant multimodal evidence depends on which parts of the history are activated for the current decision. Large models provide another interface for preference modeling. TALLRec establishes instruction tuning as an efficient way to align an LLM with recommendation data~. MLLM-MSR converts item images into textual summaries, recurrently summarizes the evolution of user preference, and fine-tunes an MLLM for sequential recommendation~. MLLMRec converts images into semantic descriptions and reasons over each behavioral description list to obtain a purified user preference profile, while also refining the item--item graph~. These methods move beyond uniform history pooling through target-conditioned representations or language-level user profiles. Recent work further develops explicit preference profiles through KG-augmented conversational reasoning~, multimodal multi-interest profiling~, and online natural-language interest personas~. MKGE represents preference through a persistent factor-level interface. A concept discovered in one item retains the same identity when it recurs elsewhere. A history selects supported concepts, and a candidate connects to the user through those same nodes, allowing preference evidence to accumulate across otherwise different items. Knowledge-aware recommenders make side information reusable through entities, relations, and paths. RippleNet expands a user's clicked items along KG links to form candidate-aware preference distributions~. KGAT propagates attentive messages over a unified collaborative knowledge graph~, while KGIN represents latent user intents as attentive combinations of KG relations and preserves relational paths during propagation~. These methods show how shared entities can transfer preference evidence beyond direct item co-occurrence. Multimodal and large-model methods enrich the structures available for such propagation. MKGAT encodes visual and textual information for entities and aggregates their neighbors in a multimodal KG~. DOGE uses LLM-enhanced cross-modal features to construct a hyper-knowledge graph containing user--user, item--item, and higher-order relations~. MMQ quantizes multimodal item content into semantic IDs and behaviorally adapts those codes for recommendation~. These methods make multimodal semantics accessible to graph propagation or discrete sequential modeling. LLM-based information extraction and KG construction provide relevant precedents for creating semantic structure. Text2DB adapts extraction to a target database schema~; SAC-KG uses generation, verification, and pruning to construct domain KGs~; and IncPrompt incrementally induces and verifies open-domain event schemas~. The induced item graph also improves three established KG recommenders, supporting its utility across architectures. Grounded concepts summarize history-supported preference associations with observable multimodal factors. Direct collaborative representations complement this view with signals related to price, popularity, social influence, and latent behavioral structure. Domain changes may require vocabulary refresh, and concept quality remains linked to multimodal recognition and normalization. Broader domains and evolving catalogs offer natural directions for future evaluation. Explicit concept profiles also motivate studying user inspection and revision. Further extensions could examine how shared semantic factors support preference-guidance paths~ and user recommendation that accounts for information propagation~. MKGE shows how multimodal item understanding can become a shared language for user preference modeling. More broadly, stable factor identities let catalog construction, temporal grounding, and recommendation evolve separately through a shared semantic interface. This offers a route for turning increasingly capable multimodal understanding into structured user models that remain reusable across candidates and downstream architectures. This work uses public benchmark datasets. MKGE summarizes historical interactions as user--concept edges, which may reflect recognition errors and representational biases inherited from the construction model and source media. Responsible deployment requires compliance with data licenses, concept audits for target populations, and mechanisms for users to inspect, update, or delete stored preference representations.
