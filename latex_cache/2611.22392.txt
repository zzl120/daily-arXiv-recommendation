The other six carry no worst-case guarantee, and their expected cuts are those of Proposition~. We subsequently introduce , a simple scheduling method built on these findings. It implements the proposed, low-round-count schedule of Proposition~ on a dealt split, with no repair round. In our $w{=}20$ evaluation setting, it is a two-round schedule: for $N{=}100$, it deals the candidates into $H{=}5$ hands of twenty, each presented in retriever order, ranks the hands concurrently and advances the top $q{=}4$ of each to the final ranking call, which lists them by their place in their hand, then by retriever rank. At higher $N$ values, it follows the same logic but becomes , re-ordering $N{=}1000$ documents in just 3 rounds by first creating $H{=}50$ hands that each keep $q{=}2$, which are then re-dealt, still in retriever order, into $H{=}5$ hands, whose $q{=}4$ candidates advance to the final ranking call in retriever order. For $N{=}500$, 25 hands keep 3, then 4 hands keep 5. Candidates eliminated in later rounds are ranked above those eliminated earlier, and within a round by their place in their hand, then by retriever rank. Listwise reranking with large language models , where the model is provided with a query and a certain number of documents to order according to relevance, has experienced significant interest due to its high quality~. This method is primarily used in the typical ``retrieve-then-rerank'' 2-stage pipeline~, where a first-stage retriever, commonly BM25~ or neural-based methods such as dense embeddings~, SPLADE sparse embeddings~ or ColBERT-inspired~ multi-vector embeddings, first retrieves the candidates. The common practice is to rerank a small, but not insignificant number of documents, with 100 and 1000 being commonly evaluated targets~. Listwise rerankers experience latency and context window constraints, either due to models supporting small windows~ or models experiencing context rot~, with degraded performance as window sizes climb. As such, they require a , which decides which windows to rank and in what order, in order to produce the final ranking. The sliding window of the original RankGPT~, which covers a hundred candidates from the bottom up in nine calls, each waiting for the one before, is suboptimal. The problem is a known one, as presented by~: given an arbitrary number of horses, how many races do we need to accurately identify the } top performers? Many schedules aiming to fix this have been proposed: setwise approaches~, tournament brackets~, highly-parallelized approaches~, among others. correctly identify that each of these methods leaves out substantial information by under-utilizing the implicit pairwise relationships between documents, and seek to address this with a principled tournament approach, . This tournament achieves matching ranking quality at significantly reduced token counts. Token counts, however, say little about how long a user waits. Independent calls can run at the same time, so the wait is set by the chain of , where each round is a set of calls that must wait for an earlier round. BlitzRank notes that disjoint calls can run in parallel, but does not further explore parallel depth and its implications~. A parallel schedule splits the candidates into groups, ranks the groups at once and advances a fixed from each. When one group holds more of the top passages than its quota, good passages are cut, and additional rounds, such as BlitzRank's certification or TourRank's further stages~, must be added to recover them. In this short paper, we demonstrate that the split matters where a group's quota can bind. We subsequently argue that, given that the nature of re-ranking implies a first-stage retriever, the best split deals the candidates round-robin by retriever rank. The need for dealing is best shown by the methods such as BlitzRank require: in the BlitzRank implementation, the schedule can trigger an additional sequential call, which re-ranks passages that the chunks cut to check that none belongs in the top ten. This call is triggered on 59\ Dealing itself is not new. Indeed, it is a generalization of the equal-gap seeding of knockout tournaments~, and it has been suggested that approximate top-$k$ kernels on accelerators interleave their buckets, a form of dealing, because contiguous ones lose recall on correlated inputs~. Among existing schedules, TourRank deals its pool at every stage, while others select around pivots~, run tournaments of small windows~ or re-rank uncertain passages~. However, there is little analysis of why the split matters, where it does not, or which later calls a dealt split makes unnecessary, rendering existing approaches computationally wasteful. In this paper, we address the above questions with the following findings. First, where a quota can bind, dealing is the optimal split under a simple retriever model. We prove that, under this model, no split into groups with equal quotas cuts fewer of the true top ten on average. Moreover, the only splits as balanced as dealing at every retriever depth are other deals. Random groups appear equivalent to dealing at a quota of four, as the analysis predicts, but are balanced only on average, so they keep cutting however good the retriever is, and their results depend on the seed. Contiguous chunks cut more as the retriever improves, and already cost 2.7 nDCG@10 points over BM25. Second, most schedules reach similar nDCG@10 but differ widely in calls, rounds, tokens and latency. DealRank, the simplest schedule built on these findings, parallelizes all initially dealt hands at once before adjusting its final rankings in a single-call final round. It reaches performance competitive with all best-performing schedules, and outperforms weaker dealing-based ones such as TourRank with one tournament, with fewer ranking rounds than any other multi-round schedule. It additionally comes with consistently predictable numbers of rounds: the total number of rounds is fixed and based only on the number of input documents, unlike other schedules which may require additional certification calls~. Third, across five schedules, dealing helps wherever a quota can bind . It removes most of BlitzRank's certification calls without statistically significant changes in its nDCG@10. On the other hand, contiguous groups would cost TourRank, which already deals, 2.2 to 4.5 points. In TDPart, ListK and BlitzRank at $w{=}10$, where no quota can bind, dealing behaves as expected, without any major changes in quality. Building upon this, we demonstrate that DealRank, in a hierarchical form, scales considerably better than existing schedules as the list of candidate documents grows. On top 1000 reranking tasks, its ranking performance is again within noise of other strong performing schedules, while needing only three rounds and achieving an 11-fold speedup over the sliding window and a 1.26-fold one over BlitzRank with concurrent first calls. Listwise LLM rerankers cannot reliably rank hundreds of documents in a single call, and rely on schedules to orchestrate their calls. Parallel schedules split candidates into groups and advance a fixed quota from each. When a group holds more of the top passages than its quota, some are cut, and expensive repair calls become required. We show that the way candidates are split matters where such a quota can bind, and that, in expected cuts, the best split there deals them round-robin by retriever rank, like cards. Although not conceptually new, dealing has remained unanalyzed. We prove that, under a simple retriever model, no split with equal quotas cuts fewer of the true top ten on average, and that contiguous chunks cut more as the retriever improves. DealRank, the simplest schedule our analysis allows, is on average within 0.1 nDCG@10 points of the best schedules over six LLMs, in two rounds. It is consistently 1.1 to 1.4 times faster than BlitzRank with concurrent first calls up to a thousand candidates. There, it needs three rounds and is eleven times faster than the sliding window. In this short paper, we analyzed the importance of listwise scheduling across both quality and efficiency metrics. We demonstrated that many existing schedules reach similar ranking performance, but with widely varying levels of both token and time requirements. Building upon this, we formulated the importance of per-round quotas and demonstrated that the split of parallel listwise schedules greatly matters where a group's quota can bind. We showed that dealing is optimal in expected cuts under a simple retriever model and, unlike a random partition, is balanced on every query. DealRank, a naive schedule built upon these observations, is a robust schedule which appears competitive with all strong existing methods with higher efficiency and higher predictability. DealRank is also highly scalable, retaining round predictability and lower latencies even with 500 and 1{,}000 candidates. Its latency gain over concurrent BlitzRank is modest but consistent, between 1.1 and 1.4 times at every list size, and reaches eleven times over the sliding window at 1{,}000 candidates. Our intervals rule out average deficits above 0.7 nDCG@10 points, though not differences of about a point on a single LLM.
