Abstract: Reasoning rerankers write a rationale before ranking a candidate slate, and existing systems read item metadata to do so, even though generative recommenders increasingly represent items as compact Semantic IDs (SIDs). Ranking from SIDs alone has two appeals: SID prompts are far shorter than the matched item text, and although item codes are fixed from content, the backbone learns their token embeddings in part from user-item interaction sequences, so the tokens can carry behavioral signal that item text lacks. That training targets next-item generation, and whether it supports ranking a supplied slate from SIDs alone is untested. We build ReRankID, a controlled testbed in which models reason and rank from a history and slate shown only as SIDs, varying item rendering, history length, and slate size one at a time. On this testbed, such models often copy the input order instead of reranking, and readable metadata does not repair the failure. We compare supervised fine-tuning, rejection-sampled self-training, on-policy distillation, and verifier-based reinforcement learning as post-training methods and find that they elicit the missing behavior. The strongest post-trained model, the 8B model trained with a graded ranking reward, gives the best SID-only ranking and matches strong text-reading LLMs whose prompts are about 20x longer. It ranks above random with no history at all, where a caption-reading reference does not, and it keeps its gains at long histories, where text prompts pass 50k tokens and the caption-reading reference stops improving. In a paired rationale audit, this model argues trade-offs better than that reference but describes items less accurately. The common post-training methods we test thus elicit ranking from SIDs but not faithful item descriptions, which calls for post-training that improves the reasoning itself, not only the ranking it produces.

Abstract: This study investigates Jev, a System One model designed for fast, structured decision-making, in information retrieval. We use Jev-as-a-judge to generate relevance judgments for four Brazilian Portuguese IR collections covering different domains and compare them with the official relevance judgments of each collection. We also evaluate Jev as a reranker. For relevance assessment, exact agreement ranged from 52.9% to 64.9%, while quadratic weighted Cohen's kappa ranged from 0.52 to 0.82. Most disagreements involved adjacent relevance levels, with at least 94.5% of annotations matching exactly or differing by only one relevance level. Despite differences at the individual query--document level, Jev-generated qrels produced similar retrieval system rankings, with Kendall's tau ranging from 0.90 to 0.93 for NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus across the two evaluation metrics. For reranking, Jev reordered the top 1,000 BM25 candidates for each of the 50 Quati test queries, increasing nDCG@10 from 0.3329 to 0.6331 (+90.2%) and MRR from 0.6852 to 0.9033 (+31.8%). These findings support Jev as a relevance assessor and show improved ranking effectiveness over BM25 in the evaluated reranking setting.

Abstract: Open-ended search combined with foundation models has produced advances such as faster algorithms, new results on open mathematical problems, and self-improving agents. We extend this paradigm to recommendation for decision-making, focusing on decisions where the set of possible choices is fixed but the reasons for choosing them are not. In high-stakes decisions, e.g., which company to acquire or which customer to target, comparing diverse, well-reasoned options can be more useful than a single ranked list. We formalize open-ended recommendation as building a diverse, high-quality set of recommendations, each an item together with a hypothesis, a natural-language reason why the item fits the needs of a decision maker. We propose QD-Rec, a quality--diversity method that selects items with a contextual bandit and generates hypotheses with a foundation model from earlier ones kept in an archive as stepping stones. A new recommendation enters the archive if it is novel or better than its nearest neighbor. Across M&A, customer targeting, and paper and book recommendation, QD-Rec gives the highest mean quality and builds the largest archive in most settings.

Abstract: Current learned sparse approaches for recommender systems, such as STAR, represent a user by concatenating their interaction history into a single query string. For news, this creates two bottlenecks: concatenation collapses a user's distinct reading interests into one flat query, and, because news articles are long-form text, concatenating several news articles quickly overruns the context window of the language model used as backbone and forces quality-degrading truncation.
  To address both bottlenecks, we introduce learned sparse news recommendations (LSNR). LSNR encodes each article independently and constructs a sparse user vector by combining learned, attention-based weighting of the per-article sparse representations with a dense-projected expansion at the user level. Experiments on the MIND and EBNeRD datasets show that LSNR significantly outperforms STAR, with these gains holding even in short-history settings where truncation is not a factor. Compared to state-of-the-art dense recommenders, LSNR is competitive in standard settings and it outperforms them when the user history is short. An ablation of the user encoder confirms that learned attention-based aggregation outperforms simpler pooling alternatives. Beyond effectiveness, an additional advantage of LSNR is that it serves from an index an order of magnitude smaller than an uncompressed dense baseline.
